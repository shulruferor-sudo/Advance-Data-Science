# Programmable Metasurface Based Multicast Systems: Design and Analysis

Xiaoling Hu, Student Member, IEEE, Caijun Zhong, Senior Member, IEEE, Yongxu Zhu, Member, IEEE, Xiaoming Chen, Senior Member, IEEE, and Zhaoyang Zhang, Member, IEEE

Abstract—This paper considers a multi-antenna multicast system with programmable metasurface (PMS) based transmitter. Taking into account of the finite-resolution phase shifts of PMSs, a novel beam training approach is proposed, which achieves comparable performance as the exhaustive beam searching method but with much lower time overhead. Then, a closed-form expression for the achievable multicast rate is presented, which is valid for arbitrary system configurations. In addition, for certain asymptotic scenario, simple approximated expressions for the multicase rate are derived. Closed-form solutions are obtained for the optimal power allocation scheme, and it is shown that equal power allocation is optimal when the pilot power or the number of reflecting elements is sufficiently large. However, it is desirable to allocate more power to weaker users when there are a large number of RF chains. The analytical findings indicate that, with large pilot power, the multicast rate is determined by the weakest user. Also, increasing the number of radio frequency (RF) chains or reflecting elements can significantly improve the multicast rate, and as the phase shift number becomes larger, the multicast rate improves first and gradually converges to a limit. Moreover, increasing the number of users would significantly degrade the multicast rate, but this rate loss can be compensated by implementing a large number of reflecting elements.

Index Terms—Programmable metasurface, multicast systems, channel estimation.

# I. INTRODUCTION

By the year 2022, there will be 28.5 billion networked devices, and the overall mobile data traffic will reach up to 77 exabytes per month according to Cisco Visual Networking Index forecast [1]. The tremendous growth in the number of communication devices calls for green and energy-efficient wireless solutions. To tackle this issue, the programmable metasurface (PMS), also known as intelligent reflecting surface (IRS), has recently been proposed as a promising solution due to its potential of both low power consumption and low deployment cost [2-4].

Specifically, a typical PMS is usually a uniform planar array composed of a large number of low-cost, passive, reflecting elements (e.g. printed dipoles), each of which can independently reflects the incident wireless signal with adjustable phase shift (controlled by an attached smart controller). By adaptively tuning the phase shift of the reflecting elements, the propagation of the incident signal can be adjusted in a desirable way, thereby realizing smart and programmable wireless environment [5,6].

# II. SYSTEM MODEL

We consider a single-cell multicast system as illustrated in Fig.1, where the BS equipped with a PMS-based transmitter

communicates with a group of  $K$  single-antenna users.

The partially connected architecture is adopted, which is realized by aligning the beam of each directional horn antenna to the corresponding sub-PMS consisting of  $L = \frac{N}{N_{\mathrm{RF}}}$  non-overlapping reflecting elements, where  $N_{\mathrm{RF}}$  is the number of RF chains (antennas) and  $N$  is the total number of reflecting elements.

The  $i$ -th sub-PMS consists of  $L$  reflecting elements corresponding to the  $i$ -th RF chain. Each element of the  $i$ -th sub-PMS behaves like a keyhole. During the uplink transmission period, the reflecting element combines all the received signals and re-scatters the combined signal to the  $i$ -th RF chains, while during the downlink period, the reflecting element combines signal from the  $i$ -th RF chain and re-scatters the signal as if from a point source.

Since the PMS is close to the BS, the channel between them can be modeled by a line-of-sight (LOS) channel. Specifically, the channel from the  $i$ -th antenna (RF chain) to the  $i$ -th sub-PMS is given by  $\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{B2P},i}^{T} = \alpha_{\mathrm{B2P}}\mathbf{a}_{i}^{T}$ , where  $\alpha_{\mathrm{B2P}}$  denotes the path loss coefficient given by  $G\frac{A_{\mathrm{e}}}{4\pi d_{\mathrm{B2P}}^2}$ , where  $G$  is the antenna gain,  $A_{\mathrm{e}}$  is the effective area of each reflecting element perpendicularly to the direction of propagation, and  $d_{\mathrm{B2P}}$  is the distance from the BS to the PMS.  $\mathbf{a}_{i}^{T}$  is the array response vector of the  $i$ -th sub-PMS, whose elements have unit amplitude.

Let  $\mathbf{c} = \beta [e^{j\theta_1},\dots,e^{j\theta_n},\dots,e^{j\theta_N}]^T$  denote the phase shift beam, where  $\theta_{n}\in [0,2\pi)$  and  $\gamma \in [0,1]$  are phase shift and amplitude coefficient, respectively. The amplitude coefficient is given by  $\beta = \gamma \alpha_{\mathrm{B2P}}$  with  $\gamma$  depicting the energy reflection efficiency of the PMS, while the impact of the array response vector  $\mathbf{a}_i$  is reflected in the phase shifts of  $\mathbf{c}$ .

In practice, the reflecting elements are controlled by the digital to analog converters (DACs), hence have finite phase shifts due to limited DAC resolution. Without loss of generality, we use  $\mathcal{Q}$  to denote the set of all possible values of  $\theta_{n}$ , which has a cardinality of  $M_{\mathrm{ph}}$ . Similarly, the set of all possible phase shift beams are denoted by  $\mathcal{C}$ , which has a cardinality of  $M = M_{\mathrm{ph}}^{N}$ .

We assume block-fading channels, i.e., the channels remain the same during each coherence interval and vary independently between different coherence intervals. The entire communication process can be separated into two phases during each coherence interval, namely, channel estimation and multicasting transmission, which we elaborate in the ensuing sections.

# III. CHANNEL ESTIMATION

The proposed channel estimation scheme consists of two steps. In the first step, beam training is performed to acquire the optimal phase shift beam. In the second step, the equivalent channels are estimated.

# A. Beam Training

Since the cardinality of phase shift beam set increases exponentially with the number of reflecting elements, the complexity of conventional exhaustive beam searching approach quickly becomes prohibitive. Responding to this, we propose a novel beam training algorithm.

Specifically, during the beam training phase, all  $K$  users simultaneously transmit unmodulated frequency tones to the BS. For user  $k$ , the transmitted signal is denoted by  $x_{k} = \sqrt{p_{k}} s$ , where  $p_{k}$  is the power and  $s$  is the frequency tone of unit power.

For any  $k$ , we assume that  $\mathbb{E}\{\| \sqrt{p_k} \mathbf{g}_k \|^2\} = \varepsilon_r$ , with  $\varepsilon_r$  being the average received power. Also,  $\mathbf{g}_k$  denotes the channel between the PMS and the  $k$ -th user and is defined as  $\mathbf{g}_k = \sqrt{\alpha_k} \mathbf{h}_k$ , where  $\alpha_k$  models the large-scale fading, and  $\mathbf{h}_k$  models the small-scale fading with elements being independent and identically distributed (i.i.d)  $\mathcal{CN}(0,1)$  RVs. Furthermore,  $\alpha_k$  is assumed to be constant and known as a priori. After simplifying  $\mathbb{E}\{\| \sqrt{p_k} \mathbf{g}_k \|^2\}$ , we have  $N \alpha_k p_k = \varepsilon_r$ .

The proposed beam training method works in a bisection manner, namely, at each stage, nearly half of the available beams will be eliminated. For instance, at the  $i$ -th stage, the BS chooses a pair of beams  $\mathbf{c}_{(i,j)}, j = 1,2$ , which have the weakest correlation from the current beam set  $\mathcal{C}_i$ . As such, the received signal after combining can be written as

$$
\mathbf {r} _ {(i, j)} = \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \mathbf {C} _ {(i, j)} \mathbf {g} _ {k} x _ {k} + \mathbf {n} _ {(i, j)}, j = 1, 2, \tag {1}
$$

where  $\mathbf{C}_{(i,j)}\in \mathbb{C}^{N_{\mathrm{RF}}\times N}$  is a block diagonal matrix defined by

$$
\mathbf {C} _ {(i, j)} = \operatorname {d i a g} \left\{\mathbf {c} _ {(i, j), 1} ^ {T}, \dots , \mathbf {c} _ {(i, j), n} ^ {T}, \dots , \mathbf {c} _ {(i, j), N _ {\mathrm {R F}}} ^ {T} \right\} \tag {2}
$$

with  $\mathbf{c}_{(i,j),n}^T\triangleq [c_{(i,j),n}^1,\dots,c_{(i,j),n}^k,\dots,c_{(i,j),n}^L ]^T\in \mathbb{C}^{L\times 1}$  being the phase shift vector of the  $n$  -th sub-PSM.

The next step is to compare the received power  $\| \mathbf{r}_{(i,1)}\|^2$  with  $\| \mathbf{r}_{(i,2)}\|^2$ . Let  $j^{\star} = \underset{j=1,2}{\arg \max} \| \mathbf{r}_{(i,j)}\|^2$  and  $j^{-\star} =$

$\arg \min_{j = 1,2}\| \mathbf{r}_{(i,j)}\|^2$  It is intuitive that the optimal beam is more likely to have stronger correlation with  $\mathbf{c}_{(i,j^*)}$ . With this key observation, the number of training beams can be approximately halved by removing the beams which have weaker correlation with  $\mathbf{c}_{(i,j^*)}$ . Specifically, the beam  $\mathbf{c} \in \mathcal{C}_i$  satisfying  $\mathbf{c}_{(i,j^*)}^H\mathbf{c} \leq \mathbf{c}_{(i,j^*)}^H\mathbf{c}$  will be removed, and the remaining beams make up a new beam set  $\mathcal{C}_{i + 1}$ . The process then continues until the cardinality of  $\mathcal{C}_{i + 1}$  becomes one. The pseudo-code of the proposed beam training method is summarized in Algorithm 1.

Remark 1. Since our proposed beam training method works in a bisection manner, a much lower complexity of  $O(\log_2(M_{\mathrm{ph}}^N))$  can be achieved, compared with the complexity of exhaustive beam searching  $O(M_{\mathrm{ph}}^N)$ .

# B. Equivalent Channel Estimation

Denote  $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_k\triangleq \mathbf{Ch}_k\in \mathbb{C}^{N_{\mathrm{RF}}\times 1}$  and define the equivalent channel between the BS and the  $k$  -th user as  $\bar{\mathbf{g}}_k = \sqrt{\alpha_k}\bar{\mathbf{h}}_k\in$ $\mathbb{C}^{N_{\mathrm{RF}}\times 1}$ . Note that  $\mathbf{C}$  is the phase shift matrix corresponding to the optimal phase shift beam obtained in the beam training phase.

Then we estimate the equivalent channel through uplink training, where all  $K$  users simultaneously transmit orthogonal pilot sequences to the BS. Let  $\tau_{c}$  be the length of the coherence interval (in symbols), and  $\tau_{p}$  be the uplink training duration (in symbols) per coherence interval such that  $\tau_{p} < \tau_{c}$ . Denote the pilot sequence used by the  $k$ -th user,  $k = 1,2,\dots,K$ , by  $\sqrt{\tau_p}\varphi_k\in \mathbb{C}^{\tau_p\times 1}$ , where  $\| \varphi_k\|^2 = 1$ . To ensure the orthogonality of the pilot sequences, i.e.  $\varphi_i^H\varphi_j = 0, i\neq j$ , it is required that  $\tau_{p}\geq K$ . Furthermore, we assume  $\tau_{p} = K$ .

Then, the  $N_{\mathrm{RF}} \times \tau_p$  received pilot matrix at the BS can be expressed as

$$
\mathbf {Y} _ {p} = \sqrt {\tau_ {p} \rho_ {p}} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \bar {\mathbf {g}} _ {k} \varphi_ {k} ^ {H} + \mathbf {W} _ {p}, \tag {9}
$$

where  $\rho_{p}$  is the normalized signal to noise ratio (SNR) of each pilot symbol,  $\mathbf{W}_p\in \mathbb{C}^{N\times \tau_p}$  is the additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) matrix, whose elements are i.i.d.  $\mathcal{CN}(0,1)$  RVs.

To estimate  $\bar{\mathbf{g}}_k$ , we first multiply  $\mathbf{Y}_p$  by  $\varphi_k$ , which gives

$$
\mathbf {y} _ {p, k} = \mathbf {Y} _ {p} \varphi_ {k} = \sqrt {\tau_ {p} \rho_ {p}} \overline {{\mathbf {g}}} _ {k} + \mathbf {n} _ {p, k}, \tag {10}
$$

where  $\mathbf{n}_{p,k} = \mathbf{W}_p\varphi_k$ . The BS then adopts the minimum mean-square (MMSE) method to estimate the equivalent channel, as such, the equivalent channel  $\bar{\mathbf{g}}_k$  can be decomposed as

$$
\bar {\mathbf {g}} _ {k} = \hat {\bar {\mathbf {g}}} _ {k} + \mathbf {e} _ {k}, \tag {11}
$$

where  $\hat{\bar{\mathbf{g}}}_k$  is the estimation of  $\bar{\mathbf{g}}_k$ ,  $\mathbf{e}_k$  is the estimation error.

To obtain the distribution of the estimated equivalent channel, we first give an important proposition corresponding to the distribution of the equivalent channel.

Proposition 2. With finite number of phase shifts, the elements of  $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_k = \mathbf{Ch}_k$  can be modeled as i.i.d. random variables  $\mathcal{CN}(u,\delta^2)$  with

$$
u = \frac {L \beta}{2} \sqrt {\frac {\pi}{K}} \frac {M _ {\mathrm {p h}}}{\pi} \sin \left(\frac {\pi}{M _ {\mathrm {p h}}}\right), \tag {12}
$$

$$
\delta^ {2} = L \beta^ {2} \left\{1 - \frac {\pi}{4 K} \left(\frac {M _ {\mathrm {p h}}}{\pi} \sin \left(\frac {\pi}{M _ {\mathrm {p h}}}\right)\right) ^ {2} \right\}. \tag {13}
$$

Proof. See Appendix A.

Remark 2. From Proposition 2, we can see that the deployment of the PMS can enhance the equivalent channel compared to the case without the PMS. Specifically, the strength of the channel without the PMS is  $\alpha_{k}$ , while the strength with the PMS is given by  $\alpha_{k}(u^{2} + \delta^{2})$ , indicating that an asymptotic gain in the order of  $\mathcal{O}(L^2)$  can be achieved. This is because the PMS not only achieves the phase shift beamforming gain of order  $L$  but also captures an inherent aperture gain of order  $L$  by collecting more signal power.

Based on Proposition 2 and the MMSE estimation property,  $\mathbf{e}_k$  and  $\hat{\mathbf{g}}_k$  are complex Gaussian distributed, and they are independent of each other. Then, we have the following proposition:

Proposition 3. The elements of  $\hat{\mathbf{g}}_k$  and  $\mathbf{e}_k$  are Gaussian RVs with the distributions  $\mathcal{CN}\left(u_{p,k},\delta_{p,k}^2\right)$  and  $\mathcal{CN}\left(0,\delta_{e,k}^2\right)$  respectively, where  $u_{p,k} = \sqrt{\alpha_k} u,\delta_{p,k}^2 = \frac{\tau_p\rho_p\alpha_k^2\delta^4}{1 + \tau_p\rho_p\alpha_k\delta^2},\delta_{e,k}^2 = \frac{\alpha_k\delta^2}{1 + \tau_p\rho_p\alpha_k\delta^2}$ .

Proof. See Appendix B.

# IV. ACHIEVABLE RATE ANALYSIS

During the multicasting phase, the BS utilizes the estimated equivalent CSI to precode the signals. To keep the processing simple, the BS adopts the transmit matched filter (MF)  $\mathbf{W} = \hat{\bar{\mathbf{G}}}^*$ , then the received signal at all users is given by

$$
\mathbf {y} = \sqrt {\rho} \bar {\mathbf {G}} ^ {T} \mathbf {W} \sqrt {\bar {\mathbf {P}}} \mathbf {s} + \mathbf {n}, \tag {14}
$$

where  $\bar{\mathbf{G}} = [\mathbf{g}_1,\dots \mathbf{g}_k,\dots ,\mathbf{g}_K]$ $\rho$  is the total average transmit power (normalized by the noise power),  $\mathbf{P} =$  diag  $\{\frac{\eta_1}{E\left\{\| \mathbf{w}_1\|^2\right\}},\dots ,\frac{\eta_i}{E\left\{\| \mathbf{w}_i\|^2\right\}},\dots ,\frac{\eta_K}{E\left\{\| \mathbf{w}_K\|^2\right\}}\}$  is the power control matrix with the power control coefficient  $\eta_{k}$ $\mathbf{s} =$ $[s,\ldots ,s,\ldots ,s]^T$  is the data symbol vector satisfying  $\mathbb{E}\{|s|^2\} = 1$  and  $\mathbf{n}\sim \mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_K)$  denotes the noise.

Noticing that  $\bar{\mathbf{G}} = \bar{\mathbf{G}} +\mathbf{E}$ , the above equation can be rewritten as

$$
\mathbf {y} = \sqrt {\rho} \hat {\bar {\mathbf {G}}} ^ {T} \hat {\bar {\mathbf {G}}} ^ {*} \sqrt {\mathbf {P}} \mathbf {s} + \sqrt {\rho} \mathbf {E} ^ {T} \hat {\bar {\mathbf {G}}} ^ {*} \sqrt {\mathbf {P}} \mathbf {s} + \mathbf {n}. \tag {15}
$$

$$
R _ {k} = \log_ {2} \left(1 + \frac {\rho N _ {\mathrm {R F}} ^ {2} \left\{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\frac {\eta_ {i}}{u _ {p , i} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}} u _ {p , k} u _ {p , i} + \sqrt {\frac {\eta_ {k}}{u _ {p , k} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , k} ^ {2}}} \delta_ {p , k} ^ {2} \right\} ^ {2}}{1 + \alpha_ {k} \rho \delta^ {2} N _ {\mathrm {R F}} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\frac {\eta_ {i} u _ {p , i} ^ {2}}{u _ {p , i} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}}\right) ^ {2} + \alpha_ {k} \rho \left(u ^ {2} + \delta^ {2}\right) N _ {\mathrm {R F}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \frac {\eta_ {i} \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}{u _ {p , i} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}}\right). \tag {22}
$$

Then, the received signal at the  $k$ -th user is given by

$$
y _ {k} = \hat {\mathbf {g}} _ {k} ^ {T} \hat {\bar {\mathbf {G}}} ^ {*} \sqrt {\mathbf {P}} \mathbf {s} + \mathbf {e} _ {k} ^ {T} \hat {\bar {\mathbf {G}}} ^ {*} \sqrt {\mathbf {P}} \mathbf {s} + n _ {k} \tag {16}
$$

$$
\stackrel {(a)} {=} \sqrt {\rho} \left(\hat {\mathbf {g}} _ {k} ^ {T} + \mathbf {e} _ {k} ^ {T}\right) \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\frac {\eta_ {i}}{u _ {p , i} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}} \hat {\mathbf {g}} _ {i} ^ {*} s + n _ {k},
$$

where (a) follows the fact that  $\mathbb{E}\left\{\| \mathbf{w}_i\|^2\right\} = u_{p,i}^2 +\delta_{p,i}^2$

Next, without loss of generality, let us focus on the achievable rate of the  $k$ -th user. We consider the realistic case where the  $k$ -th user does not have access to the instantaneous CSI of the effective channel gain. Instead, the detection of desired signal  $s$  is based on the statistical CSI. As such, we can rewrite  $y_{k}$  as

$$
y _ {k} = \underbrace {\sqrt {\rho} \mathbb {E} \left\{\left(\hat {\mathbf {g}} _ {k} ^ {T} + \mathbf {e} _ {k} ^ {T}\right) \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\frac {\eta_ {i}}{u _ {p , i} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}} \hat {\mathbf {g}} _ {i} ^ {*} \right\}} _ {\text {d e s i r e d s i g n a l}} s + \underbrace {n _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {e f f}}} _ {\text {e f f e c t i v e n o i s e}}, \tag {17}
$$

where

$$
\begin{array}{l} n _ {k} ^ {\text {e f f}} = \underbrace {n _ {k}} _ {\text {n o i s e}} + \sqrt {\rho} \left(\hat {\mathbf {g}} _ {k} ^ {T} + \mathbf {e} _ {k} ^ {T}\right) \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\frac {\eta_ {i}}{u _ {p , i} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}} \hat {\mathbf {g}} _ {i} ^ {*} s \tag {18} \\ - \sqrt {\rho} \mathbb {E} \left\{\left(\hat {\tilde {\mathbf {g}}} _ {k} ^ {T} + \mathbf {e} _ {k} ^ {T}\right) \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\frac {\eta_ {i}}{u _ {p , i} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}} \hat {\tilde {\mathbf {g}}} _ {i} ^ {*} \right\} s. \\ \end{array}
$$

Capitalizing on the results in [21], the achievable rate of the  $k$ -th user can be expressed as<sup>5</sup>

$$
R _ {k} = \log_ {2} \left(1 + \frac {\left| A _ {k} \right| ^ {2}}{B _ {k} + 1}\right), \tag {19}
$$

with

$$
A _ {k} \triangleq \sqrt {\rho} \mathbb {E} \left\{\left(\hat {\tilde {\mathbf {g}}} _ {k} ^ {T} + \mathbf {e} _ {k} ^ {T}\right) \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\frac {\eta_ {i}}{u _ {p , i} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}} \hat {\tilde {\mathbf {g}}} _ {i} ^ {*} \right\}, \tag {20}
$$

$$
B _ {k} \triangleq \rho \mathbb {E} \left\{\left| \left(\hat {\hat {\mathbf {g}}} _ {k} ^ {T} + \mathbf {e} _ {k} ^ {T}\right) \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\frac {\eta_ {i}}{u _ {p , i} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}} \hat {\hat {\mathbf {g}}} _ {i} ^ {*} \right| ^ {2} \right\} - | A _ {k} | ^ {2}, \tag {21}
$$

being the desired signal power and leakage power, respectively.

Then, we have the following important result:

Theorem 1. The achievable rate of the  $k$ -th user is given by (22) on the top of the next page.

Proof. Refer to Appendix C.

Theorem 1 presents a closed-form expression for the achievable rate which reveals the impact of key system parameters, such as the number of phase shifts, reflecting elements, RF

chains and users, as well as the impact of imperfect channel estimation on the achievable rate. For instance,  $R_{k}$  is an increasing function with respect to  $N_{\mathrm{RF}}$ . Besides, it can be seen that the desired signal power decreases with the equivalent channel estimation error, indicating that we can improve the channel estimation accuracy, for example by increasing the pilot power.

After deriving the individual rate for any user  $k \in \{1, 2, \dots, K\}$ , the multicast rate  $R$  can be obtained as

$$
R = \min  _ {k = 1, 2, \dots , K} R _ {k}. \tag {23}
$$

# V. POWER CONTROL

To maximize the multicast rate, we formulate the following power control problem:

$$
\max  _ {\{\eta_ {k} \}} \min  _ {k = 1, \ldots , K} R _ {k},
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l l} \text {s . t .} & \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \eta_ {k} = 1 \\ & \eta_ {k} \geq 0, k = 1, \dots , K. \end{array} \tag {24}
$$

In the general setting, the above optimization problem is a non-convex problem, hence is difficult to solve. Responding to this, we consider some asymptotic regime, where closed-form solutions can be derived.

# A. Large pilot power

We first consider the scenario where the pilot power is sufficiently large, and we have the following important result: Theorem 2. As  $\rho_{p} \to \infty$ , the optimal power control coefficients are  $\eta_{k} = \frac{1}{K}, k = 1, \dots, K$ , and the corresponding multicast rate is given by

$$
R = \tag {25}
$$

$$
\log_ {2} \left(1 + \frac {K N _ {\mathrm {R F}} L ^ {3} \tilde {u} _ {0} ^ {4}}{K L ^ {2} \tilde {u} _ {0} ^ {2} (1 - \tilde {u} _ {0} ^ {2}) + (L \tilde {u} _ {0} ^ {2} + 1 - \tilde {u} _ {0} ^ {2}) \left(\frac {1}{\beta^ {2} N _ {\mathrm {R F}} \rho \alpha} + 1 - \tilde {u} _ {0} ^ {2}\right)}\right),
$$

where  $\alpha \triangleq \min_{k = 1,\dots ,K}\alpha_{k}$ ,  $\tilde{u}_0\triangleq \frac{u_0}{\beta} = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4K}}\frac{M_{\mathrm{ph}}}{\pi}\sin \left(\frac{\pi}{M_{\mathrm{ph}}}\right)$ .

Proof. Refer to Appendix D.

![](images/35fa0c2006869d04b1f1eb386fec6a24302458def711ca7771aa18975ca97577.jpg)

Theorem 2 shows that, with large pilot power, the multicast rate is an increasing function with respect to  $L$ . This is because the equivalent channel can be enhanced by increasing the number of reflecting elements. Also, as the amplitude reflection coefficient increases, the achievable rate becomes larger, due to the fact that larger amplitude reflection coefficient implies less power loss when the transmit signal is reflected by the PMS. In addition, the multicast rate is a decreasing function with respect to  $K$ . This is reasonable because with fewer users, highly directional beams can be obtained. Furthermore, the multicast rate is constrained by the large-fading coefficient of the weakest user, but this negative effect of the weakest user can be compensated by increasing the number of reflecting elements or RF chains.

# B. A large number of RF chains

Theorem 3. When  $L$  is fixed while  $N_{\mathrm{RF}} \to \infty$ , the optimal power control coefficients are

$$
\eta_ {k} = \frac {\alpha_ {k} \left(u _ {p , k} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , k} ^ {2}\right)}{\phi \delta_ {p , k} ^ {4}}, k = 1, \dots , K, \tag {26}
$$

where  $\phi = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{\alpha_k (u_{p,k}^2 + \delta_{p,k}^2)}{\delta_{p,k}^4}$ . And the corresponding multicast rate is given by

$$
\begin{array}{l} R = \tag {27} \\ \log_ {2} \left(1 + \frac {N _ {\mathrm {R F}} \left\{\frac {u ^ {2}}{\delta_ {2}} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \frac {1 + \tau_ {p} \rho_ {p} \alpha_ {k} \delta^ {2}}{\tau_ {p} \rho_ {p} \alpha_ {k} \delta^ {2}} + 1 \right\} ^ {2}}{\frac {u ^ {2}}{\delta^ {2}} \left(\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \frac {1 + \tau_ {p} \rho_ {p} \alpha_ {k} \delta^ {2}}{\tau_ {p} \rho_ {p} \alpha_ {k} \delta^ {2}}\right) ^ {2} + \left(\frac {u ^ {2}}{\delta^ {2}} + 1\right) \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \frac {1 + \tau_ {p} \rho_ {p} \alpha_ {k} \delta^ {2}}{\tau_ {p} \rho_ {p} \alpha_ {k} \delta^ {2}}}\right). \\ \end{array}
$$

Proof. Refer to Appendix E.

Theorem 3 implies that with a large number of RF chains, the effect of noise vanishes, and the multicast rate is determined by the channel conditions of all users. Moreover, the maximum signal to interference plus noise ratio (SINR) is proportional to  $N_{\mathrm{RF}}$ , indicating that increasing the number of RF chains can significantly improve the multicast rate. Besides, increasing the pilot power can improve the multicast rate, due to more accurate channel estimation.

Proposition 4. The power control coefficients  $\eta_{k}$  is a decreasing function with respect to  $\alpha_{k}$ , indicating that more power should be allocated to users with poor channel conditions.

Proof. Utilizing the results given by Proposition 3, the optimal power control coefficient can be rewritten as  $\eta_{k} = \frac{u^{2} + \tilde{\delta}_{p,k}^{2}}{\phi\tilde{\delta}_{p,k}^{4}}$  where  $\tilde{\delta}_{p,k}^2 \triangleq \frac{\tau_p\rho_p\alpha_k\delta^4}{1 + \tau_p\rho_p\alpha_k\delta^2}$ . Let  $\tilde{\eta}_k \triangleq \phi \tilde{\eta}_k$ . Due to  $\frac{\partial\tilde{\eta}_k}{\partial\delta_{p,k}} < 0$  and  $\frac{\partial\tilde{\delta}_{p,k}}{\partial\alpha_k} > 0$ , we have  $\frac{\partial\tilde{\eta}_k}{\partial\alpha_k} = \frac{\partial\tilde{\eta}_k}{\partial\delta_{p,k}}\frac{\partial\tilde{\delta}_{p,k}}{\partial\alpha_k} < 0$ , indicating that  $\tilde{\eta}_k$  is a decreasing function with respect to  $\alpha_{k}$ . To this end, noticing that  $\eta_{k} = 1 / (1 + \frac{1}{\tilde{\eta}_{k}}\sum_{i\neq k}^{K}\tilde{\eta}_{i})$  increases with  $\tilde{\eta}_k$ , we complete the proof.

# C. A large number of reflecting elements

Theorem 4. When  $N_{\mathrm{RF}}$  is fixed while  $L \to \infty$ , the optimal power control coefficients are

$$
\eta_ {k} = \frac {1}{K}, k = 1, \dots , K, \tag {28}
$$

and the multicast rate is given by

$$
R = \log_ {2} \left\{1 + \frac {\pi N _ {\mathrm {R F}} L \left(\frac {M _ {\mathrm {p h}}}{\pi} \sin \frac {\pi}{M _ {\mathrm {p h}}}\right) ^ {2}}{\left\{4 - \frac {\pi}{K} \left(\frac {M _ {\mathrm {p h}}}{\pi} \sin \frac {\pi}{M _ {\mathrm {p h}}}\right) ^ {2} \right\} (K + 1)} \right\}. \tag {29}
$$

Proof. Refer to Appendix F.

Theorem 4 shows that with a large  $L$ , the effect of noise as well as the equivalent channel estimation error vanishes. The reason is that a large number of reflecting elements can significantly enhance the equivalent channel. Also, as the

number of reflecting elements increases, the amplitude reflection coefficient becomes irrelevant, indicating that increasing the number of reflecting elements can compensate for the power loss caused by PMS reflection. In addition, the SINR is proportional to  $N = N_{\mathrm{RF}}L$ , which implies that the multicast rate can be greatly improved by increasing the number of reflecting elements.

# 1) The impact of phase shift number:

Proposition 5. With large  $L$ , the multicast rate is an increasing function with respect to the phase shift number  $M_{\mathrm{ph}}$ . Furthermore, when the phase shift number is sufficiently large, the multicast rate is given by

$$
R = \log_ {2} \left\{1 + \frac {\pi N _ {\mathrm {R F}} L K}{(4 K - \pi) \{K + 1 \}} \right\}. \tag {30}
$$

Proof. Starting from  $R$  given in Theorem 4, we can see that  $\frac{M_{\mathrm{ph}}}{\pi} \sin \left( \frac{\pi}{M_{\mathrm{ph}}} \right)$  and  $R$  are increasing functions with respect to  $M_{\mathrm{ph}}$  and  $\frac{M_{\mathrm{ph}}}{\pi} \sin \left( \frac{\pi}{M_{\mathrm{ph}}} \right)$ , respectively. Thus,  $R$  increases with  $M_{\mathrm{ph}}$ . Noticing that  $\lim_{M_{\mathrm{ph}} \to \infty} \frac{M_{\mathrm{ph}}}{\pi} \sin \frac{\pi}{M_{\mathrm{ph}}} = 1$ , we can obtain the desired result.

Proposition 5 is rather intuitive since highly accurate beam can be obtained with high-resolution phase shifts. Moreover, as the phase shift number becomes sufficiently large, the multicast rate becomes independent of  $M_{\mathrm{ph}}$  and gradually converges to a limit, indicating that the gain of using high-resolution phase shift diminishes gradually.

# 2) The impact of user number:

Proposition 6. With a large number of reflecting elements, the multicast rate is a decreasing function with respect to the user number. Furthermore, with a large number of users, the multicast rate is given by

$$
R = \log_ {2} \left\{1 + \frac {\pi N _ {\mathrm {R F}} L \left(\frac {M _ {\mathrm {p h}}}{\pi} \sin \frac {\pi}{M _ {\mathrm {p h}}}\right) ^ {2}}{4 K} \right\}. \tag {31}
$$

Proof. Starting from Theorem 4, we can easily obtain the desired result.  $\square$

From Proposition 6, it can be seen that with massive users, the SINR is inversely proportional to the user number, which implies that increasing the number of users can severely degrade the multicast rate. To compensate this rate loss, it is desired to employ a large number of reflecting elements.

3) Increasing  $N_{RF}$  V.S. Increasing  $L$ : Although a higher rate can be achieved by increasing either  $N_{\mathrm{RF}}$  or  $L$ , it is better to increase the number of reflecting elements rather than the number of RF chains, because the power consumption and hardware cost of PMS are much lower than that of RF chains. In addition, with massive reflecting elements, the negative effects of noise, estimation error as well as the amplitude reflection coefficient can be effectively compensated, while with a large number of RF chains, only the effects of noise can be mitigated.

# APPENDIX A PROOF OF PROPOSITION 2

Without loss of generality, we focus on the  $n$ -th element of  $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_k$ :  $\bar{h}_{k,n} = \mathbf{c}_n^T\mathbf{h}_{k,n} = \sum_{l = 1}^L c_n^l h_{k,n}^l$ . Since we assume a large number of reflecting elements and limited number of RF chains,  $L = \frac{N}{N_{\mathrm{RF}}}$  is large. According to the central-limit theorem,  $\bar{h}_{k,n}$  approximately follows normal distribution  $\mathcal{CN}(Lu_0,L\delta_0^2)$ , where  $u_{0}$  and  $\delta_0^2$  are the mean and variance of  $c_{n}^{l}h_{k,n}^{l}$ , respectively. In the following, we try to derive  $u_{0}$  and  $\delta_0^2$ .

# (1) Compute  $u_{0}$

Denote the phase error resulted from the finite phase shift number  $M_{\mathrm{ph}}$  by  $\Delta \theta$ , and we have

$$

\begin{array}{l} B _ {k} = \alpha_ {k} \rho \delta^ {2} N _ {\mathrm {R F}} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\frac {\eta_ {i} u _ {p , i} ^ {2}}{u _ {p , i} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}}\right) ^ {2} \tag {57} \\ + \alpha_ {k} \rho \left(u ^ {2} + \delta^ {2}\right) N _ {\mathrm {R F}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \frac {\eta_ {i} \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}{u _ {p , i} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}. \\ \end{array}
$$

Combining 1) and 2), we obtain the desired result.

# APPENDIX D PROOF OF THEOREM 2

As  $\rho_p\to \infty$  , we have  $u_{p,i} = \sqrt{\alpha_i} u$  and  $\delta_{p,i}^2 = \alpha_i\delta^2$  , based on which, the achievable rate of the  $k$  -th user can be expressed as

$$
R _ {k} = \tag {58}
$$

$$
\log_ {2} \left\{1 + \frac {\frac {N _ {\mathrm {R E}} ^ {2} \rho \alpha_ {k}}{u ^ {2} + \delta^ {2}} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\eta_ {i}} u ^ {2} + \sqrt {\eta_ {k}} \delta^ {2}\right) ^ {2}}{1 + \frac {N _ {\mathrm {R E}} \rho \alpha_ {k}}{u ^ {2} + \delta^ {2}} u ^ {2} \delta^ {2} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\eta_ {i}}\right) ^ {2} + N _ {\mathrm {R F}} \rho \alpha_ {k} \delta^ {2} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \eta_ {i}} \right\}.
$$

Noticing that  $\sum_{i=1}^{K} \eta_i = 1$ , we have

$$
R _ {k} = \tag {59}
$$

$$
\log_ {2} \left\{1 + \frac {\frac {N _ {\mathrm {R F}} ^ {2} \rho \alpha_ {k}}{u ^ {2} + \delta^ {2}} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\eta_ {i}} u ^ {2} + \sqrt {\eta_ {k}} \delta^ {2}\right) ^ {2}}{1 + \frac {N _ {\mathrm {R F}} \rho \alpha_ {k}}{u ^ {2} + \delta^ {2}} u ^ {2} \delta^ {2} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\eta_ {i}}\right) ^ {2} + N _ {\mathrm {R F}} \rho \alpha_ {k} \delta^ {2}} \right\}.
$$

Comparing  $\sum_{i=1}^{K} \sqrt{\eta_i} u^2$  with  $\sqrt{\eta_k} \delta^2$ , we have  $\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{K} \sqrt{\eta_i} u^2}{\sqrt{\eta_k} \delta^2} = \frac{L \tilde{u}_0}{1 - \tilde{u}_0} \left(1 + \sum_{i \neq k}^{K} \sqrt{\frac{\eta_i}{\eta_k}}\right)^2$ , which shows that  $\sum_{i=1}^{K} \sqrt{\eta_i} u^2$  is much greater than  $\sqrt{\eta_k} \delta^2$ , due to the fact that  $L$  and  $K$  are large in general. Thus, ignoring the term  $\sqrt{\eta_k} \delta^2$  in (59), we have the following approximation:

$$
R _ {k} \approx \tag {60}
$$

$$
\log_ {2} \left\{1 + \frac {\frac {N _ {\mathrm {R F}} ^ {2} \rho \alpha_ {k}}{u ^ {2} + \delta^ {2}} u ^ {4} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\eta_ {i}}\right) ^ {2}}{1 + \frac {N _ {\mathrm {R F}} \rho \alpha_ {k}}{u ^ {2} + \delta^ {2}} u ^ {2} \delta^ {2} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\eta_ {i}}\right) ^ {2} + N _ {\mathrm {R F}} \rho \alpha_ {k} \delta^ {2}} \right\}.
$$

Next, we consider the maximum power control problem, which can be formulated as

$$
\max  _ {\{\eta_ {i} \}} \min  _ {i = 1, \ldots , K} R _ {i},
$$

$$
s. t. \quad \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \eta_ {i} = 1, \eta_ {i} \geq 0, i = 1, \dots , K. \tag {61}
$$

According to (60), we can observe

$$
\min  _ {i = 1, \dots , K} R _ {i} = R _ {i ^ {*}}, i ^ {*} = \underset {i = 1, \dots , K} {\arg \min } \alpha_ {i}, \tag {62}
$$

based on which, the above optimization problem can be rewritten as

$$
\max  _ {\left\{\eta_ {i} \right\}} \log_ {2} \left\{1 + \frac {\frac {N _ {\mathrm {R F}} ^ {2} \rho \alpha}{u ^ {2} + \delta^ {2}} u ^ {4} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\eta_ {i}}\right) ^ {2}}{1 + \frac {N _ {\mathrm {R F}} \rho \alpha}{u ^ {2} + \delta^ {2}} u ^ {2} \delta^ {2} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\eta_ {i}}\right) ^ {2} + N _ {\mathrm {R F}} \rho \alpha \delta^ {2}} \right\}, \tag {63}
$$

$$
\text {s . t .} \quad \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \eta_ {i} = 1 \eta_ {i} \geq 0, i = 1, \dots , K,
$$

where we define  $\alpha \triangleq \min_{k = 1,\dots ,K}\alpha_k$

Noticing that the objective function is an increasing function with respect to  $\sum_{i=1}^{K} \sqrt{\eta_i}$ , the optimization problem is equivalent to

$$
\begin{array}{l l} \max  _ {\{\eta_ {i} \}} & \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\eta_ {i}}, \\ \text {s . t .} & \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \eta_ {i} = 1, \eta_ {i} \geq 0, i = 1, \dots , K. \end{array} \tag {64}
$$

Denote  $x_{i} \triangleq \sqrt{\eta_{i}}$ . Then we have a convex problem:

$$
\max  _ {\{x _ {i} \}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} x _ {i}, \tag {65}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {s . t .} \quad \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} x _ {i} ^ {2} = 1, x _ {i} \geq 0, i = 1, \dots , K. \end{array}
$$

By applying KKT conditions, we can obtain the optimal power control coefficients  $\eta_{i} = \frac{1}{K}, i = 1,\dots,K$ . To this end, substituting the optimal coefficients into the objective function of (63), we complete our proof.

# APPENDIX E PROOF OF THEOREM 3

Starting from Theorem 1, we have

$$
\begin{array}{l} \underset {N _ {\mathrm {R F}} \rightarrow \infty} {R _ {k}} = \tag {66} \\ \log_ {2} \left(1 + \frac {N _ {\mathrm {R F}} \left\{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\frac {\eta_ {i}}{u _ {p , i} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}} u _ {p , k} u _ {p , i} + \sqrt {\frac {\eta_ {k}}{u _ {p , k} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , k} ^ {2}}} \delta_ {p , k} ^ {2} \right\} ^ {2}}{\alpha_ {k} \delta^ {2} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\frac {\eta_ {i} u _ {p , i} ^ {2}}{u _ {p , i} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}}\right) ^ {2} + \alpha_ {k} (u ^ {2} + \delta^ {2}) \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \frac {\eta_ {i} \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}{u _ {p , i} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}}\right). \\ \end{array}
$$

Recall that  $u_{p,k} = \sqrt{\alpha_k} u$ , and the above equation can be written as

$$
\begin{array}{l} R _ {k} \underset {N _ {\mathrm {R F}} \rightarrow \infty} {=} \tag {67} \\ \log_ {2} \left(1 + \frac {N _ {\mathrm {R F}} \left\{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\frac {\eta_ {i} u _ {p , i} ^ {2}}{u _ {p , i} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}} u + \sqrt {\frac {\eta_ {k} \delta_ {p , k} ^ {4}}{\alpha_ {k} \left(u _ {p , k} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , k} ^ {2}\right)}} \right\} ^ {2}}{\delta^ {2} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\frac {\eta_ {i} u _ {p , i} ^ {2}}{u _ {p , i} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}}\right) ^ {2} + (u ^ {2} + \delta^ {2}) \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \frac {\eta_ {i} \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}{u _ {p , i} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}}\right). \\ \end{array}
$$

Next, we try to deal with the maximum power control problem:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \max  _ {\left\{\eta_ {i} \right\}} \min  _ {k = 1, \dots , K} R _ {k}, (68) \\ s. t. \quad \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \eta_ {i} = 1, \eta_ {i} \geq 0, i = 1, \dots , K, (68) \\ \end{array}
$$

which is equivalent to

$$
\begin{array}{l} \max  _ {\left\{\eta_ {i} \right\}} \min  _ {\substack {k = 1, \dots , K \\ K}} S I N R _ {k}, (69) \\ \begin{array}{l} \text {s . t .} \quad \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \eta_ {i} = 1, \eta_ {i} \geq 0, i = 1, \dots , K, \end{array} (69) \\ \end{array}
$$

where

$$
S I N R _ {k} = \frac {N _ {\mathrm {R F}} \left\{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\frac {\eta_ {i} u _ {p , i} ^ {2}}{u _ {p , i} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}} u + \sqrt {\frac {\eta_ {k} \delta_ {p , k} ^ {4}}{\alpha_ {k} \left(u _ {p , k} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , k} ^ {2}\right)}} \right\} ^ {2}}{\delta^ {2} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\frac {\eta_ {i} u _ {p , i} ^ {2}}{u _ {p , i} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}}\right) ^ {2} + (u ^ {2} + \delta^ {2}) \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \frac {\eta_ {i} \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}{u _ {p , i} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}}. \tag {70}
$$

With optimal power control coefficients, the following equation holds:  $SINR_{1} = \ldots = SINR_{k} = \ldots = SINR_{K}$ , which can be simplified as

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {\eta_ {1} \delta_ {p , 1} ^ {4}}{\alpha_ {1} \left(u _ {p , 1} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , 1} ^ {2}\right)} = \dots = \frac {\eta_ {k} \delta_ {p , k} ^ {4}}{\alpha_ {k} \left(u _ {p , k} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , k} ^ {2}\right)} \tag {71} \\ = \ldots = \frac {\eta_ {K} \delta_ {p , K} ^ {4}}{\alpha_ {K} \left(u _ {p , K} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , K} ^ {2}\right)}. \\ \end{array}
$$

Utilizing (71) and noticing  $\sum_{k=1}^{K} \eta_k = 1$ , we can obtain the

optimal power control coefficient

$$
\eta_ {k} = \frac {\alpha_ {k} \left(u _ {p , k} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , k} ^ {2}\right)}{\phi \delta_ {p , k} ^ {4}}, \phi = \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \frac {\alpha_ {k} \left(u _ {p , k} ^ {2} + \delta_ {p , k} ^ {2}\right)}{\delta_ {p , k} ^ {4}}. \tag {72}
$$

Then, substituting (72) into (67), we have

$$
R = \log_ {2} \left(1 + \frac {N _ {\mathrm {R F}} \left(u \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \frac {\sqrt {\alpha_ {i}} u _ {p , i}}{\delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}} + 1\right) ^ {2}}{\delta^ {2} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \frac {\sqrt {\alpha_ {i}} u _ {p , i}}{\delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}\right) ^ {2} + \left(u ^ {2} + \delta^ {2}\right) \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \frac {\alpha_ {i}}{\delta_ {p , i} ^ {2}}}\right). \tag {73}
$$

Finally, substituting the results given by Proposition 3 into (73) yields the desired result.

# APPENDIX F PROOF OF THEOREM 4

As  $L \to \infty$ , we have  $\delta^2 = L\beta^2\left(1 - \tilde{u}_0^2\right) \to \infty$ . Then, using the results given in Proposition 3, we have  $\delta_{p,i}^2 = \alpha_i\delta^2\frac{\tau_p\rho_p\alpha_k}{\frac{1}{\delta^2} + \tau_p\rho_p\alpha_i\delta^2} \approx \alpha_i\delta^2$ .

Leveraging the above equation and noticing that  $u_{p,i} = \sqrt{\alpha_i u}$ , we obtain

$$
R _ {k} = \log_ {2} \left\{1 + \frac {\frac {N _ {\mathrm {R F}} ^ {2} \rho \alpha_ {k}}{u ^ {2} + \delta^ {2}} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\eta_ {i}} u ^ {2} + \sqrt {\eta_ {k}} \delta^ {2}\right) ^ {2}}{1 + \frac {N _ {\mathrm {R F}} \rho \alpha_ {k}}{u ^ {2} + \delta^ {2}} u ^ {2} \delta^ {2} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\eta_ {i}}\right) ^ {2} + N _ {\mathrm {R F}} \rho \alpha_ {k} \delta^ {2}} \right\}. \tag {74}
$$

Substituting  $u = L\beta \tilde{u}_0$  and  $\delta^2 = L\beta^2 - L\beta \tilde{u}_0^2$  into (74) and after some manipulations, we express  $R_k$  as (75) given on the top of the next page.

Neglecting the small items that do not scale with  $L^2$ , the above equation can be simplified as

$$
R _ {k} = \log_ {2} \left\{1 + \frac {N _ {\mathrm {R F}} L \tilde {u} _ {0} ^ {2} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\eta_ {i}}\right) ^ {2}}{\left(1 - \tilde {u} _ {0} ^ {2}\right) \left\{\left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \sqrt {\eta_ {i}}\right) ^ {2} + 1 \right\}} \right\}, \tag {76}
$$

which is an increasing function with respect to  $\sum_{i=1}^{K} \sqrt{\eta_i}$ . Then, following the similar process in the proof of Theorem 2, we can obtain the optimal maximum power control coefficients  $\eta_k = \frac{1}{K}, k = 1, \ldots, K$ .

To this end, substituting the optimal power control coefficients into (76) yields the desired result.
