# Performance Analysis of Intelligent Reflective Surfaces for Wireless Communication

Dhanushka Kudathanthirige, Dulaj Gunasinghe and Gayan Amarasuriya

Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Southern Illinois University, Carbondale, IL, USA 62901

Email: {dhanushka.kudathanthirige,dulaj.gunasinghe,gayan.baduge} @siu.edu

Abstract—A statistical characterization of the fundamental performance bounds of an intelligent reflective surface (IRS) intended for aiding wireless communications is presented. To this end, the outage probability, average symbol error probability and achievable rate bounds are derived in closed-form. By virtue of an asymptotic analysis in high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) regime, the achievable diversity order is derived. Thereby, we show that a diversity gain in the order of the number of passive reflective elements embedded within the IRS can be achieved with only controllable phase adjustments. Thus, IRS has a great potential of boosting the wireless performance by intelligently controlling the propagation channels without employing additional active radio frequency chains.

# I. INTRODUCTION

Over the past five generations of wireless standards, performance of the transmitter and receiver has been optimized to mitigate various transmission impairments of propagation channels, which are generally assumed to be uncontrollable in the wireless system designer's perspective. However, owing to the recent research advancements of meta-materials and meta-surfaces, a novel concept of coating physical objects such as building walls and windows with intelligent reflective surfaces (IRSs) with reconfigurable reflective properties has been envisioned [1]-[4]. The ultimate goal of IRS is to enable a smart wireless propagation environment by controlling the reflective properties of the underlying channels [4].

An IRS comprises of a very large number of passive reflective elements, which are capable of reconfiguring properties of electromagnetic (EM) waves impinging upon them. On one hand, reflected EM waves can be added constructively at a desired receiver by intelligently controlling phase-shifts at each reflective element to boost the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and coverage. On the other hand, a reflected signal can be made to add destructively and thereby to mitigate co-channel interference towards an undesired direction. Moreover, IRS facilitates full-duplex reflections, and hence, large blockages between a pair of transmitter-receiver can be circumvented through smart reflections without trading-off additional time, frequency or power resources. Since an IRS does not generate new EM waves, costly transmit radio-frequency (RF) chains/amplifiers in relays can be eliminated and thereby improving the energy efficiency. Thus, the concept of IRS presents a paradigm shift in wireless communication research.

The prototypes of meta-surfaces and meta-tiles with artificial thin film of EM materials, which can be used to coat objects within a smart wireless environment, have already been developed [5], [6]. In [9], precoder optimization techniques for a multi-antenna transmitter in the presence of an IRS are investigated to maximize the received SNR. Motivation and our contribution: The key idea of an IRS is to enable a programmable control over the wireless propagation channels. This necessitates innovations of radically novel techniques for modeling, designing and analyzing wireless systems as the resulting smart propagation channels can now be able to interact with EM waves impinging upon them in a software-controlled manner. Although several important attempts have recently been made [9]–[13], the fundamental research on IRS in wireless communication's perspective is still at an embryonic stage. To this end, our work presents a performance analysis framework for deriving the fundamental bounds pertaining to an IRS intended for aiding the end-to-end communication between a single-antenna source  $(S)$  and a destination  $(D)$ . Thus, tight bounds/approximations for the outage probability, average symbol error rate (SER), and average achievable rate are derived in closed-form. The accuracy of our analysis is validated through a rigorous set of Monte-Carlo simulations. We obtain useful design insights about the achievable diversity order via an asymptotic analysis in the high SNR regime. We reveal that the achievable diversity order is equal to the number of passive reflective elements  $(M)$ , and it is achieved without using any active RF chains at the IRS. Thus, by virtue of smart passive reflections, an  $M$ -fold diversity gain can be achieved with respect to a single-input single-output (SISO) channel. Through our analysis, we verify that an IRS has a true potential of boosting the reliability of end-to-end wireless communication with only passive controllable phase-shifts.

# II. SYSTEM, CHANNEL AND SIGNAL MODELS

# A. System model

We consider an end-to-end wireless communication set-up in which a single-antenna source  $(S)$  communicates with a single-antenna destination  $(D)$  through an IRS having  $M$ -passive reflective elements (see Fig. 1). Phase-shifts of waves impinging upon the IRS are assumed to be controlled perfectly to implement coherent/constructive signal combining at  $D$ , while the corresponding amplitudes are attenuated by a factor defined as per reflective coefficient. The direct channel between  $S$  and  $D$  is assumed to be unavailable due to severe blockage effects. The channel between  $S$  and the  $m$ th reflective element is denoted by  $h_m$ , while the channel between the  $m$ th reflective element and  $D$  is given by  $g_m$ . The channel envelopes are assumed to be independent Rayleigh distributed, and hence,  $h_m$  and  $g_m$  are modeled as

$$
h _ {m} = \sqrt {\zeta_ {h _ {m}}} \tilde {h} _ {m} \quad \text {a n d} \quad g _ {m} = \sqrt {\zeta_ {g _ {m}}} \tilde {g} _ {m}, \tag {1}
$$

where  $\tilde{h}_m$  and  $\tilde{g}_m$  follow complex Gaussian distribution with zero mean and unit variance;  $\tilde{h}_m \sim \mathcal{CN}(0,1)$  and  $\tilde{g}_m \sim \mathcal{CN}(0,1)$ . In (1),  $\zeta_{h_m}$  and  $\zeta_{g_m}$  capture the path-losses of the corresponding channels.

# B. Signal model

The signal transmitted by  $S$  is reflected by the IRS towards  $D$ . The received signal at  $D$  through  $M$  reflective elements can be written as

$$
y = \sqrt {P} \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {M} g _ {m} \eta_ {m} \mathrm {e} ^ {j \theta_ {m}} h _ {m} x + n, \tag {2}
$$

where  $x$  is the transmitted signal by  $S$  satisfying  $\mathbb{E}\left[|x|^2\right] = 1$  while  $P$  is the transmit power at  $S$ . Moreover,  $n$  is an additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) at D with zero mean and variance  $\sigma_N^2$  such that  $n \sim \mathcal{CN}(0, \sigma_N^2)$ . In (2),  $\eta_m$  and  $\theta_m$  represent the reflection coefficient and the phase-shift introduced by the  $m$ th reflective component of the IRS, respectively. Next, (2) can be alternatively written as [9]

$$
y = \sqrt {P} \mathbf {g} ^ {T} \boldsymbol {\Theta} \mathbf {h} x + n, \tag {3}
$$

where  $\mathbf{h} = [h_1, \dots, h_m, \dots, h_M]^T$  and  $\mathbf{g} = [g_1, \dots, g_m, \dots, g_M]^T$  capture the corresponding channel vectors, while  $\Theta = \mathrm{diag}([\eta_1\mathrm{e}^{j\theta_1}, \dots, \eta_m\mathrm{e}^{j\theta_m}, \dots, \eta_M\mathrm{e}^{j\theta_M}])$  is a

By using (3), the SNR is derived as

$$
\Gamma = \bar {\gamma} | \mathbf {g} ^ {T} \boldsymbol {\Theta} \mathbf {h} | ^ {2}, \tag {4}
$$

where  $\bar{\gamma} = P / \sigma_N^2$  is the average transmit SNR. The channels  $h_m$  and  $g_{m}$  in (1) can be rewritten as

$$
h _ {m} = \nu_ {m} \exp (j \phi_ {m}) \quad \text {a n d} \quad g _ {m} = \rho_ {m} \exp (j \omega_ {m}), \tag {5}
$$

where  $\nu_{m} = |h_{m}|$  and  $\rho_{m} = |g_{m}|$  are the channel amplitudes with Rayleigh distribution, while  $\phi_{m}$  and  $\omega_{m}$  are the corresponding channel phases, which are uniformly distributed between  $(- \pi, \pi]$ . By substituting (5) into (4), the SNR in (4) can be expanded as

$$
\Gamma = \bar {\gamma} \left| \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {M} \rho_ {m} \nu_ {m} \eta_ {m} \exp \left(j \left[ \phi_ {m} + \omega_ {m} + \theta_ {m} \right]\right) \right| ^ {2}. \tag {6}
$$

The  $M$  terms inside the summation of (6) must be constructively added to maximize the received SNR. This can be accomplished by intelligently controlling the reflective properties  $(\theta_{m})$  of each element within the IRS. More specifically,  $\Gamma$  in (6) can be maximized by co-phasing each term in its summation. Thus, the optimal choice of  $\theta_{m}$  is given by  $\operatorname{argmax}_{-\pi < \theta_{m} \leq \pi} \{\Gamma\} = \theta_{m}^{*} = -(\phi_{m} + \omega_{m})$  [9], and thereby, the optimal received SNR at  $D$  can be written as

$$
\Gamma^ {*} = \bar {\gamma} \left| \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {M} \rho_ {m} \nu_ {m} \eta_ {m} \right| ^ {2}. \tag {7}
$$

# III. PERFORMANCE ANALYSIS

# A. Statistical characterization of the optimal received SNR

We notice that  $\rho_{m}$  and  $\nu_{m}$  are independently Rayleigh distributed RVs for  $m\in \{1,\dots ,M\}$ . Thus, according to the central limit theorem (CLT),  $Y = \sum_{m = 1}^{M}\rho_{m}\nu_{m}\eta_{m}$  coverages to a Gaussian distribution for a sufficiently large number of passive elements in the IRS [14]. Thereby, the distribution of  $\Gamma^{*}$  in (7) can be tightly approximated by a non-central chi-squared distribution with a single degree-of-freedom [14];  $\Gamma^{*}\approx \gamma \sim \mathcal{X}_{1}^{2}(\nu)$ , where  $\nu$  is the non-centrality parameter. The accuracy of this approximation is verified via the probability density function (PDF) curves in Fig. 2 for different

$M$  and  $\eta_{m}$ . Consequently, the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of  $\gamma$  can be written as (see Appendix A)

$$
F _ {\gamma} (z) = 1 - \psi \mathcal {Q} \left(\left(\sqrt {z / \bar {\gamma}} - \mu_ {Y}\right) / \sigma_ {Y}\right), \tag {8}
$$

where  $z \geq 0$ , and  $\mathcal{Q}(\cdot)$  denotes the Gaussian  $\mathcal{Q}$  function, which is defined as  $\mathcal{Q}(x) = \int_{x}^{\infty} \mathrm{e}^{-t^{2}/2} / \sqrt{2\pi} dt$  [15]. In (8),  $\mu_{Y}, \sigma_{Y}$ , and  $\psi$  are given by

$$
\mu_ {Y} = \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {M} \pi \lambda_ {m} / 2 \quad \text {a n d} \quad \sigma_ {Y} ^ {2} = \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {M} \lambda_ {m} ^ {2} [ 1 6 - \pi^ {2} ] / 4, \tag {9a}
$$

$$
\psi = 1 / \left[ \mathcal {Q} (- \sqrt {\kappa}) \right], \tag {9b}
$$

where  $\lambda_{m}^{2} = \zeta_{h_{m}}\zeta_{g_{m}}\eta_{m}^{2} / 4,\kappa = \mu_{Y}^{2} / \sigma_{Y}^{2}$  and  $\Gamma (t) = \int_0^\infty x^{t - 1}\mathrm{e}^{-x}dx$  is the Gamma function [16, Eqn. (8.310.1)].

# B. Outage Probability

The SNR outage is defined as the probability that the instantaneous SNR  $(\gamma)$  falls below a threshold SNR  $(\gamma_{th})$ . By using (8), a tight approximation for the outage probability in moderately large  $M$  regime can be obtained as

$$
P _ {o u t} = \Pr \left(\Gamma^ {*} \leq \gamma_ {t h}\right) \approx F _ {\gamma} (\gamma_ {t h}), \tag {10}
$$

where  $F_{\gamma}(\cdot)$  is defined in (8).

Remark 1: The PDF of  $\Gamma^{*}$  in (7) for  $M = 1$  is given by  $f_{\Gamma^{*}}(z) = (2\bar{\gamma}\lambda_{1}^{2})^{-1}K_{0}(\sqrt{z / (\bar{\gamma}\lambda_{1}^{2}))}$  [17], and the exact outage probability can be derived by using [16, Eqn. (5.56.2)] as

$$
P _ {o u t} = F _ {\Gamma^ {*}} \left(\gamma_ {t h}\right) = 1 - \sqrt {\gamma_ {t h} / \bar {\gamma} \lambda_ {1} ^ {2}} K _ {1} \left(\sqrt {\gamma_ {t h} / \bar {\gamma} \lambda_ {1} ^ {2}}\right), \tag {11}
$$

where  $K_{v}(\cdot)$  is the  $v$ th order modified Bessel function of the second kind [16, Eqn. 8.407.1].

C. Average achievable rate

The average achievable rate can be defined as

$$
\mathcal {R} = \mathbb {E} \left[ \log_ {2} (1 + \Gamma^ {*}) \right] \approx \mathbb {E} \left[ \log_ {2} (1 + \gamma) \right]. \tag {12}
$$

The exact derivation of the expectation in (12) appears mathematically intractable. Thus, we resort to upper and lower bounds by invoking Jensen's inequality as  $\mathcal{R}_{lb} \lesssim \mathcal{R} \lesssim \mathcal{R}_{ub}$  [18], where  $\mathcal{R}_{lb}$  and  $\mathcal{R}_{ub}$  are defined as

$$
\mathcal {R} _ {l b} = \log_ {2} \left(1 + \left[ \mathbb {E} \left[ 1 / \gamma \right] \right] ^ {- 1}\right), \tag {13}
$$

$$
\mathcal {R} _ {u b} = \log_ {2} (1 + \mathbb {E} [ \gamma ]). \tag {14}
$$

The upper bound in (14) can be derived as (see Appendix B)

$$
\mathcal {R} _ {u b} = \log_ {2} \left(1 + \left(\bar {\gamma} \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {M} \lambda_ {m} ^ {2}\right) \left[ \frac {(1 6 - \pi^ {2}) (1 + \kappa)}{4} \right]\right). \tag {15}
$$

Next, a tight approximation for the lower bound in (13) can be derived as (see Appendix B)

$$
\mathcal {R} _ {l b} \approx \log_ {2} \left(1 + \left(\bar {\gamma} \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {M} \lambda_ {m} ^ {2}\right) \left[ \frac {(1 6 - \pi^ {2}) (\kappa + 1) ^ {3}}{4 (\kappa^ {2} + 6 \kappa + 3)} \right]\right). \tag {16}
$$

# D. Average symbol error rate (SER)

The average SER is defined as the expectation of conditional error probability  $(P_{e|\Gamma^{*}})$  over the distribution of  $\Gamma^{*}$  [15]. For wide range of modulation schemes,  $P_{e|\gamma}$  is given by  $P_{e|\Gamma^{*}} = \alpha \mathcal{Q}(\sqrt{\beta\Gamma^{*}})$ , where  $\alpha$  and  $\beta$  are modulation dependent fixed parameters [15]. In this context, the average SER can be derived as  $\bar{P}_{e} = \mathbb{E}\big[\alpha \mathcal{Q}\big(\sqrt{\beta\Gamma^{*}}\big)\big]$ . By using (8) and by evaluating the expectation, a tight approximation for  $\bar{P}_{e}$  can be derived as (see Appendix C)

$$
\begin{array}{l} \bar {P} _ {e} \approx \mathbb {E} \left[ \alpha \mathcal {Q} \left(\sqrt {\beta \gamma}\right) \right] \\ = \frac {\alpha \psi \exp (- \mu_ {Y} ^ {2} / 2 \sigma_ {Y} ^ {2})}{\sqrt {2} \pi \sigma_ {Y}} \int_ {0} ^ {\pi / 2} \frac {\exp \left(\mu_ {Y} ^ {2} / \left(\frac {2 \beta \bar {\gamma} \sigma_ {Y} ^ {4}}{\sin^ {2} \theta} + 2 \sigma_ {Y} ^ {2}\right)\right)}{\sqrt {\frac {\beta \bar {\gamma}}{2 \sin^ {2} \theta} + \frac {1}{2 \sigma_ {Y} ^ {2}}}} \\ \times \mathcal {Q} \left(\mu_ {Y} / \sqrt {\frac {\beta \bar {\gamma} \sigma_ {Y} ^ {4}}{\sin^ {2} \theta} + \sigma_ {Y} ^ {2}}\right) d \theta . \tag {17} \\ \end{array}
$$

We upper bound (17) by setting  $\theta = \pi /2$  as (see Appendix C)

$$
\begin{array}{l} \bar {P} _ {e} ^ {u b} = \frac {\alpha \psi \exp (- \mu_ {Y} ^ {2} / 2 \sigma_ {Y} ^ {2})}{2 \sigma_ {Y}} \frac {\exp \left(\mu_ {Y} ^ {2} / \left(2 \beta \bar {\gamma} \sigma_ {Y} ^ {4} + 2 \sigma_ {Y} ^ {2}\right)\right)}{\sqrt {\beta \bar {\gamma} + 1 / \sigma_ {Y} ^ {2}}} \\ \times \mathcal {Q} \left(\mu_ {Y} / \sqrt {\beta \bar {\gamma} \sigma_ {Y} ^ {4} + \sigma_ {Y} ^ {2}}\right). \tag {18} \\ \end{array}
$$

Remark 2: The exact average SER for  $M = 1$  can be derived by evaluating  $\bar{P}_e = \mathbb{E}\big[\alpha \mathcal{Q}\left(\sqrt{\beta\Gamma^*}\right)\big]$  using  $F_{\Gamma^{*}}(z)$  and invoking [16, Eqn. (6.614.5)] as follows:

$$
\bar {P} _ {e} = \frac {\alpha}{2} - \frac {\alpha \delta}{2} \exp (\delta) \left(K _ {1} (\delta) - K _ {0} (\delta)\right), \tag {19}
$$

where  $\delta = (4\beta \bar{\gamma}\lambda_1^2)^{-1}$

# E. Achievable diversity order

The diversity order is defined as the negative slope of the outage probability or average SER versus the average SNR curve in a log-log scale as [19]

$$
G _ {d} = \lim  _ {\bar {\gamma} \rightarrow \infty} - \frac {\log \left(P _ {o u t}\right)}{\log (\gamma)} = \lim  _ {\bar {\gamma} \rightarrow \infty} - \frac {\log \left(\bar {P} _ {e}\right)}{\log (\gamma)}, \tag {20}
$$

from which useful information about how the outage probability or average SER decays in high SNR regime can be obtained. Since the outage probability and the average SER have identical diversity orders [19], we first proceed our diversity order derivation by using  $P_{out}$ .

In general, the outage probability can be asymptotically approximated in the high SNR regime as  $P_{out} \approx (O_c\bar{\gamma})^{-G_d}$  where  $G_{d}$  is the diversity order and  $O_{c}$  is a measure of the coding gain [19]. A single-polynomial approximation of  $P_{out}$  in (10) can be derived as (see Appendix D)

$$
P _ {o u t} ^ {\infty} = \Omega_ {o p} \left(\frac {\gamma_ {t h}}{\bar {\gamma}}\right) ^ {G _ {d}} + \mathcal {O} \left(\bar {\gamma} ^ {- (G _ {d} + 1)}\right), \tag {21}
$$

where the diversity order  $G_{d}$  is given by

$$
G _ {d} = M, \tag {22}
$$

where  $M$  is the number of passive reflective elements in the IRS. In (21),  $\Omega_{op} = \xi \prod_{m=1}^{M} (\lambda_m^2(2M)!)^{-1}$ , where the constant  $\xi$  depends on the coding/array gain.

Similarly, an asymptotic approximation for the average SER in high SNR regime can be derived as (see Appendix D)

$$
\bar {P} _ {e} ^ {\infty} = \left(G _ {c} \bar {\gamma}\right) ^ {- M} + \mathcal {O} \left(\bar {\gamma} ^ {- (G _ {d} + 1)}\right), \tag {23}
$$

However, the IRS is able to provide a significant diversity order by virtue of just passive reflectors with reconfigurable phases.

Moreover, the tightness of our lower bound improves with increasing  $M$ . # V. CONCLUSION

The performance of IRS for wireless communication has been investigated by deriving the outage probability, average SER and achievable rate bounds and approximations. The accuracy of these metrics becomes tighter when the number of reflective elements in the IRS grows large. By deriving single-polynomial high SNR approximations of the CDF and PDF of the SNR, the achievable diversity order has been quantified. This high SNR analysis reveals that the achievable diversity order is equal to the number of passive reflective elements. A set of rigorous numerical results is provided to validate our performance analysis and to obtain useful insights about employing IRS for boosting the performance of next-generation wireless systems.

We rewrite  $\gamma = \bar{\gamma} Y^2$ , where  $Y = \sum_{m=1}^{M} X_m$  and  $X_m = \rho_m \nu_m \eta_m$ . Here,  $\rho_m$  and  $\nu_m$  in (7) are two independent Rayleigh distributed RVs with parameters  $\zeta_{h_m} / 2$  and  $\zeta_{g_m} / 2$ , respectively. The  $k$ th moment of  $X_m$  is given by [17]

$$
\bar {x} _ {k, m} = \mathbb {E} \left[ X _ {m} ^ {k} \right] = \left(\zeta_ {h _ {m}} \zeta_ {g _ {m}} \eta_ {m} ^ {2}\right) ^ {k / 2} \left[ \Gamma (k / 2 + 1) \right] ^ {2}. \tag {24}
$$

By invoking CLT, the PDF of  $Y$  can be approximated by a Gaussian distribution in moderately large  $M$  regime;  $Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_Y, \sigma_Y^2)$ , where  $\mu_Y$  and  $\sigma_Y^2$  are given by

$$
\mu_ {Y} = \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {M} \bar {x} _ {1, m} \quad \text {a n d} \quad \sigma_ {Y} ^ {2} = \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {M} \left(\bar {x} _ {2, m} - (\bar {x} _ {1, m}) ^ {2}\right). \tag {25}
$$

The final expressions for  $\mu_{Y}$  and  $\sigma_Y^2$  are given in (9a). Thereby, the PDF of  $Y$  can be written as

$$
f _ {Y} (y) = \psi \exp \left(- \left(y - \mu_ {Y}\right) ^ {2} / 2 \sigma_ {Y} ^ {2}\right) / \sqrt {2 \pi \sigma_ {Y} ^ {2}}, y \geq 0, \tag {26}
$$

where  $f_{Y}(y) = 0$  for  $y < 0$ ,  $\psi$  is a normalization coefficient, which is defined in (9b), and computed using the fact that  $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{Y}(u)du = 1$ . The CDF of  $Y$  is derived as

$$
F _ {Y} (y) = \int_ {- \infty} ^ {y} f _ {Y} (u) d u = 1 - \psi \mathcal {Q} \left(\left(y - \mu_ {Y}\right) / \sigma_ {Y}\right), \tag {27}
$$

for  $y \geq 0$  and  $F_{Y}(y) = 0$  elsewhere. The CDF of  $\gamma = \bar{\gamma} Y^{2}$  can be derived via transformation of RVs as

$$
\begin{array}{l} F _ {\gamma} (z) = \Pr (\gamma \leq z) = \Pr \left(- \sqrt {z / \bar {\gamma}} \leq Y \leq \sqrt {z / \bar {\gamma}}\right) \\ = F _ {Y} \left(\sqrt {z / \bar {\gamma}}\right) - F _ {Y} \left(- \sqrt {z / \bar {\gamma}}\right) \text {f o r} z \geq 0, \tag {28} \\ \end{array}
$$

and  $F_{\gamma}(z) = 0$  otherwise. By substituting (27) to (28), the CDF for  $\gamma$  can be derived as given in (8).

# APPENDIX B

DERIVATION OF  $\mathcal{R}_{ub}$  IN (15) AND  $\mathcal{R}_{lb}$  IN (16)

By using  $\gamma = \bar{\gamma} Y^2$ , (12) can be rewritten as  $\mathcal{R} = \mathbb{E}_Y[\log_2(1 + \bar{\gamma} Y^2)]$ . By noticing that  $\log_2(1 + x^2)$  is a concave function for  $x > 0$ , we invoke the Jensen's inequality [15] to derive an upper bound for  $\mathcal{R}$  as

$$
\mathcal {R} \leq \mathcal {R} _ {u b} = \log_ {2} \left(1 + \tilde {\gamma} \mathbb {E} \left[ Y ^ {2} \right]\right). \tag {29}
$$

where  $\mathbb{E}\big[Y^2\big]$  can be derived as  $\mathbb{E}\big[Y^2\big] = \mu_Y^2 +\sigma_Y^2$  . By substituting  $\mathbb{E}\big[Y^2\big]$  into (29), the desired upper bound for the average achievable rate can be derived as (15).

Next, the derivation of  $\mathcal{R}_{lb}$  in (16) is outlined. To begin with, by applying the Taylor series expansion of  $1 / \gamma$  around  $\mathbb{E}[\gamma]$  [16], the term  $\mathbb{E}[1 / \gamma]$  in (13) can be approximated as [18]

$$
\mathbb {E} \left[ 1 / \gamma \right] \approx 1 / \mathbb {E} [ \gamma ] + \mathbb {V a r} [ \gamma ] / [ \mathbb {E} [ \gamma ] ] ^ {3}. \tag {30}
$$

Since  $\gamma$  follows a non-central chi-squared distribution with one degree-of-freedom, mean and variance are given by [14]

$$
\mathbb {E} [ \gamma ] = \bar {\gamma} \left(\sigma_ {Y} ^ {2} + \mu_ {Y} ^ {2}\right) = \left(\bar {\gamma} \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {M} \lambda_ {m} ^ {2}\right) \left[ \frac {(1 6 - \pi^ {2}) (1 + \kappa)}{4} \right], \tag {31a}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbb {V} \operatorname {a r} [ \gamma ] = 2 \sigma_ {Y} ^ {2} \bar {\gamma} ^ {2} \left(\sigma_ {Y} ^ {2} + 2 \mu_ {Y} ^ {2}\right) \\ = \left(\bar {\gamma} \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {M} \lambda_ {m} ^ {2}\right) ^ {2} \left[ \frac {(1 6 - \pi^ {2}) ^ {2} (1 + 2 \kappa^ {2})}{8} \right]. \tag {31b} \\ \end{array}
$$

By first replacing  $\mathbb{E}[\gamma ]$  and  $\mathbb{Var}[\gamma ]$  in (30) via (31a) and (31b), respectively, and then by substituting the resultant expression into (13),  $\mathcal{R}_{lb}$  can be approximated as shown in (16).

DERIVATION OF  $\bar{P}_e$  IN (17)

By substituting  $\gamma = \bar{\gamma} Y^{2}$  into  $\bar{P}_e\approx \mathbb{E}\left[\alpha \mathcal{Q}\left(\sqrt{\beta\gamma}\right)\right]$ , we have

$$
\bar {P} _ {e} = \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} \alpha \mathcal {Q} \left(x \sqrt {\beta \tilde {\gamma}}\right) f _ {Y} (x) d x, \tag {32}
$$

where  $f_{Y}(y)$  is given in (26). By substituting  $f_{Y}(y)$  and after several mathematical manipulations, (32) can be simplified as

$$
\bar {P} _ {e} = \Delta \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} \mathcal {Q} (\sqrt {a} x) \exp (- (b x ^ {2} - 2 c x)) d x, \tag {33}
$$

where  $\Delta, a, b$ , and  $c$  coefficients are given by

$$
\Delta = \alpha \psi \exp \left(- \mu_ {Y} ^ {2} / 2 \sigma_ {Y} ^ {2}\right) / \sqrt {2 \pi \sigma_ {Y} ^ {2}}, a = \beta \bar {\gamma}, \tag {34}
$$

$$
b = 1 / 2 \sigma_ {Y} ^ {2} \text {a n d} c = \mu_ {Y} / 2 \sigma_ {Y} ^ {2}. \tag {35}
$$

Here,  $\mathcal{Q}(\sqrt{a} x)$ , in (33) can be alternatively written as [16]

$$
\mathcal {Q} (\sqrt {a} x) = \frac {1}{\pi} \int_ {0} ^ {\pi / 2} \exp \left(- \frac {a x ^ {2}}{2 \sin^ {2} \theta}\right) d \theta . \tag {36}
$$

By substituting (36) into (33) and by applying several mathematical manipulations, we have

$$
\mathcal {M} _ {X _ {m}} (s) = \mathbb {E} \left[ \mathrm {e} ^ {- s X _ {m}} \right] = \left((s \lambda_ {m}) ^ {2} - 1\right) ^ {- 1} \left[ s \lambda_ {m} \ln \left(s \lambda_ {m} + \sqrt {\left(s \lambda_ {m}\right) ^ {2} - 1}\right) \left(\sqrt {\left(s \lambda_ {m}\right) ^ {2} - 1}\right) ^ {- 1 / 2} - 1 \right] \tag {40}
$$

$$
\bar {P} _ {e} = \frac {\Delta}{\pi} \int_ {0} ^ {\pi / 2} \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} \exp \left(- \left[ \left(\frac {a}{2 \sin^ {2} \theta} + b\right) x ^ {2} + 2 c x \right]\right) d x d \theta . \tag {37}
$$

By invoking [16, 2.33.1], the inner integral of (37) can be evaluated, and then, (37) reduces to

$$
\bar {P} _ {e} = \frac {\Delta}{\sqrt {\pi}} \int_ {0} ^ {\frac {\pi}{2}} \frac {\exp \left(c ^ {2} / \left(\frac {a}{2 \sin^ {2} \theta} + b\right)\right)}{\sqrt {\frac {a}{2 \sin^ {2} \theta} + b}} \mathcal {Q} \left(- \frac {\sqrt {2} c}{\sqrt {\frac {a}{2 \sin^ {2} \theta} + b}}\right) d \theta . \tag {38}
$$

By substituting  $\Delta$ ,  $a$ ,  $b$ , and  $c$  from (34)-(35),  $\bar{P}_e$  can be written as (17).

# DERIVATION OF DIVERSITY ORDER IN (21)

The PDF of a product of two independent Rayleigh RVs,  $X_{m} = \rho_{m}\nu_{m}\eta_{m}$ , is given by [17]

$$
f _ {X _ {m}} (x) = x / \lambda_ {m} ^ {2} K _ {0} (x / \lambda_ {m}), \quad x \geq 0, \tag {39}
$$

and  $f_{X_m}(x) = 0$  for  $x < 0$ . The moment generating function (MGF) of  $X_m$  can be derived as (40) at the top of this page [16, 6.624.1]. Since  $\{X_1, \dots, X_m, \dots, X_M\}$  are independent RVs, the MGF of  $Y = \sum_{m=1}^{M} X_m$  can be derived as  $\mathcal{M}_Y(s) = \prod_{m=1}^{M} \mathcal{M}_{X_m}(s)$  [14].

The order of smoothness of the PDF of  $\gamma = \bar{\gamma} Y^2$  at the origin can be used to investigate the asymptotic behavior of the outage probability or average SER at the high SNR regime [19]. The corresponding order of smoothness of  $f_{\gamma}(x)$  at the origin can be translated into the decaying order of the pertinent MGF,  $M_{\gamma}(s)$ , which decays as a function of  $s$  [19]. To this end,  $\mathcal{M}_Y(s)$  in (40) can be approximated when  $s\to \infty$  as

$$
\lim  _ {s \rightarrow \infty} \mathcal {M} _ {Y} (s) = \mathcal {M} _ {Y} ^ {\infty} (s) \approx \prod_ {m = 1} ^ {M} \frac {1}{(\lambda_ {m} s) ^ {2}} \left[ \xi (\lambda_ {m} s) ^ {1 / \xi} - \xi^ {\prime} \right], \tag {41}
$$

where  $\xi$  is a large number such that  $\ln (\lambda_m s) \approx \xi (\lambda_m s)^{1 / \xi} - \xi$  and  $\xi' = \ln 2 - \xi$ . The approximation in (41) becomes tight for large  $\xi$  values. Hence, for large values of  $\xi$ , we have

$$
\mathcal {M} _ {Y} ^ {\infty} (s) = \Omega_ {Y} / s ^ {2 M} + \mathcal {O} \left(s ^ {- 2 M - \epsilon}\right), \quad \epsilon > 0, \tag {42}
$$

where  $\Omega_{Y} = \xi \prod_{m=1}^{M} (\lambda_{m}^{2})^{-1}$ . By invoking inverse Laplace transform [16] on (42), the PDF of  $Y$  can be approximated by a single polynomial term for  $y \to 0^{+}$  (i.e.,  $y$  approaches zero from above) as

$$
f _ {Y} ^ {0 ^ {+}} (y) = \Omega_ {Y} / (2 M!) y ^ {2 M - 1} + \mathcal {O} \left(y ^ {2 M - 1 + \epsilon}\right), \tag {43}
$$

for  $\epsilon > 0$ . From (43), the corresponding CDF can be readily derived as  $F_{Y}^{0^{+}}(y) = \int_{0}^{y} f_{Y}^{0^{+}}(t) dt$ . Then, by performing the variable transformation,  $y = \sqrt{z / \bar{\gamma}}$ , a single polynomial approximation of the CDF of  $\gamma$  can be derived as

$$
F _ {\gamma} ^ {0 ^ {+}} (z) = \Omega_ {o p} \left(z / \bar {\gamma}\right) ^ {M} + \mathcal {O} \left(\left(z / \bar {\gamma}\right) ^ {M + 1 + \epsilon}\right), \text {f o r} \epsilon > 0. \tag {44}
$$

where  $\Omega_{op} = \Omega_Y / (2M!)$ . Then, the asymptotic outage probability can be derived as  $P_{out}^{\infty} = F_{\gamma}^{0^{+}}(\gamma_{th})$  as in (21).

Next, the derivation of asymptotic average SER (23) is outlined. An integral for computing the average SER is given

by  $\bar{P}_e = \alpha \sqrt{\beta} /(2\sqrt{2\pi})\int_0^\infty x^{-1 / 2}\exp (-\beta x / 2)F_\gamma (x)dx$  [20]. By substituting (44) into  $\bar{P}_e$  , an asymptotic approximation for the average SER can be derived as

$$
\bar {P} _ {e} ^ {\infty} = \frac {\alpha \Omega_ {o p}}{2 \bar {\gamma} ^ {M}} \sqrt {\frac {\beta}{2 \pi}} \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} x ^ {M - \frac {1}{2}} \exp (- \beta x / 2) d x. \tag {45}
$$

By substituting  $t = \beta x / 2$  into (45), and evaluating the integral via [16, Eqn. (8.310.1)], the asymptotic average SER at high SNR regime can be derived as (23).
