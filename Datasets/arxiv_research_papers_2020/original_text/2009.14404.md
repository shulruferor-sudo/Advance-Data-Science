# Learning to Reflect and to Beamform for Intelligent Reflecting Surface with Implicit Channel Estimation

Tao Jiang, Graduate Student Member, IEEE, Hei Victor Cheng, Member, IEEE, and Wei Yu, Fellow, IEEE

Abstract—Intelligent reflecting surface (IRS), which consists of a large number of tunable reflective elements, is capable of enhancing the wireless propagation environment in a cellular network by intelligently reflecting the electromagnetic waves from the base-station (BS) toward the users. The optimal tuning of the phase shifters at the IRS is, however, a challenging problem, because due to the passive nature of reflective elements, it is difficult to directly measure the channels between the IRS, the BS, and the users. Instead of following the traditional paradigm of first estimating the channels then optimizing the system parameters, this paper advocates a machine learning approach capable of directly optimizing both the beamformers at the BS and the reflective coefficients at the IRS based on a system objective. This is achieved by using a deep neural network to parameterize the mapping from the received pilots (plus any additional information, such as the user locations) to an optimized system configuration, and by adopting a permutation invariant/equivariant graph neural network (GNN) architecture to capture the interactions among the different users in the cellular network. Simulation results show that the proposed implicit channel estimation based approach is generalizable, can be interpreted, and can efficiently learn to maximize a sum-rate or minimum-rate objective from a much fewer number of pilots than the traditional explicit channel estimation based approaches.

Index Terms—Intelligent reflecting surface, channel estimation, beamforming, deep learning, graph neural network.

# I. INTRODUCTION

Conventional physical-layer communication system design has always been based on the paradigm of first modeling the channel, then optimizing the transmitter and the receiver design parameters according to the channel model. While this conventional approach has served the communication engineers well for many practical communication scenarios, the recent emergence of new communication modalities involving passive reflectors for which channel estimation may not be straightforward to perform has motivated the need for new approaches. This paper investigates the design of reflective patterns for intelligent reflecting surfaces (IRS), also known as reconfigurable intelligent surface, composed of a large number of tunable reflective elements. The promise of IRS stems from its ability to manipulate incident electromagnetic waves toward the intended directions by controlling the phase responses of the passive elements [2], [3]. By intelligently adjusting these phases, in effect modifying the signal propagation environment, the IRS can enhance the communication channel between the transmitter and the receiver. In addition, due to its passive structure, the IRS requires very little energy to produce the desired phase shifts for signals reflection. Further, it can be flexibly integrated into various objects (e.g., walls, ceilings), thus enabling a smooth deployment of IRS in existing wireless communication networks [2]. As a result, a wide range of applications for IRS have been explored in the literature, e.g., for improving network coverage [4], boosting wireless spectral efficiency [5], [6], reducing power consumption of data transmission [7]–[9], enhancing over-the-air computation performance [10] and enabling secure wireless communications [11].

This paper tackles the problem of how to optimally tune the IRS elements for capacity enhancement in a multiuser cellular network. A conventional design would have followed the approach of first estimating the channel, then optimizing the design parameters. This is, however, not necessarily the best approach for designing an IRS system, due to the following reasons. First, the passive elements in the IRS have no ability to perform active signal transmission and reception, so the channels to and from an IRS can only be estimated indirectly. Second, an IRS typically has a large number of reflective elements, so the number of channel parameters to estimate can be very large. Third, conventional channel estimation is always with respect to some artificial criterion (such as the mean squared error), which does not necessarily correspond to the ultimate system objective. Finally, even if the channel is perfectly known, the optimization of the beamformers and phase shifts is a high-dimensional nonconvex problem, so finding an optimal solution remains a difficult numerical problem.

The main idea of this paper is that a data-driven approach can be used to overcome some of these difficulties. We show that by adopting a graph neural network (GNN) architecture that models the interaction between the IRS and the multiple users in the system, it is possible to directly learn the mapping from the received pilots to a desired set of beamformers at the base-station (BS) and a desired reflective pattern at the IRS for maximizing a system-wide objective, such as the sum rate or the minimum rate across the multiple users. The GNN structure has the properties that a permutation of the

ordering of the users induces the same permutation on the BS beamformers but keeps the IRS reflective pattern fixed (known as permutation equivariant and permutation invariant properties, respectively). Such an architecture allows the possibility of generalizability to networks with an arbitrary number of users. Numerical results show that the proposed approach produces solutions that can be easily interpreted. Overall, this paper shows that by bypassing the explicit channel estimation phase altogether, a machine learning approach can achieve a significantly higher transmission rate than the conventional channel estimation based approach, especially in the limited pilot length regime.

# A. Related Works

Many of the existing works on the optimization of IRS assume that perfect channel state information (CSI) is available at the BS. Based on the perfect CSI assumption, joint optimization of the IRS reflection and BS beamforming can be carried out for different network objectives, e.g., minimizing the energy consumption [7], maximizing the spectral efficiency [5], or maximizing the minimum rate [12]. In practice, CSI needs to be estimated. Due to the passive nature of the IRS elements, directly estimating the CSI for IRS is not feasible. Instead, CSI estimation needs to be carried out either at the BS or at the users based on the end-to-end reflected signals. In this direction, [13] proposes to solve the channel estimation problem based on a binary reflection method by turning on the IRS elements one at a time, while [14] proposes a channel estimation method based on parallel factor decomposition for estimating the BS-IRS channel and the individual IRS-user channels. However, as the number of elements in an IRS is typically quite large (in order to achieve higher beamforming gain [15]), these channel estimation methods typically require a large pilot overhead. To address this issue, [16] proposes to group the IRS elements into sub-surfaces, but at a cost of reduced beamforming capability. More recently, [17] proposes a compressed sensing based channel estimation method for the multiuser IRS-aided system, which reduces the training overhead significantly but requires the assumption of channel sparsity. Further, [18] proposes to reduce the training overhead by exploiting the common reflective channels among all the users. All these works fall into the paradigm of first estimating the channels from the received pilot signals, then solving the reflection optimization problems based on the estimated channels.

Recently, data-driven approaches have been introduced to address the challenges either in channel estimation or beamforming [19]. For the channel estimation problem, [20] proposes a deep denoising neural network to enhance the performance of the model-based compressive channel estimation for mmWave IRS systems. The authors of [21] propose a convolutional neural network to estimate both direct and cascaded channels from the received pilot signals through end-to-end training. Given perfect channels, the beamforming problem has been investigated from the perspective of the data-driven approach in [22]-[24]. In particular, [22] proposes a multi-layer fully connected neural network to learn the phase

shifts for a single-user system to reduce the algorithm runtime complexity of optimizing the IRS. Furthermore, deep reinforcement learning is leveraged to optimize the phase shifts for the single-user system in [23], and for the multiuser case in [24].

This paper proposes to use a data-driven approach for optimizing the IRS. The proposed approach is motivated by the success of using deep learning to optimize wireless communication systems without explicitly estimating the channel [25]–[27]. In particular, [25] shows that the beamforming vectors learned from the received pilot signals can approach the achievable rate of the optimal beamforming vectors for highly-mobile mmWave systems. Further, [26] shows that based on the geographical locations of the users, the deep learning approach is able to learn the optimal scheduling without channel estimation. Location information is also utilized in [27] to configure the IRS for indoor signal focusing using a deep learning approach. In this paper, we primarily use the received pilots as the input to the neural network, because the received pilot signal contains rich information about both the large-scale and small-scale fading, but we also show that incorporating the location information can further reduce the required pilot length.

# B. Main Contributions

This paper casts the problem of designing the beamforming and reflective patterns in an IRS system as a variational optimization problem whose optimization variables are functionals, i.e., mappings from the received pilots to the phase shifts at IRS and beamforming matrix at the BS. We propose to parameterize this mapping using a neural network and to train the neural network based on the training data to directly maximize a network utility function.

While a fully connected neural network has already been shown to be able to significantly reduce pilot length for sum-rate maximization in the conference version of this paper [1], a further contribution of this journal paper is that we propose a GNN architecture to better model the interference among the different users in the network. The proposed GNN is permutation invariant/equivariant across the users and therefore provides better scalability and generalization ability. For example, while a fully connected neural network would not have been able to generalize when the number of users in the network changes (except by re-training a new neural network), a GNN structure can easily have shared parameters across the components for different users, thereby achieving generalizability. It is worth noting that GNNs have been proposed to solve radio resource allocation problems in [28]–[30], but these prior works all require perfect CSI and are not designed for IRS systems.

A key benefit of the data-driven approach for communication system design is that it can easily incorporate different types of data as inputs to the neural network. In this paper, we propose to incorporate the locations of the users, so that the neural network can focus on learning the small-scale fading component of the wireless channels. The numerical results show that this significantly improves the utility maximization

performance. We remark that incorporating such heterogeneous information is not easy to do in the conventional model-based approach.

This paper also shows that the same machine learning framework can be used to design systems under different overall network objectives. In particular, this paper shows the superior performance of the GNN for both the sum-rate maximization and the minimum-rate maximization problems in terms of reducing the length of pilots required to achieve a target performance level.

A crucial requirement for the eventual adoption of the machine learning approach to system-level optimization is the interpretability of its solutions. Toward this end, this paper analyzes the beamforming patterns and the reflective patterns that the GNN learns from training data. We show visually that the deep learning approach is indeed performing the task of focusing the electromagnetic waves toward the directions of the target users, hence providing a level of confidence that the proposed approach is capable of finding an optimized solution in the overall highly nonconvex optimization landscape.

To summarize, the main novelties and the key findings of this paper are as follows:

1) A deep neural network is used to directly map the received pilots to the optimized phase-shifts at the IRS and optimized beamforming matrix at the BS. By bypassing the explicit channel estimation stage, the proposed machine learning framework is able to utilize pilots more efficiently than the conventional channel estimation based approaches.  
2) A GNN architecture is used to map the received pilots to the beamformers at the BS and the reflective pattern at the IRS. The GNN is permutation invariant for the reflective pattern and permutation equivariant for the user beamformers, thereby allowing generalizability to systems with different number of users.  
3) The proposed neural network is able to incorporate heterogeneous inputs such as the location information of the users to further improve the system performance.  
4) The beamforming patterns obtained from the machine learning approach are visualized, showing that the proposed GNN is able to learn the correct beam focusing patterns for both the IRS and the BS.  
5) Numerical simulations show that the proposed deep learning framework outperforms conventional model-based approaches for both the sum-rate maximization and the minimum-rate maximization problems. Further, the proposed GNN generalizes well to different signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs).

We remark that a parallel and independent work [31] has also proposed the idea of using the received pilot signals to learn the configuration of the phase shifts. However, [31] only considers a single-user IRS system. Moreover, their method first uses an alternating optimization approach to find an optimized set of beamforming matrix and phase shifts, then uses a fully connected neural network to learn the optimized solution in a supervised fashion. In comparison, the method proposed in this paper is more direct. Further, our method

# II. PROBLEM FORMULATION

# A. System Model

Consider an IRS assisted downlink multiuser MISO system where  $K$  single-antenna users are served by a BS with  $M$  antennas. An IRS equipped with an array of  $N$  passive reflective elements is deployed to aid the transmission between the BS and users. The BS controls the IRS through an IRS controller, which is capable of adjusting the phases of the array elements in order to reflect the incident signals to desired directions, as shown in Fig. 1.

We use  $\pmb{G} \in \mathbb{C}^{M \times N}$  to denote the uplink channel matrix from the IRS to the BS,  $\pmb{h}_k^{\mathrm{r}} \in \mathbb{C}^N$  to denote the uplink channel vectors from user  $k$  to the IRS, and  $\pmb{h}_k^{\mathrm{d}} \in \mathbb{C}^M$  to denote the uplink channel vectors from user  $k$  to the BS. We assume channel reciprocity, so that the channel matrices in the downlink direction are the transpose of the uplink channels.

Let  $s_k \in \mathbb{C}$  be the symbol to be transmitted from the BS to user  $k$ . The BS uses a beamforming strategy to transmit  $s_k$  through a beamforming vector  $\boldsymbol{w}_k \in \mathbb{C}^M$ , where the beamformers must satisfy a power constraint  $\sum_{k=1}^{K} \| \boldsymbol{w}_k \|_2^2 \leq P_d$ . Let  $\boldsymbol{v} = [e^{j\omega_1}, e^{j\omega_2}, \dots, e^{j\omega_N}]^\top$  be the reflection coefficients at the IRS, where  $\omega_i \in [-\pi, \pi)$  is the phase shift of the  $i$ -th element. Then, the received signal  $r_k$  at the user  $k$  is given by

$$
\begin{array}{l} r _ {k} = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {K} \left(\boldsymbol {h} _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {d}} + \boldsymbol {G} \operatorname {d i a g} (\boldsymbol {v}) \boldsymbol {h} _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {r}}\right) ^ {\top} \boldsymbol {w} _ {j} s _ {j} + n _ {k} (1) \\ = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {K} \left(\boldsymbol {h} _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {d}} + \boldsymbol {A} _ {k} \boldsymbol {v}\right) ^ {\top} \boldsymbol {w} _ {j} s _ {j} + n _ {k}, (2) \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\mathbf{A}_k = \mathbf{G}\mathrm{diag}(\pmb {h}_k^{\mathrm{r}})\in \mathbb{C}^{M\times N}$  is the cascaded channel between the user  $k$  and the BS through reflection at the IRS, and  $n_k\sim \mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma_0^2)$  is the additive white Gaussian noise.

A block-fading model is assumed in which the channel coefficients remain constant during a coherence block, but change independently from block to block. An achievable rate  $R_{k}$  for user  $k$  can be computed as

$$
R _ {k} = \log \left(1 + \frac {\left| \left(\boldsymbol {h} _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {d}} + \boldsymbol {A} _ {k} \boldsymbol {v}\right) ^ {\top} \boldsymbol {w} _ {k} \right| ^ {2}}{\sum_ {i = 1 , i \neq k} ^ {K} \left| \left(\boldsymbol {h} _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {d}} + \boldsymbol {A} _ {k} \boldsymbol {v}\right) ^ {\top} \boldsymbol {w} _ {i} \right| ^ {2} + \sigma_ {0} ^ {2}}\right), \tag {3}
$$

where multiuser interference is treated as noise. The beamforming vectors  $\pmb{w}_k$ 's at the BS and phase shifts  $\pmb{v}$  at the IRS can be jointly optimized to maximize a network utility  $U(R_1 \dots R_K)$ , which is a function of the achievable rates of all the users. Common utility functions include the sum rate  $\sum_{k=1}^{K} R_k$ , and the minimum rate  $\min_k R_k$ .

To optimize the transmit beamformers at the BS and the reflection coefficients at the IRS, the knowledge of the cascaded channel matrix  $A_{k}$  and the channel vector  $h_{k}^{\mathrm{d}}$  for  $k = 1,\dots ,K$  is needed either explicitly or implicitly. Toward this end, a pilot transmission phase is used before the data transmission phase to gain knowledge of  $A_{k}$ 's and  $h_k^{\mathrm{d}}$ 's. By uplink-downlink channel reciprocity, channel estimation can take place in the uplink [13]. Specifically, we use an uplink pilot transmission phase, in which the user  $k$  sends a pilot sequence,  $x_{k}(\ell),\ell = 1,\dots ,L$ , which is reflected through the IRS and received at the BS. The received signal  $y(\ell)$  at the BS in the time slot  $\ell$  can be expressed as

$$
\begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {y} (\ell) = \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \left(\boldsymbol {h} _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {d}} + \boldsymbol {G} \operatorname {d i a g} (\boldsymbol {v} (\ell)) \boldsymbol {h} _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {r}}\right) x _ {k} (\ell) + \boldsymbol {n} (\ell) (4) \\ = \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \left(\boldsymbol {h} _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {d}} + \boldsymbol {A} _ {k} \boldsymbol {v} (\ell)\right) x _ {k} (\ell) + \boldsymbol {n} (\ell), (5) \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\mathrm{diag}(\pmb{v}(\ell))$  is a diagonal matrix with  $\pmb{v}(\ell)$ , the phase shifts of IRS in time slot  $\ell$  of the pilot phase, on its diagonal, and  $\pmb{n}(\ell) \sim \mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma_1^2\mathbf{I})$  is the additive Gaussian noise. Note that there are  $(M + N)K + MN$  unknown channel coefficients in  $\pmb{G}$ ,  $\pmb{h}_k^{\mathrm{d}}$ 's and  $\pmb{h}_k^{\mathrm{r}}$ 's, for  $k = 1, \dots, K$ . Since the number of elements  $N$  in a typical IRS is generally large (possibly in the hundreds), it is challenging to estimate the channels when the pilot length is short.

# B. Problem Formulation

The main idea of this paper is that since the final goal is to optimize the rates  $R_{k}$ 's, instead of explicitly estimating all the channel coefficients as an intermediary step, we can exploit the pilot phase more efficiently by mapping the received pilots directly to the optimized transmission strategy for utility maximization, in effect, bypassing channel estimation.

To this end, we propose to design the optimal beamforming vector  $\pmb{w}_k$ 's and the reflection phase shifts  $\pmb{v}$  based directly on the received pilots  $\pmb{Y} = [\pmb{y}(1), \pmb{y}(2), \dots, \pmb{y}(L)] \in \mathbb{C}^{M \times L}$ . Specifically, given the matrix  $\pmb{Y}$ , our goal is to solve the following optimization problem

$$
\begin{array}{l} \underset {(\boldsymbol {W}, \boldsymbol {v}) = g (\boldsymbol {Y})} {\text {m a x i m i z e}} \quad \mathbb {E} \left[ U \left(R _ {1} (\boldsymbol {v}, \boldsymbol {W}), \dots , R _ {K} (\boldsymbol {v}, \boldsymbol {W})\right) \right] \\ \text {s u b j e c t} \quad \sum_ {k} \| \boldsymbol {w} _ {k} \| ^ {2} \leq P _ {d}, \tag {6} \\ \left| v _ {i} \right| = 1, i = 1, 2, \dots , N, \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\mathbf{W} = [\pmb{w}_1, \dots, \pmb{w}_k]$  is the beamforming matrix at BS,  $g(\cdot)$  is a function that maps the received pilots to the beamforming matrix  $\mathbf{W}$  and the phase shift vector  $\pmb{v}$ , and  $U(\cdot)$  is the network utility function. The expectation here is over the random channel realizations and the noise in the uplink pilot transmission phase. In the rest of the paper, we use the sum-rate maximization and the max-min fairness problem as examples to illustrate our idea, but the proposed method can be extended to other utility functions as well.

Solving problem (6) is computationally challenging, because it is a variational optimization problem with a nonconvex objective function. To tackle this problem, we propose to parameterize the mapping function  $g(\cdot)$  by a deep neural network, and to learn the parameters of the neural network from data. This is motivated by the universal approximation property of the neural networks [32]. Before presenting the proposed approach, we first discuss the uplink pilot transmission stage and the conventional approach of channel estimation followed by network utility maximization for solving the problem (6).

# III. UPLINK PILOT TRANSMISSION AND CONVENTIONAL CHANNEL ESTIMATION

In this section, we describe the conventional approach to solving the problem (6), which consists of an uplink channel estimation phase and a downlink utility maximization phase. Given the estimated channels, the downlink utility maximization problem is well investigated. For instance, the downlink sum-rate maximization problem can be solved using the algorithm proposed in [5], and the minimum rate maximization problem is studied in [12]. Below we focus on the uplink pilot transmission and the channel estimation step.

# A. Uplink Pilot Transmission

We adopt the pilot transmission strategy proposed in [17] to design the uplink pilots and the phase shifts at the IRS in the pilot phase. In particular, the total training slots  $L$  is divided into  $\tau$  sub-frames, each of which consists of  $L_{0} = K$  symbols (i.e.,  $L = \tau L_{0}$ ). The users simultaneously send their

pilot sequences  $\boldsymbol{x}_k^{\mathsf{H}} = [x_k(1), x_k(2), \dots, x_k(L_0)]$  of length  $L_0$  to the BS, repeated over  $\tau$  sub-frames. The pilot sequences of all users are designed to be orthogonal to each other so that they can be decorrelated at the BS, i.e.,  $\boldsymbol{x}_{k_1}^{\mathsf{H}}\boldsymbol{x}_{k_2} = 0$  if  $k_{1}\neq k_{2}$  and  $\boldsymbol{x}_{k_i}^{\mathsf{H}}\boldsymbol{x}_{k_i} = L_0P_u$  where  $P_{u}$  is the uplink pilot transmission power. In the meanwhile, the IRS keeps the phase shifts fixed within each sub-frame, but uses different phase shifts in different sub-frames so that both the users-to-IRS and the IRS-to-BS channels can be measured.

The BS decorrelates the received pilots in each sub-frame by matching the pilot sequence for each user. Let  $\bar{Y}(t) = [y((t - 1)L_0 + 1),\dots ,y(tL_0)]$  denote the received pilots in sub-frame  $t$ . Let  $\bar{v}(t)$  be the phase shifts at the IRS in sub-frame  $t$ . Then,  $\bar{Y}(t)$  can be expressed as:

$$
\bar {\boldsymbol {Y}} (t) = \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \left(\boldsymbol {h} _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {d}} + \boldsymbol {A} _ {k} \bar {\boldsymbol {v}} (t)\right) \boldsymbol {x} _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {H}} + \bar {\boldsymbol {N}} (t), \quad t = 1, \dots , \tau , \tag {7}
$$

where  $\bar{N}(t)$  is a noise matrix with each column independently distributed as  $\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},\sigma_1^2\mathbf{I})$ . By the orthogonality of the pilots, we can form  $\bar{\mathbf{y}}_k(t)\in \mathbb{C}^M$ , i.e., the contribution from user  $k$  at the  $t$ -th sub-frame, as given in [17]:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \bar {\boldsymbol {y}} _ {k} (t) = \frac {1}{L _ {0}} \bar {\boldsymbol {Y}} (t) \boldsymbol {x} _ {k} = \boldsymbol {h} _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {d}} + \boldsymbol {A} _ {k} \bar {\boldsymbol {v}} (t) + \bar {\boldsymbol {n}} (t) (8) \\ \triangleq \boldsymbol {F} _ {k} \boldsymbol {q} (t) + \bar {\boldsymbol {n}} (t), (9) \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\bar{\pmb{n}}(t) = \frac{1}{L_0} \bar{\pmb{N}}(t) \pmb{x}_k$ , and the combined channel matrix is defined as  $\pmb{F}_k \triangleq [\pmb{h}_k^{\mathrm{d}}, \pmb{A}_k]$  and the combined phase shifts is defined as  $\pmb{q}(t) \triangleq [1, \bar{\pmb{v}}(t)^{\top}]^{\top}$ . Recall that we have  $\tau$  subframes in total. Then, by denoting  $\tilde{\pmb{Y}}_k = [\bar{\pmb{y}}_k(1), \dots, \bar{\pmb{y}}_k(\tau)]$  as a matrix of received pilots across  $\tau$  sub-frames, we have

$$
\tilde {\mathbf {Y}} _ {k} = \mathbf {F} _ {k} \mathbf {Q} + \tilde {\mathbf {N}}, \tag {10}
$$

where  $\pmb {Q} = [\pmb {q}(1),\dots ,\pmb {q}(\tau)]$  and  $\tilde{N} = [\bar{n} (1),\dots ,\bar{n} (\tau)]$

The channel estimation problem aims to estimate the combined matrix  $\pmb{F}_k$  for  $k = 1, \dots, K$ . Typically, to ensure that the matrix  $\pmb{Q}$  is full rank so that  $\pmb{F}_k$  can be recovered successfully, we need at least  $\tau = N + 1$ , i.e., a total  $(N + 1)K$  pilot symbols are needed. When  $\tau = N + 1$ , one choice of  $\pmb{Q}$  is a DFT matrix as suggested in [16]. For comparison purposes, we also consider the more general case where  $\tau \neq N + 1$ . In this case,  $\pmb{Q}$  is not a square matrix. One way to construct  $\pmb{Q}$  is to first form a  $d \times d$  DFT matrix  $Q'$  with  $d = \max(\tau, N + 1)$ , then truncate  $Q'$  to the first  $\tau$  columns or the first  $N + 1$  rows. Another possibility is to independently construct vectors  $\bar{\pmb{v}}(t), t = 1, \dots, \tau$ , randomly. Specifically, the phase of  $[\bar{\pmb{v}}(t)]_i$  can be constructed by drawing from a uniform random variable in  $[-\pi, \pi)$ . In this paper, we use the second approach to construct  $\pmb{Q}$  if  $\tau < N + 1$ , and use the first approach in other cases. These are empirically good choices.

# B. Conventional Channel Estimation

To estimate the channel matrix  $\mathbf{F}_k$  from (10), we can use the minimum mean-squared error (MMSE) estimator, obtained by solving the following problem

$$
\underset {f (\cdot)} {\text {m i n i m i z e}} \quad \mathbb {E} \left[ \| f \left(\tilde {\boldsymbol {Y}} _ {k}\right) - \boldsymbol {F} _ {k} \| _ {F} ^ {2} \right]. \tag {11}
$$

The optimal solution to problem (11) is given by

$$
f \left(\tilde {\mathbf {Y}} _ {k}\right) = \mathbb {E} \left[ \boldsymbol {F} _ {k} \mid \tilde {\mathbf {Y}} _ {k} \right]. \tag {12}
$$

However, for general channel fading distributions, the optimal solution is computationally intensive to implement. A low-complexity approach is to constrain the estimator  $f(\cdot)$  to be linear, which results in the linear MMSE (LMMSE) method. When the rows of  $F_{k}$  and the rows of  $\tilde{N}$  are i.i.d., the LMMSE estimator is as follows [33]

$$
\begin{array}{l} \hat {\boldsymbol {F}} _ {k} = \left(\tilde {\boldsymbol {Y}} _ {k} - \mathbb {E} [ \tilde {\boldsymbol {Y}} _ {k} ]\right) \left(\mathbb {E} \left[ (\tilde {\boldsymbol {Y}} _ {k} - \mathbb {E} [ \tilde {\boldsymbol {Y}} _ {k} ]) ^ {\mathsf {H}} (\tilde {\boldsymbol {Y}} _ {k} - \mathbb {E} [ \tilde {\boldsymbol {Y}} _ {k} ]) \right]\right) ^ {- 1} \\ \mathbb {E} \left[ \left(\tilde {\boldsymbol {Y}} _ {k} - \mathbb {E} \left[ \tilde {\boldsymbol {Y}} _ {k} \right]\right) ^ {\mathsf {H}} \left(\boldsymbol {F} _ {k} - \mathbb {E} \left[ \boldsymbol {F} _ {k} \right]\right) \right] + \mathbb {E} \left[ \boldsymbol {F} _ {k} \right]. \tag {13} \\ \end{array}
$$

The estimates of  $h_k^{\mathrm{d}}$ ,  $A_{k}$  can then be obtained from  $\hat{F}_k$ . Note that the LMMSE estimator is an optimal solution to (11) only if the unknown  $F_{k}$  is Gaussian distributed. We note that this LMMSE channel estimation method for multiuser MISO system is also proposed in [12].

# IV. PROPOSED DEEP LEARNING FRAMEWORK

The conventional channel estimation approach aims to solve (11), i.e., to recover entries of  $\pmb{F}_k$  given the received pilots  $\tilde{\pmb{Y}}_k$  using a mean squared error metric. However, recovering the channels is not the goal. Our ultimate objective is to maximize the network utility as in (6). The main idea of this paper is to bypass explicit channel estimation and to solve problem (6) directly.

Specifically, we aim to use a neural network to represent the mapping function  $g(\cdot)$  in problem (6), and to pursue a data-driven approach to train the neural network so that it mimics the optimal mapping from the received pilot signals to the beamformers and the phase shifts for network utility maximization. This overall framework is depicted in Fig. 2(a). In this section, we describe the neural network architecture suited for this task.

# A. Graphical Representation of Users and IRS

A central task in a multiuser cellular network is the management of the interference between the users. Toward this end, the beamformers at the BS and the phase shifts at the IRS must be coordinated so that the mutual interference is minimized. This paper proposes to use a neural network architecture, called GNN, which is based on a graph representation of the beamformers for the users and the phase shifts at IRS, to capture the multiuser interference. The graph consists of  $K + 1$  nodes as shown in Fig. 2(b). The IRS is represented by node 0 and the  $K$  users are represented by nodes 1 to  $K$ . A representation vector, denoted as  $z_{k}$ ,  $k = 0,1,\dots ,K$ , is associated with each node. The goal is to encode all the useful information about each corresponding node in these representation vectors. The representation vectors are updated layer by layer in a GNN, taking into account all the representation vectors in the previous layer as input. After multiple layers, the representation vector of each node would contain sufficient information for designing the beamforming vectors and the reflection coefficients. Specifically, the GNN is trained in such a way so that the phase shifts of the IRS can be

As compared to a fully connected neural network, the GNN more naturally captures the interactions between the users and the IRS. By explicitly embedding these interactions into the neural network architecture, the GNN is better able to learn a mechanism for reducing the interference between users. In particular, the update of each user node is a function of all its neighboring user nodes and the IRS node, which enables the GNN to learn to avoid interference. The update of the IRS node is a function of all the user nodes, which enables the GNN to learn to configure the phase shifts to spatially separate the channels for all the users.

A useful feature of the GNN is that it is able to capture the permutation invariant and permutation equivariant properties [28], [30] of the network utility maximization problem (6). That is, if we permute the index labels of the users in the problem, the neural network should output the same set of beamforming vectors  $\boldsymbol{w}_k$  with permuted indices and the same phase shifts  $\boldsymbol{v}$ . Here, permutation invariance means that the phase shifts  $\boldsymbol{v}$  is independent of the ordering of the user channels, and permutation equivariance means that if the user channels are permuted, the beamforming vectors  $\boldsymbol{w}_k$  would be permuted in the same way. These properties are not easy to learn in a fully connected neural network, but are naturally embedded in the GNN.

By tailoring to the problem structure, the GNN also reduces the model complexity as compared to the fully connected neural network. More importantly, the parameters of the GNN can be tied across the users, so that it can be easily generalized to scenarios with different number of users. This is in contrast to fully connected neural networks, whose parameters need to scale with the number of users, which makes it difficult to generalize.

# B. GNN Architecture

We now describe the proposed GNN architecture and the training process. The overall GNN aims to learn the graph representation vector  $\mathbf{z}_k$  through an initialization layer,  $D$

aggregation and combination layers, and a final layer that includes normalization. It takes the input feature from the user node of the graph as the initial value of the representation vector, denoted as  $z_{k}^{0}$ , then updating them through the  $D$  layers to produce  $z_{k}^{d}$ ,  $d = 1, \dots, D$ , and finally a linear layer to produce  $z_{k}^{D + 1}$ , which is mapped to the beamformer matrix  $W$  and the phase shifts  $v$  via normalization. The overall architecture is shown in Fig. 3.

1) Initialization Layer: The initialization layer takes the input features from the user nodes as the initial representation vector  $\mathbf{z}_k^0$ ,  $k = 1, \dots, K$ , then trains one layer of the neural network to produce  $\mathbf{z}_k^1$ ,  $k = 0, \dots, K$  for the subsequent layers. Note that the IRS node does not have input features, because only the users transmit pilots.

The input features from the user nodes are simply the received pilots, i.e., vectorized form of the matrix  $\mathbf{Y}_k$  with real and imaginary components separated:

$$
\boldsymbol {z} _ {k} ^ {0} = \left[ \operatorname {v e c} \left(\Re \left\{\tilde {\boldsymbol {Y}} _ {k} \right\}\right) ^ {\top}, \operatorname {v e c} \left(\Im \left\{\tilde {\boldsymbol {Y}} _ {k} \right\}\right) ^ {\top} \right] ^ {\top}. \tag {14}
$$

As mentioned earlier, it is easy for the neural network to incorporate additional useful information about each user into the input feature vector  $\boldsymbol{z}_k^0$ . For example, if the locations of the users are available, the input feature vector  $\boldsymbol{z}_k^0$  can be

$$
\boldsymbol {z} _ {k} ^ {0} = \left[ \operatorname {v e c} \left(\Re \left\{\tilde {\boldsymbol {Y}} _ {k} \right\}\right) ^ {\top}, \operatorname {v e c} \left(\Im \left\{\tilde {\boldsymbol {Y}} _ {k} \right\}\right) ^ {\top}, \boldsymbol {l} _ {k} ^ {\top} \right] ^ {\top}, \tag {15}
$$

where  $l_{k}$  is the three-dimensional vector denoting the coordinates of the location of the user  $k$ .

Given the input feature vector  $\mathbf{z}_k^0$ , we use a layer of fully connected neural networks, denoted as  $f_w^0(\cdot)$ , to produce  $\mathbf{z}_k^1$  for the user nodes, i.e.,

$$
\boldsymbol {z} _ {k} ^ {1} = f _ {w} ^ {0} \left(\boldsymbol {z} _ {k} ^ {0}\right), \quad k = 1, \dots , K. \tag {16}
$$

For the IRS node, we take inputs from all the user nodes and process them using a permutation invariant function  $\psi_0(\cdot)$  first, then a fully connected neural network  $f_v^0 (\cdot)$  as

$$
\left. \boldsymbol {z} _ {0} ^ {1} = f _ {v} ^ {0} \left(\psi_ {0} \left(\boldsymbol {z} _ {1} ^ {0}, \dots , \boldsymbol {z} _ {K} ^ {0}\right)\right). \right. \tag {17}
$$

In the implementation, we choose  $\psi_0$  as the element-wise mean function, i.e.,

$$
[ \psi_ {0} \left(\boldsymbol {z} _ {1} ^ {0}, \dots , \boldsymbol {z} _ {K} ^ {0}\right) ] _ {i} = \frac {1}{K} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} [ \boldsymbol {z} _ {k} ^ {0} ] _ {i}. \tag {18}
$$

This is a reasonable choice, because all the pilots are reflected through the IRS, so all the received pilots contain equal amount of information about IRS.

The representation vectors  $(z_0^1, z_1^1, \dots, z_K^1)$  now contain features about the IRS and the user channels, respectively, and are subsequently passed to the  $D$  updating layers of the GNN in order to eventually produce the IRS reflective coefficients  $\mathbf{v}$  from  $z_0^D$  and the user beamformers  $\mathbf{w}_k$  from  $z_k^D$ .

2) Updating Layers: The update of the representation vector  $z_{k}$  in the  $d$ -th layer is based on combining its previous

representation and the aggregation of the representations from its neighboring nodes. In a general GNN, this is given by [34]

$$
\left. \boldsymbol {z} _ {k} ^ {d} = f _ {\text {c o m b i n e}} ^ {d} \left(\boldsymbol {z} _ {k} ^ {d - 1}, f _ {\text {a g g r e g a t e}} ^ {d} \left(\left\{\boldsymbol {z} _ {j} ^ {d - 1} \right\} _ {j \in \mathcal {N} (k)}\right)\right), \right. \tag {19}
$$

where  $\mathcal{N}(k)$  denotes the set of neighboring nodes of the node  $k$ ,  $f_{\mathrm{aggregate}}^{d}(\cdot)$  and  $f_{\mathrm{combine}}^{d}(\cdot)$  are the aggregation function and combining function of the layer  $d$ , respectively.

A key in designing the GNN is to choose a suitable aggregation function  $f_{\mathrm{aggregate}}^{d}(\cdot)$  and combining function  $f_{\mathrm{combine}}^{d}(\cdot)$  in (19) so that the GNN is scalable and generalizes well. An efficient implementation of  $f_{\mathrm{aggregate}}^{d}(\cdot)$  takes the following form [30], [35]:

$$
f _ {\text {a g g r e g a t e}} ^ {d} \left(\left\{\boldsymbol {z} _ {j} ^ {d - 1} \right\} _ {j \in \mathcal {N} (k)}\right) = \psi \left(\left\{f _ {\mathrm {n n}} ^ {d} \left(\boldsymbol {z} _ {j} ^ {d - 1}\right) \right\} _ {j \in \mathcal {N} (k)}\right), \tag {20}
$$

where  $\psi (\cdot)$  is a function that is invariant to the permutation of the inputs, e.g., element-wise max-pooling or element-wise

mean pooling, and  $f_{\mathrm{nn}}^d(\cdot)$  is a fully connected neural network. In addition, the combining function  $f_{\mathrm{combine}}^d(\cdot)$  in (19) can also be implemented by a fully connected neural network for complicated optimization problems [30], [35].

We adopt this framework to design the GNN for solving the problem (6). We should note that our problem requires permutation equivariance with respect to the user nodes and permutation invariance with respect to the IRS node, so the aggregation and combination operations for the IRS and the user nodes are designed differently.

For the IRS node, we derive  $z_0^d$ , the node representation vector in the  $d$ -th layer from the previous layer as follows:

$$
\left. \boldsymbol {z} _ {0} ^ {d} = f _ {2} ^ {d} \left(f _ {0} ^ {d} \left(\boldsymbol {z} _ {0} ^ {d - 1}\right), \psi_ {0} \left(f _ {1} ^ {d} \left(\boldsymbol {z} _ {1} ^ {d - 1}\right), \dots , f _ {1} ^ {d} \left(\boldsymbol {z} _ {K} ^ {d - 1}\right)\right)\right), \right. \tag {21}
$$

where  $f_0^d(\cdot)$ ,  $f_1^d(\cdot)$  and  $f_2^d(\cdot)$  are fully connected neural networks, and the aggregation function  $\psi_0(\cdot)$  is chosen to be the element-wise mean function over the users as in (18), which performs well empirically and corresponds to the fact that the IRS reflective pattern needs to serve all users.

For the user nodes, we recognize that aggregation should be with respect to all the other user nodes excluding the IRS node and propose the update equation for the node representation vectors  $\mathbf{z}_k^d$  for  $k = 1,\dots ,K$  in the  $d$ -th layer to take the form

$$
\left. \boldsymbol {z} _ {k} ^ {d} = f _ {4} ^ {d} \left(f _ {0} ^ {d} \left(\boldsymbol {z} _ {0} ^ {d - 1}\right), \boldsymbol {z} _ {k} ^ {d - 1}, \psi_ {1} \left(\left\{f _ {3} ^ {d} \left(\boldsymbol {z} _ {j} ^ {d - 1}\right) \right\} _ {\forall j \neq 0, j \neq k}\right)\right), \right. \tag {22}
$$

where  $f_3^d(\cdot), f_4^d(\cdot)$  are fully connected neural networks and  $\psi_1(\cdot)$  is chosen to be the element-wise max-pooling function,

$$
\left[ \psi_ {1} \left(\boldsymbol {z} _ {1}, \dots , \boldsymbol {z} _ {K}\right) \right] _ {i} = \max  \left(\left[ \boldsymbol {z} _ {1} \right] _ {i}, \dots , \left[ \boldsymbol {z} _ {K} \right] _ {i}\right), \tag {23}
$$

which performs well empirically and corresponds to the fact that multiuser interference is typically dominated by the strongest user.

The update of the node representation vectors in (21) and (22) is shown in Fig. 4(a) and Fig. 4(b), respectively. We remark that there are many different design choices for the aggregation and combination operations. There is no general theory on how to choose these permutation invariant functions. In most works in the literature, the GNN architectures are designed based on empirical trials. In the simulation section of this paper, we shall see that the adopted GNN framework and the choice of the permutation invariant functions can achieve excellent performance.

3) Normalization layer: After  $D$  update layers, the representation vectors produced by the GNN, i.e.,  $\boldsymbol{z}_k^D$ ,  $k = 0,\dots ,K$ , are passed to a normalization layer to produce the reflective coefficients  $\pmb{v}\in \mathbb{C}^{N}$  and the beamforming matrix  $\pmb {W}\in \mathbb{C}^{M\times K}$ , while ensuring that the unit modulus constraints on  $\pmb{v}$  and the total power constraint on  $\pmb{W}$  are satisfied.

To this end, we first take  $z_0^D$  as the input to a linear layer  $f_v^{D + 1}(\cdot)$  with  $2N$  fully connected units as follows:

$$
\boldsymbol {z} _ {0} ^ {D + 1} = f _ {v} ^ {D + 1} \left(\boldsymbol {z} _ {0} ^ {D}\right) \in \mathbb {R} ^ {2 N}. \tag {24}
$$

Then a normalization layer outputs the real and imaginary components of the reflection coefficients  $\pmb{v}$  as follows:

$$
\boldsymbol {Z} _ {v} = \left[ \boldsymbol {z} _ {0} ^ {D + 1} (1: N), \boldsymbol {z} _ {0} ^ {D + 1} (N + 1: 2 N) \right] \in \mathbb {R} ^ {N \times 2}, \tag {25}
$$

$$
v _ {i} = \frac {\left[ \boldsymbol {Z} _ {v} \right] _ {i 1}}{\sqrt {\left[ \boldsymbol {Z} _ {v} \right] _ {i 1} ^ {2} + \left[ \boldsymbol {Z} _ {v} \right] _ {i 2} ^ {2}}} + j \frac {\left[ \boldsymbol {Z} _ {v} \right] _ {i 2}}{\sqrt {\left[ \boldsymbol {Z} _ {v} \right] _ {i 1} ^ {2} + \left[ \boldsymbol {Z} _ {v} \right] _ {i 2} ^ {2}}}, \forall i, \tag {26}
$$

where  $[Z]_{ik}$  denotes the element in the  $i$ -th row and  $k$ -th column of the matrix  $Z$ , and the notation  $z(i_1:i_2)$  denotes the subvector of  $z$  indexed from  $i_1$  to  $i_2$ .

Similarly, to output the beamforming matrix, we first pass  $\mathbf{z}_k^D$  through a linear layer  $f_w^{D + 1}(\cdot)$  with  $2M$  units

$$
\boldsymbol {z} _ {k} ^ {D + 1} = f _ {w} ^ {D + 1} \left(\boldsymbol {z} _ {k} ^ {D}\right) \in \mathbb {R} ^ {2 M}, k = 1, \dots , K, \tag {27}
$$

then use the following normalization steps to produce the beamforming matrix  $\mathbf{W}$ :

$$
\boldsymbol {Z} _ {w} = \left[ \boldsymbol {z} _ {1} ^ {D + 1}, \dots , \boldsymbol {z} _ {K} ^ {D + 1} \right] \in \mathbb {R} ^ {2 M \times K}, \tag {28}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol {Z} _ {w} = \sqrt {P _ {d}} \frac {\boldsymbol {Z} _ {w}}{\| \boldsymbol {Z} _ {w} \| _ {F}}, \tag {29}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol {W} = \boldsymbol {Z} _ {w} (1: M,:) + j \boldsymbol {Z} _ {w} (M + 1: 2 M,:), \tag {30}
$$

where the notation  $Z(i_{1} : i_{2},:)$  denotes the submatrix of  $Z$  constructed by taking the rows of  $Z$  indexed from  $i_{1}$  to  $i_{2}$ .

Note that throughout the GNN architecture, we use the same  $f_{w}^{0}(\cdot), f_{0}^{d}(\cdot), f_{1}^{d}(\cdot), f_{2}^{d}(\cdot), f_{3}^{d}(\cdot), f_{4}^{d}(\cdot)$ , and  $f_{w}^{D + 1}(\cdot)$  to update the node representation vectors for all the user nodes. This allows the GNN to be generalizable to an IRS network with arbitrary number of users. The learned combining and aggregation operations (21) and (22) are independent of the number of users. If we increase or decrease the number of users in the system, we only need to increase or decrease the number of nodes in the graph, the same learned combining and aggregation operations can still be used without having to re-train the neural network.

# C. Neural Network Training

Since the existing deep learning software packages do not support complex-valued operations, to compute the network utility during the training phase, we rewrite the achievable rate  $R_{k}$  as a function of the real and imaginary parts of  $\pmb{w}_{k}$  and  $\pmb{v}$  as follows:

$$
R _ {k} = \log \left(1 + \frac {\left\| \boldsymbol {\gamma} _ {k} \right\| ^ {2}}{\sum_ {i = 1 , i \neq k} ^ {K} \left\| \boldsymbol {\gamma} _ {i} \right\| ^ {2} + \sigma_ {0} ^ {2}}\right), \tag {31}
$$

where

$$
\begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {\gamma} _ {i} = \left[ \begin{array}{c c} \mathfrak {R} \{\boldsymbol {w} _ {i} ^ {\top} \} & - \mathfrak {S} \{\boldsymbol {w} _ {i} ^ {\top} \} \\ \mathfrak {S} \{\boldsymbol {w} _ {i} ^ {\top} \} & \mathfrak {R} \{\boldsymbol {w} _ {i} ^ {\top} \} \end{array} \right] \\ \left. \right. \cdot \left(\left[\begin{array}{l l}{\Re \{\boldsymbol {h} _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {d}} \}}\\{\Im \{\boldsymbol {h} _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {d}} \}}\end{array}\right] + \left[\begin{array}{l l}{\Re \{\boldsymbol {A} _ {k} \}}&{- \Im \{\boldsymbol {A} _ {k} \}}\\{\Im \{\boldsymbol {A} _ {k} \}}&{\Re \{\boldsymbol {A} _ {k} \}}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{l}{\Re \{\boldsymbol {v} \}}\\{\Im \{\boldsymbol {v} \}}\end{array}\right]\right). \tag {32} \\ \end{array}
$$

Given this real representation of  $R_{k}$ , the loss function of the GNN can be expressed as  $-\mathbb{E}\left[U(R_1(\pmb {v},\pmb {W}),\dots ,R_K(\pmb {v},\pmb {W}))\right]$ . # A. Simulation Setting

We consider an IRS assisted multiuser MISO communication system as illustrated in Fig. 5, consisting of a BS with 8 antennas and an IRS with 100 passive elements. As shown in the Fig. 5, the  $(x,y,z)$ -coordinates of the BS and the IRS locations in meters are  $(100,100,0)$  and  $(0,0,0)$ , respectively. There are 3 users uniformly distributed in a rectangular area  $[5,35] \times [-35,35]$  in the  $(x,y)$ -plane with  $z = -20$  as shown in Fig. 5. We assume that the IRS is equipped with a uniform

rectangular array placed on the  $(y,z)$ -plane in a  $10 \times 10$  configuration. The BS antennas have a uniform linear array configuration parallel to the  $x$ -axis.

We assume that the direct channels from the BS to the users follow Rayleigh fading, i.e.,

$$
\boldsymbol {h} _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {d}} = \beta_ {0, k} \tilde {\boldsymbol {h}} _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {d}}, \tag {34}
$$

where  $\tilde{h}_k^{\mathrm{d}}\sim \mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$  and  $\beta_{0,k}$  denotes the path-loss of the direct link between the BS and the user  $k$  modeled (in dB) as  $32.6 + 36.7\log (d_k^{\mathrm{BU}})$ , where  $d_k^{\mathrm{BU}}$  is the distance of the direct link from the BS to the user  $k$ . We assume that the IRS is deployed at a location where a line-of-sight channel exists between the IRS and the users/BS, so we model the channel  $h_k^{\mathrm{r}}$ s between the IRS and the user  $k$  and the channel  $G$  between the BS and the IRS as Rician fading channels:

$$
\boldsymbol {h} _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {r}} = \beta_ {1, k} \left(\sqrt {\frac {\varepsilon}{1 + \varepsilon}} \tilde {\boldsymbol {h}} _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {r}, \mathrm {L O S}} + \sqrt {\frac {1}{1 + \varepsilon}} \tilde {\boldsymbol {h}} _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {r}, \mathrm {N L O S}}\right), \tag {35}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol {G} = \beta_ {2} \left(\sqrt {\frac {\varepsilon}{1 + \varepsilon}} \tilde {\boldsymbol {G}} ^ {\mathrm {L O S}} + \sqrt {\frac {1}{1 + \varepsilon}} \tilde {\boldsymbol {G}} ^ {\mathrm {N L O S}}\right), \tag {36}
$$

where the superscript LOS represents the line-of-sight part of the channel and the superscript NLOS represents non-line-of-sight part,  $\varepsilon$  is the Rician factor which is set to be 10 in simulations, and  $\beta_{1,k},\beta_{2}$  are the path-loss from the IRS to the user  $k$  and the path-loss from the BS to the IRS, respectively. The entries of  $\tilde{G}^{\mathrm{NLOS}}$  and  $\tilde{h}_k^{\mathrm{r,NLOS}}$  are modeled as i.i.d. standard Gaussian distributions, i.e.,  $[\tilde{G}^{\mathrm{NLOS}}]_{ij}\sim \mathcal{CN}(0,1)$  and  $[\tilde{h}_k^{\mathrm{r,NLOS}}]_i\sim \mathcal{CN}(0,1)$ . The path-losses (in dB) of the BS-IRS link and the IRS-user link are modeled as  $30 + 22\log (d^{\mathrm{BI}})$  and  $30 + 22\log (d_k^{\mathrm{IU}})$ , respectively, where  $d^{\mathrm{BI}}$  is the distance between the BS and the IRS, and  $d_k^{\mathrm{IU}}$  is the distance between the IRS and the user  $k$  [5], [7]. The uplink pilot transmit power and the downlink data transmit power are set to be 15dBm and 20dBm, and the uplink noise power is  $-100\mathrm{dBm}$  and the downlink noise power is  $-85\mathrm{dBm}$  unless otherwise stated.

The line-of-sight part of the channel  $\pmb{h}_k^{\mathrm{r}}$  is a function of the IRS/user locations. Specifically, let  $\phi_{3,k}^{*},\theta_{3,k}^{*}$  denote the azimuth and elevation angles of arrival from the user  $k$  to the

IRS, as shown in Fig. 5. Then the  $n$ -th element of the IRS steering vector  $\pmb{a}_{\mathrm{IRS}}(\phi_{3,k}^{*},\theta_{3,k}^{*})$  can be expressed as [36]

$$
\begin{array}{l} [ \boldsymbol {a} _ {\mathrm {I R S}} (\phi_ {3, k} ^ {*}, \theta_ {3, k} ^ {*}) ] _ {n} = \\ e ^ {j \frac {2 \pi d ^ {\mathrm {I R S}}}{\lambda_ {c}} \left\{i _ {1} (n) \sin \left(\phi_ {3, k} ^ {*}\right) \cos \left(\theta_ {3, k} ^ {*}\right) + i _ {2} (n) \sin \left(\theta_ {3, k} ^ {*}\right) \right\}}, \tag {37} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $d^{\mathrm{IRS}}$  is the distance between two adjacent IRS elements, and  $\lambda_{c}$  is the carrier wavelength,  $i_1(n) = \mod (n - 1,10)$ , and  $i_2(n) = \lfloor (n - 1) / 10\rfloor$ . In the simulations, we assume  $\frac{2d^{\mathrm{IRS}}}{\lambda_c} = 1$  without loss of generality. Thus, the line-of-sight channel  $\tilde{\pmb{h}}_k^{\mathrm{r,LOS}}$  can be written as

$$
\tilde {\boldsymbol {h}} _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {r}, \mathrm {L O S}} = \boldsymbol {a} _ {\mathrm {I R S}} \left(\phi_ {3, k} ^ {*}, \theta_ {3, k} ^ {*}\right). \tag {38}
$$

Let  $(x_{k},y_{k},z_{k})$  denote the location of the user  $k$  and  $(x^{\mathrm{IRS}},y^{\mathrm{IRS}},z^{\mathrm{IRS}})$  denote the location of the IRS, we have

$$
\sin \left(\phi_ {3, k} ^ {*}\right) \cos \left(\theta_ {3, k} ^ {*}\right) = \frac {y _ {k} - y ^ {\mathrm {I R S}}}{d _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {I U}}}, \tag {39}
$$

$$
\sin \left(\theta_ {3, k} ^ {*}\right) = \frac {z _ {k} - z ^ {\mathrm {I R S}}}{d _ {k} ^ {\mathrm {I U}}}, \tag {40}
$$

where  $d_k^{\mathrm{IU}}$  is the distance between the IRS and the user  $k$ .

Similarly, let  $\phi_1^*$ ,  $\theta_1^*$  denote the azimuth and elevation angles of arrival (AOA) to the BS, then the steering vector of the BS is given by

$$
\boldsymbol {a} _ {\mathrm {B S}} \left(\phi_ {1} ^ {*}, \theta_ {1} ^ {*}\right) = \left[ 1, \dots , e ^ {j \frac {2 \pi (M - 1) d ^ {\mathrm {B S}}}{\lambda_ {c}} \cos \left(\phi_ {1} ^ {*}\right) \cos \left(\theta_ {1} ^ {*}\right)} \right], \tag {41}
$$

where  $d^{\mathrm{BS}}$  is the distance between two adjacent BS antennas, and we assume  $\frac{2d^{\mathrm{BS}}}{\lambda_c} = 1$ . Let  $\phi_2^*, \theta_2^*$  denote the azimuth and elevation angles of departure (AoD) from the IRS to the BS, then we can write

$$
\tilde {\boldsymbol {G}} ^ {\mathrm {L O S}} = \boldsymbol {a} _ {\mathrm {B S}} \left(\phi_ {1} ^ {*}, \theta_ {1} ^ {*}\right) \boldsymbol {a} _ {\mathrm {I R S}} \left(\phi_ {2} ^ {*}, \theta_ {2} ^ {*}\right) ^ {\mathsf {H}}. \tag {42}
$$

Given the location of the BS  $(x^{\mathrm{BS}},y^{\mathrm{BS}},z^{\mathrm{BS}})$  and the location of the IRS  $(x^{\mathrm{IRS}},y^{\mathrm{IRS}},z^{\mathrm{IRS}})$ , we have

$$
\cos \left(\phi_ {1} ^ {*}\right) \cos \left(\theta_ {1} ^ {*}\right) = \frac {x ^ {\mathrm {I R S}} - x ^ {\mathrm {B S}}}{d ^ {\mathrm {B I}}}, \tag {43}
$$

$$
\sin \left(\phi_ {2} ^ {*}\right) \cos \left(\theta_ {2} ^ {*}\right) = \frac {y ^ {\mathrm {B S}} - y ^ {\mathrm {I R S}}}{d ^ {\mathrm {B I}}}, \tag {44}
$$

$$
\sin \left(\theta_ {2} ^ {*}\right) = \frac {z ^ {\mathrm {B S}} - z ^ {\mathrm {I R S}}}{d ^ {\mathrm {B I}}}, \tag {45}
$$

where  $d^{\mathrm{BI}}$  is the distance between the IRS and the BS.

# VI. PERFORMANCE FOR MAXIMIZING MINIMUM RATE

The previous section shows the benefits of bypassing explicit channel estimation for the sum-rate maximization problem. But the sum-rate objective does not provide fairness across the users, which is typically required in practical wireless communication systems. In this section, we consider the problem of maximizing the minimum user rate, i.e., max-min problem, as formulated below:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \underset {(\boldsymbol {W}, \boldsymbol {v}) = g (\boldsymbol {Y})} {\mathrm {m a x i m i z e}} \qquad \mathbb {E} \left[ \underset {k} {\min } R _ {k} (\boldsymbol {v}, \boldsymbol {W}) \right] \\ \text {s u b j e c t} \quad \sum_ {k} \| \boldsymbol {w} _ {k} \| ^ {2} \leq P _ {d}, \tag {46} \\ \left| v _ {i} \right| = 1, i = 1, 2, \dots , N. \\ \end{array}
$$

For the max-min problem (46), we use the same neural network architecture as in the sum-rate maximization problem, but the loss function is now  $-\mathbb{E}\left[\min_k R_k(\boldsymbol{v}, \boldsymbol{W})\right]$ . We should note that the minimum user rate function is differentiable with respect to  $\boldsymbol{W}$  and  $\boldsymbol{v}$  almost everywhere except at the points when  $R_k = R_j$  for  $k \neq j$ , thus the gradient based back-propagation method can still be applied for training the proposed neural network.

The simulation setting is that of an IRS assisted wireless communication system in which a BS with  $M = 4$  and an IRS with  $N = 20$  serve  $K = 3$  users distributed in the rectangular area of [5,15] × [-15,15] in the  $(x,y)$ -plane with  $z = -20$ .

# VII. INTERPRETATION OF SOLUTIONS FROM GNN

To interpret the solutions obtained by GNN, in this section, we visually verify that the learned IRS indeed reflects the signal in the desired directions. We train the proposed neural network architecture for both a single-user case and a multiuser case, and use the array response as a way to illustrate qualitatively the correctness of the beamforming pattern. Let  $\phi_1, \phi_2, \phi_3$  denote the azimuth angle of arrival from the IRS to the BS, the azimuth angle of departure from the IRS to the BS, and the azimuth angle of arrival from the user to the IRS, respectively. The corresponding elevation angle parameters are denoted by  $\theta_1, \theta_2, \theta_3$ , respectively.

The array response at the IRS is a function of the incident angle and the reflection angle of the wireless signals, which

$$

$$

$$
[ \tilde {\boldsymbol {a}} _ {\mathrm {I R S}} (\phi_ {2}, \theta_ {2}, \phi_ {3}, \theta_ {3}) ] _ {n} =
$$

# VIII. CONCLUSION

Conventional communication system design always involves obtaining accurate CSI first, then designing the optimal transmission scheme according to the CSI. This design strategy is not practical for IRS due to the large number of passive reflective elements involved. This paper proposes an approach that learns to configure the IRS and beamforming at the BS to maximize the system utility function directly based on the received pilots, in effect bypassing explicit channel estimation. This is accomplished by a generalizable graph neural network architecture that unveils the direct mapping from the received

pilots to the desired IRS configuration and the desired per-user beamformers at the BS. Simulation results show that the trained neural network produces interpretable results and can efficiently learn to solve utility maximization problems using much fewer pilots as compared to the conventional approach.
