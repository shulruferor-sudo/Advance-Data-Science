# Abstract

We study the power of deep neural networks (DNNs) with sigmoid activation function. Recently, it was shown that DNNs approximate any  $d$ -dimensional, smooth function on a compact set with a rate of order  $W^{-p / d}$ , where  $W$  is the number of nonzero weights in the network and  $p$  is the smoothness of the function. Unfortunately, these rates only hold for a special class of sparsely connected DNNs. We ask ourselves if we can show the same approximation rate for a simpler and more general class, i.e., DNNs which are only defined by its width and depth. In this article we show that DNNs with fixed depth and a width of order  $M^d$  achieve an approximation rate of  $M^{-2p}$ . As a conclusion we quantitatively characterize the approximation power of DNNs in terms of the overall weights  $W_0$  in the network and show an approximation rate of  $W_0^{-p / d}$ . This more general result finally helps us to understand which network topology guarantees a special target accuracy.

Key words and phrases: deep learning, full connectivity, neural networks, uniform approximation

# 1 Introduction

The outstanding performance of deep neural networks (DNNs) in the application on various tasks like pattern recognition ([11, 17]), speech recognition ([5]) and game intelligence ([19]) have led to an increasing interest in the literature in showing good theoretical properties of these networks. Till now there is still a lack of mathematical understanding of why neural networks with many hidden layers, also known as Deep Learning, are so successful in practice. Recently, there are different research topics to also prove the power of DNNs from a theoretical point of view. One key question we search an answer for is the approximation capacity of DNNs, i.e., we are interested in how multivariate functions can

be approximated by DNNs. Several results already contributed to this: The analysis of neural networks with one hidden layer resulted in the so-called universal approximation theorem, stating that any continuous function can be approximated arbitrarily well by single-hidden-layer networks provided the number of neurons is large enough (see, e.g., [2, 6]). In case that the regarded function is differentiable, it could also been shown that the networks are able to approximate the first order derivative of the function ([14]). Nevertheless, the number of required neurons per layer have to be very large in some cases. An overview of approximation results by shallow neural networks is given in [16]. [13] could show the same approximation result for deep neural networks as for shallow ones for a class of compositional functions, with the main advantage that in case of deep networks a lower number of training parameters is needed for the same degree of accuracy. [3] showed, that three-layer networks with a small number of parameters are as efficient as really large two-layer networks. [20] stated examples of functions that cannot be efficiently represented by shallow neural networks but by deep ones. General approximation results concerning multilayer neural networks were presented in [4] for continuous functions and in [15] for functions and their derivatives. For smooth activation functions satisfying  $\lim_{x\to -\infty}\sigma (x) = 0$  and  $\lim_{x\to \infty}\sigma (x) = 1$  and some further properties [1] could show, that networks with two hidden layers are able to approximate any smooth function with an approximation error of size  $W^{-p / d}$ , where  $W$  is the number of nonzero weights in the network (see Theorem 2 in [1]). The functions under study are  $(p,C)$ -smooth, i.e., they fulfill the following definition:

Definition 1. Let  $p = q + s$  for some  $q \in \mathbb{N}_0$  and  $0 < s \leq 1$ . A function  $m: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$  is called  $(p, C)$ -smooth, if for every  $\alpha = (\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_d) \in \mathbb{N}_0^d$  with  $\sum_{j=1}^{d} \alpha_j = q$  the partial derivative  $\partial^q m / (\partial x_1^{\alpha_1} \ldots \partial x_d^{\alpha_d})$  exists and satisfies

$$
\left| \frac {\partial^ {q} m}{\partial x _ {1} ^ {\alpha_ {1}} \ldots \partial x _ {d} ^ {\alpha_ {d}}} (\mathbf {x}) - \frac {\partial^ {q} m}{\partial x _ {1} ^ {\alpha_ {1}} \ldots \partial x _ {d} ^ {\alpha_ {d}}} (\mathbf {z}) \right| \leq C \| \mathbf {x} - \mathbf {z} \| ^ {s}
$$

for all  $\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\in \mathbb{R}^d$  , where  $\| \cdot \|$  denotes the Euclidean norm.

A similar result for networks with ReLU activation functions was presented by [18] (see Theorem 5 in [18]). Unfortunately, both results only hold for a special class of sparsely connected DNNs, where the number of nonzero weights  $W$  is much smaller than the number of overall weights  $W_{0}$ . A simpler class of DNNs, so-called fully connected DNNs, was analyzed by [9] and [21]. Those networks are only defined by its width and depth and do not depend on a further sparsity constraint. [9] divided their work in two different cases: 1) DNNs with varying width and logarithmic depth 2) DNNs with varying depth and fixed width. For the first case they derived an approximation rate of  $W_{0}^{-p / d}$  and in their second case they even improved this rate to  $W_{0}^{-2p / d}$ . The second case was also shown in [21]. [12] went one step further and presented a result where both width and depth are varied simultaneously. Beside [1], all the above mentioned results focus on the ReLU activation function. While [1] already shows an approximation error of order  $W^{-p / d}$  for sigmoidal networks, this result only holds for a special class of DNNs. In this article we define our DNNs only by its depth and width and show that for a fixed-depth DNN with

bounded weights and width of order  $M^d$  we can achieve an approximation rate of  $M^{-2p}$ . This in turn generalizes the result of [1], since this also proves an rate of  $W_0^{-p / d}$  in terms of the overall number of weights  $W_{0}$ . The topology of our networks is clearly defined, and can therefore been seen as a guideline of how the network architecture have to be chosen to receive good approximation results for different function classes. In the proofs we generalize the techniques from the proof of the approximation results in [9] to smooth activation function. This enables us to derive the same approximation results as in [1] but with respect to the supremum norm on a cube.

Our class of DNNs is defined as follows: As an activation function  $\sigma : \mathbb{R} \to [0,1]$  we choose the sigmoid activation function

$$
\sigma (x) = \frac {1}{1 + \exp (- x)} \quad (x \in \mathbb {R}). \tag {1}
$$

The network architecture  $(L,\mathbf{k})$  depends on a positive integer  $L$  called the number of hidden layers or depth and a width vector  $\mathbf{k} = (k_{1},\dots,k_{L})\in \mathbb{N}^{L}$  that describes the number of neurons in the first, second, ...,  $L$ -th hidden layer. A feedforward DNN with network architecture  $(L,\mathbf{k})$  and sigmoid activation function  $\sigma$  is a real-valued function defined on  $\mathbb{R}^d$  of the form

$$
f (\mathbf {x}) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {k _ {L}} c _ {1, i} ^ {(L)} f _ {i} ^ {(L)} (x) + c _ {1, 0} ^ {(L)}, \tag {2}
$$

for some  $c_{1,0}^{(L)},\ldots ,c_{1,k_L}^{(L)}\in \mathbb{R}$  and for  $f_{i}^{(L)},$  s recursively defined by

$$
f _ {i} ^ {(s)} (\mathbf {x}) = \sigma \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {k _ {s - 1}} c _ {i, j} ^ {(s - 1)} f _ {j} ^ {(s - 1)} (x) + c _ {i, 0} ^ {(s - 1)}\right) \tag {3}
$$

for some  $c_{i,0}^{(s - 1)},\ldots ,c_{i,k_{s - 1}}^{(s - 1)}\in \mathbb{R},s\in \{2,\dots ,L\}$  and

$$
f _ {i} ^ {(1)} (\mathbf {x}) = \sigma \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {d} c _ {i, j} ^ {(0)} x ^ {(j)} + c _ {i, 0} ^ {(0)}\right) \tag {4}
$$

for some  $c_{i,0}^{(0)},\ldots ,c_{i,d}^{(0)}\in \mathbb{R}$ . The space of DNNs with depth  $L$ , width  $r$  and all coefficients bounded by  $\alpha$  is defined by

$$
\mathcal {F} (L, r, \alpha) = \{f: f \text {i s o f t h e f o r m (2)} \text {w i t h} k _ {1} = k _ {2} = \dots = k _ {L} = r
$$

$$
\left. \left| c _ {i, j} ^ {(\ell)} \right| \leq \alpha \text {f o r a l l} i, j, \ell \right\}. \tag {5}
$$

Here it is easy to see that DNNs of the class  $\mathcal{F}(L,r,\alpha)$  are not restricted by a further sparsity constraint and are only defined by the depth  $L$ , width  $r$  and a bound  $\alpha$  for the weights in the network.

# 1.1 Notation

Throughout the paper, the following notation is used: The sets of natural numbers, natural numbers including 0 and real numbers are denoted by  $\mathbb{N}$ ,  $\mathbb{N}_0$  and  $\mathbb{R}$ , respectively. For  $z\in \mathbb{R}$  we set  $z_{+} = \max \{z,0\}$ . Vectors are denoted by bold letters, e.g.,  $\mathbf{x} = (x^{(1)},\ldots ,x^{(d)})^T$ . We define  $\mathbf{1} = (1,\dots ,1)^T$  and  $\mathbf{0} = (0,\dots ,0)^T$ . A  $d$ -dimensional multi-index is a  $d$ -dimensional vector  $\mathbf{j} = (j^{(1)},\dots ,j^{(d)})^T\in \mathbb{N}_0^d$ . As usual, we define

$$
\| \mathbf {j} \| _ {1} = j ^ {(1)} + \dots + j ^ {(d)}, \quad \mathbf {x} ^ {\mathbf {j}} = (x ^ {(1)}) ^ {j ^ {(1)}} \dots (x ^ {(d)}) ^ {j ^ {(d)}},
$$

$$
\mathbf {j}! = j ^ {(1)}! \dots j ^ {(d)}!, \quad \partial^ {\mathbf {j}} = \frac {\partial^ {j ^ {(1)}}}{\partial (x ^ {(1)}) ^ {j ^ {(1)}}} \dots \frac {\partial^ {j ^ {(d)}}}{\partial (x ^ {(d)}) ^ {j ^ {(d)}}}.
$$

Let  $D \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$  and let  $f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$  be a real-valued function defined on  $\mathbb{R}^d$ . The Euclidean, the supremum and the  $L_1$  norms of  $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$  are denoted by  $\| \mathbf{x}\|$ ,  $\| \mathbf{x}\|_{\infty}$  and  $\| \mathbf{x}\|_1$ , respectively. For  $f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$

$$
\| f\|_{\infty} = \sup_{\mathbf{x}\in \mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(\mathbf{x})|
$$

is its supremum norm, and the supremum norm of  $f$  on a set  $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$  is denoted by

$$
\| f\|_{\infty ,A} = \sup_{\mathbf{x}\in A}|f(\mathbf{x})|.
$$

Furthermore we define  $\| \cdot \|_{C^q (A)}$  of the smooth function space  $C^q (A)$  by

$$
\left\| f \right\| _ {C ^ {q} (A)} := \max  \left\{\left\| \partial^ {\mathbf {j}} f \right\| _ {\infty , A}: \| \mathbf {j} \| _ {1} \leq q, \mathbf {j} \in \mathbb {N} ^ {d} \right\}
$$

for any  $f \in C^q (A)$ .

# 2 Main result

Our main result, concerning the approximation of  $(p,C)$ -smooth functions by DNNs with a fixed depth and a bound for all coefficients of the network, is the following:

Theorem 1. Let  $1 \leq a < \infty$ . Let  $p = q + s$  for some  $q \in \mathbb{N}_0$  and  $s \in (0,1]$ , let  $C \geq 1$ . Let  $f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$  be a  $(p,C)$ -smooth function, which satisfies

$$
\| f \| _ {C ^ {q} ([ - 2 a, 2 a ] ^ {d})} \leq c _ {1} \tag {6}
$$

for some constant  $c_1 > 0$ . Let  $\sigma : \mathbb{R} \to [0,1]$  be the sigmoid activation function  $\sigma(x) = 1 / (1 + \exp(-x))$ . For any  $M \in \mathbb{N}$  sufficiently large (independent of the size of  $a$ , but

$$
M ^ {2 p} \geq 2 c _ {2} \left(\max \left\{a, \| f \| _ {C ^ {q} ([ - a, a ] ^ {d})} \right\}\right) ^ {5 q + 3} a n d M ^ {2 p} \geq \max \left\{c _ {3}, 2 ^ {d}, 1 2 d \right\}
$$

must hold) we set

(i)  $L = 8 + \lceil \log_2(\max \{d,q + 1\})\rceil$  
(ii)  $r = 2^d\left(\max \left\{\left(\binom{d + q}{d} + d\right)M^d(2 + 2d) + d, 4(q + 1)\binom{d + q}{d}\right\} + M^d(2d + 2) + 12d\right)$  
(iii)  $\alpha = c_4\left(\max \left\{a,\| f\|_{C^q ([-a,a]^d)}\right\}\right)^{12}e^{6\times 2^{(d + 1) + 1}ad}M^{10p + 2d + 10}.$

Then a neural network  $f_{net}$  of the network class  $\mathcal{F}(L, r, \alpha)$  exists such that

$$
\| f _ {n e t} - f \| _ {\infty , [ - a, a ] ^ {d}} \leq \frac {c _ {5} \left(\max \left\{a , \| f \| _ {C ^ {q} ([ - a , a ] ^ {d})} \right\}\right) ^ {5 q + 3}}{M ^ {2 p}}
$$

holds for a constant  $c_{5} > 0$

Remark 1. Theorem 1 shows, that there exists a DNN with

$$
W _ {0} = (d + 1) r + (L - 1) r (r + 1) + (r + 1) = c _ {6} M ^ {2 d}
$$

weights that achieve the supremum norm approximation rate  $W^{-p / d} = M^{-2p}$ .

# 3 Approximating different function classes by DNNs

# 3.1 Some computing operations for DNNs

In the following approximation results we often combine subnetworks for simpler tasks to construct networks for more complex tasks, i.e., the combination of networks approximating a multiplication finally leads to a network approximating monomials. To get a better understanding of how our networks are put together, we give a short overview of two different computing operations.

Combined neural network: Let  $f \in \mathcal{F}(L_f, r_f, \alpha_f)$  and  $g \in \mathcal{F}(L_g, r_g, \alpha_g)$  with  $L_f, L_g, r_f, r_g \in \mathbb{N}$  and  $\alpha_f, \alpha_g \in \mathbb{R}$ , then we call  $f \circ g$  the combined network, which is contained in the network class  $\mathcal{F}(L_f + L_g, \max\{r_f, r_g\}, \max\{\alpha_f, 1\} \max\{\alpha_g, 1\})$ . Here we "melt" the output layer of  $g$  with the input layer of  $f$ . This is why we multiply the bound of the weights of both networks in the combined network.

Parallelized neural network: Let  $f \in \mathcal{F}(L, r_f, \alpha_f)$  and  $g \in \mathcal{F}(L, r_g, \alpha_g)$  be two neural networks with the same number of hidden layers  $L \in \mathbb{N}$ . Then we call  $(f, g)$  the parallelized network, which computes  $f$  and  $g$  parallel in a joint network. Thus  $(f, g)$  is a network with depth  $L$ , width  $r_f + r_g$  and a bound of the coefficients of size  $\max\{\alpha_f, \alpha_g\}$ .

# 3.2 Approximation of identity and multiplication

The following lemma presents a neural network that approximates the identity function.

Lemma 1. Let  $\sigma : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$  be a function, let  $R \geq 1$  and  $a > 0$ . Assume that  $\sigma$  is two times continuously differentiable and let  $t_{\sigma, id} \in \mathbb{R}$  be such that  $\sigma'(t_{\sigma, id}) \neq 0$ . Then there exists a neural network  $f_{id} \in \mathcal{F}(1,1,c_7R)$  that satisfies for any  $x \in [-a,a]$ :

| f _ {i d} (x) - x | \leq \frac {\| \sigma^ {\prime \prime} \| _ {\infty}}{2 | \sigma^ {\prime} (t _ {\sigma , i d}) |} \frac {a ^ {2}}{R}.
$$

Proof of Lemma 1. The result follows in a straightforward way from the proof of Theorem 2 in [16], cf., e.g., Lemma 1 in [10].  $\square$

# 3.3 Approximation of multivariate polynomials

Let  $\mathcal{P}_N$  be the linear span of all monomials of the form

$$
\prod_ {k = 1} ^ {d} (x ^ {(k)}) ^ {r _ {k}}
$$

for some  $r_1, \ldots, r_d \in \mathbb{N}_0$ ,  $r_1 + \dots + r_d \leq N$ . Then  $\mathcal{P}_N$  is a linear vector space of functions of dimension

$$
\dim \mathcal {P} _ {N} = \left| \left\{(r _ {1}, \ldots , r _ {d}) \in \mathbb {N} _ {0} ^ {d}: r _ {1} + \dots + r _ {d} \leq N \right\} \right| = \binom {d + N} {d}.
$$

Our next lemma presents a neural network that approximates multivariate polynomials multiplied by an additional factor. This modified form of multivariate polynomials is later needed in the construction of the network for the main result.

Lemma 3. Let  $N \in \mathbb{N}_0$  and let  $p \in \mathcal{P}_N$ . Let  $m_1, \ldots, m_{\binom{d+N}{d}}$  denote all monomials in  $\mathcal{P}_N$ . For  $p \in \mathcal{P}_N$  define  $r_1, \ldots, r_{\binom{d+N}{d}} \in \mathbb{R}$  by

$$
p \left(\mathbf {x}, y _ {1}, \dots , y _ {\binom {d + N} {d}}\right) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {\binom {d + N} {d}} r _ {i} y _ {i} m _ {i} (\mathbf {x}), \quad \mathbf {x} \in [ - a, a ] ^ {d}, y _ {i} \in [ - a, a ] \tag {7}

$$

$$

a neural network  $f_{p}\in \mathcal{F}(L,r,\alpha_{p})$  with  $L = \lceil \log_2(N + 1)\rceil$ ,  $r = 4(N + 1)\binom{d + N}{d}$  and  $\alpha_{p} = 9\max \{\bar{r} (p),1\} R^{4}$  exists such that

$$
\left| f _ {p} \left(\mathbf {x}, y _ {1}, \dots , y _ {\binom {d + N} {d}}\right) - p \left(\mathbf {x}, y _ {1}, \dots , y _ {\binom {d + N} {d}}\right) \right| \leq c _ {9} \bar {r} (p) \frac {a ^ {5 N + 3}}{R}

# 4 Proof of the main result

# 4.1 Idea of the proof of Theorem 1

The proof of Theorem 1 is based on the same idea as described in Theorem 2 a) in [9]. In the proof we will approximate a piecewise Taylor polynomial. To define this piecewise Taylor polynomial, we partition  $[-a,a)^d$  into  $M^d$  and  $M^{2d}$  half-open equivolume cubes of the form

$$
[ \tilde {\mathbf {a}}, \tilde {\mathbf {b}}) = [ \tilde {a} ^ {(1)}, \tilde {b} ^ {(1)}) \times \dots \times [ \tilde {a} ^ {(d)}, \tilde {b} ^ {(d)}), \quad \tilde {\mathbf {a}}, \tilde {\mathbf {b}} \in \mathbb {R} ^ {d},
$$

respectively. Let

$$
\mathcal {P} _ {1} = \left\{C _ {k, 1} \right\} _ {k \in \{1, \dots , M ^ {d} \}} \text {a n d} \mathcal {P} _ {2} = \left\{C _ {j, 2} \right\} _ {j \in \{1, \dots , M ^ {2 d} \}} \tag {9}
$$

be the corresponding partitions. If  $\mathcal{P}$  is a partition of  $[-a, a)^d$  and  $\mathbf{x} \in [-a, a)^d$ , then we denote the cube  $C \in \mathcal{P}$ , which satisfies  $\mathbf{x} \in C$ , by  $C_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{x})$ . If  $C$  is a cube we denote the "bottom left" corner of  $C$  by  $C_{left}$ . Therefore, each cube  $C$  with side length  $s$  (which is half-open as the cubes in  $\mathcal{P}_1$  and  $\mathcal{P}_2$ ) can be written as a polytope defined by

$$
- x ^ {(j)} + C _ {l e f t} ^ {(j)} \leq 0 \mathrm {a n d} x ^ {(j)} - C _ {l e f t} ^ {(j)} - s <   0 (j \in \{1, \ldots , d \}).
$$

Furthermore, we describe by  $C_{\delta}^{0} \subset C$  the cube, which contains all  $\mathbf{x} \in C$  that lie with a distance of at least  $\delta$  to the boundaries of  $C$ , i.e.,  $C_{\delta}^{0}$  is a polytope defined by

$$
- x ^ {(j)} + C _ {l e f t} ^ {(j)} \leq - \delta \mathrm {a n d} x ^ {(j)} - C _ {l e f t} ^ {(j)} - s <   - \delta (j \in \{1, \ldots , d \}).
$$

For each  $i \in \{1, \ldots, M^d\}$  we denote those cubes of  $\mathcal{P}_2$  that are contained in  $C_{i,1}$  by  $\tilde{C}_{1,i}, \ldots, \tilde{C}_{M^{d,i}}$ . Here we order the cubes in such a way that

$$
\left(\tilde {C} _ {k, i}\right) _ {\text {l e f t}} = \left(C _ {i, 1}\right) _ {\text {l e f t}} + \mathbf {v} _ {k}, \tag {10}
$$

holds for all  $k \in \{1, \ldots, M^d\}$ ,  $i \in \{1, \ldots, M^d\}$  and for some vector  $\mathbf{v}_k = (v_k^{(1)}, \ldots, v_k^{(d)})$  with entries in  $\{0, 2a / M^2, \ldots, (M - 1)2a / M^2\}$ . The vector  $\mathbf{v}_k$  describes the position of  $(\tilde{C}_{k,i})_{left}$  relative to  $(C_{i,1})_{left}$ , and we order the above cubes such that this position is independent of  $i$ .

The following lemma helps us to approximate our function by a local Taylor polynomial.

Lemma 5. Let  $p = q + s$  for some  $q \in \mathbb{N}_0$  and  $s \in (0,1]$ , and let  $C > 0$ . Let  $f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$  be a  $(p,C)$ -smooth function, let  $\mathbf{x}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^d$  and let  $T_{f,q,\mathbf{x}_0}$  be the Taylor polynomial of total degree  $q$  around  $\mathbf{x}_0$  defined by

$$
T_{f,q,\mathbf{x}_{0}}(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{\substack{\mathbf{j}\in \mathbb{N}^{d}:||\mathbf{j}||_{1}\leq q}}(\partial^{\mathbf{j}}f)(\mathbf{x}_{0})\frac{(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_{0})^{\mathbf{j}}}{\mathbf{j}!}.
$$

Then for any  $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$

$$
\left| f (\mathbf {x}) - T _ {f, q, \mathbf {x} _ {0}} (\mathbf {x}) \right| \leq c _ {1 1} C \| \mathbf {x} - \mathbf {x} _ {0} \| ^ {p}
$$

holds for a constant  $c_{11} = c_{11}(q, d)$  depending only on  $q$  and  $d$ .

Proof of Lemma 5. See Lemma 1 in [7].

From Lemma 5 we can conclude that the piecewise Taylor polynomial

$$
T _ {f, q, \left(C _ {\mathcal {P} _ {2}} (\mathbf {x})\right) _ {l e f t}} (\mathbf {x}) = \sum_ {k \in \{1, \dots , M ^ {d} \}, i \in \{1, \dots , M ^ {d} \}} T _ {f, q, \left(\tilde {C} _ {k, i}\right) _ {l e f t}} (\mathbf {x}) \mathbb {1} _ {\tilde {C} _ {k, i}} (\mathbf {x}) \tag {11}
$$

satisfies

$$
\| f - T _ {f, q, (C _ {\mathcal {P} _ {2}} (\mathbf {x})) _ {l e f t}} \| _ {\infty , [ - a, a) ^ {d}} \leq \frac {c _ {1 1} (2 a d) ^ {2 p} C}{M ^ {2 p}}.
$$

This means, that a network approximating the above defined piecewise Taylor polynomial with an error of size  $M^{-2p}$  also approximates a  $(p,C)$ -smooth function  $f$  with the same error.

In the following we divide our proof in four key steps:

1. Compute  $T_{f,q,(C_{\mathcal{P}_2}(\mathbf{x}))_{left}}(\mathbf{x})$  by using recursively defined functions.  
2. Approximate the recursive functions by neural networks. The resulting network is a good approximation for  $f(\mathbf{x})$  in case that

$$
\mathbf{x}\in \bigcup_{k\in \{1,\ldots ,M^{2d}\}}(C_{k,2})_{1 / M^{2p + 2}}^{0}.
$$

3. Construct a neural network to approximate  $w_{\mathcal{P}_2}(\mathbf{x})f(\mathbf{x})$  in supremum norm, where

$$
w _ {\mathcal {P} _ {2}} (\mathbf {x}) = \prod_ {j = 1} ^ {d} \left(1 - \frac {M ^ {2}}{a} \left| (C _ {\mathcal {P} _ {2}} (\mathbf {x})) _ {\text {l e f t}} ^ {(j)} + \frac {a}{M ^ {2}} - x ^ {(j)} \right|\right) _ {+} \tag {12}
$$

is a linear tensorproduct B-spline which takes its maximum value at the center of  $C_{\mathcal{P}_2}(\mathbf{x})$ , which is nonzero in the inner part of  $C_{\mathcal{P}_2}(\mathbf{x})$  and which vanishes outside of  $C_{\mathcal{P}_2}(\mathbf{x})$ .

4. Apply those networks to  $2^{d}$  slightly shifted partitions of  $\mathcal{P}_2$  to approximate  $f(\mathbf{x})$  in supremum norm.

# 4.2 Key step 1: A recursive definition of  $T_{f,q,(C_{\mathcal{P}_2}(\mathbf{x}))_{left}}(\mathbf{x})$

$$

It is easy to see that  $w_{v}(\mathbf{x})$  is a linear tensorproduct B-spline which takes its maximum value at the center of  $C_{\mathcal{P}_{2,v}}(\mathbf{x})$ , which is nonzero in the inner part of  $C_{\mathcal{P}_{2,v}}(\mathbf{x})$  and which vanishes outside of  $C_{\mathcal{P}_{2,v}}(\mathbf{x})$ . Consequently we have  $w_{1}(\mathbf{x}) + \dots + w_{2^{d}}(\mathbf{x}) = 1$  for  $\mathbf{x} \in [-a/2, a/2]^{d}$ . Let  $f_{net,1}, \ldots, f_{net,2^{d}}$  be the networks of Lemma 12 corresponding to the partitions  $\mathcal{P}_{1,v}$  and  $\mathcal{P}_{2,v}$  ( $v \in \{1, \ldots, 2^{d}\}$ ), respectively. Since  $[-a/2, a/2]^{d} \subset [-a + a/M^{2}, a + a/M^{2})^{d}$  each  $\mathcal{P}_{1,v}$  and  $\mathcal{P}_{2,v}$  form a partition of a set which contains  $[-a/2, a/2]^{d}$  and the error bounds of Lemma 12 hold for each network  $f_{net,v}$  on  $[-a/2, a/2]^{d}$ . We set

$$
f _ {n e t} (\mathbf {x}) = \sum_ {v = 1} ^ {2 ^ {d}} f _ {n e t, v} (\mathbf {x}).
$$

Since

$$
f (\mathbf {x}) = \sum_ {v = 1} ^ {2 ^ {d}} w _ {v} (\mathbf {x}) f (\mathbf {x})
$$

it follows directly by Lemma 12

$$
\begin{array}{l} | f _ {n e t} (\mathbf {x}) - f (\mathbf {x}) | = \left| \sum_ {v = 1} ^ {2 ^ {d}} f _ {m u l t} \left(f _ {i d} ^ {t _ {1}} \left(f _ {w _ {v}} (\mathbf {x})\right), f _ {i d} ^ {t _ {2}} \left(f _ {n e t, \mathcal {P} _ {2, v}, t r u e} (\mathbf {x})\right)\right) - \sum_ {v = 1} ^ {2 ^ {d}} w _ {v} (\mathbf {x}) f (\mathbf {x}) \right| \\ \leq \sum_ {v = 1} ^ {2 ^ {d}} \left| f _ {m u l t} \left(f _ {i d} ^ {t _ {1}} \left(f _ {w _ {v}} (\mathbf {x})\right), f _ {i d} ^ {t _ {2}} \left(f _ {n e t, \mathcal {P} _ {2, v}, t r u e} (\mathbf {x})\right)\right) - w _ {v} (\mathbf {x}) f (\mathbf {x}) \right| \\ \leq \frac {c _ {2 8} \left(\operatorname* {m a x} \left\{a , \| f \| _ {C ^ {q} ([ - a , a ] ^ {d})} \right\}\right) ^ {5 q + 3}}{M ^ {2 p}}, \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $t_1$  and  $t_2$  are chosen as in the proof of Lemma 12.
