# SISO RIS-Enabled Joint 3D Downlink Localization and Synchronization

Kamran Keykhosravi*, Musa Furkan Keskin*, Gonzalo Seco-Granados†, and Henk Wymeersch*

* Department of Electrical Engineering, Chalmers University of Technology, Sweden

† Department of Telecommunications and Systems Engineering, Universitat Autonòma de Barcelona, Spain email: kamrank@chalmers.se

Abstract-We consider the problem of joint three-dimensional localization and synchronization for a single-input single-output (SISO) multi-carrier system in the presence of a reconfigurable intelligent surface (RIS), equipped with a uniform planar array. First, we derive the Cramér-Rao bounds (CRBs) on the estimation error of the channel parameters, namely, the angle-of-departure (AOD), composed of azimuth and elevation, from RIS to the user equipment (UE) and times-of-arrival (TOAs) for the path from the base station (BS) to UE and BS-RIS-UE reflection. In order to avoid high-dimensional search over the parameter space, we devise a low-complexity estimation algorithm that performs two 1D searches over the TOAs and one 2D search over the AODs. Simulation results demonstrate that the considered RIS-aided wireless system can provide submeter-level positioning and synchronization accuracy, materializing the positioning capability of Beyond 5G networks even with single-antenna BS and UE. Furthermore, the proposed estimator is shown to attain the CRB at a wide interval of distances between UE and RIS. Finally, we also investigate the scaling of the position error bound with the number of RIS elements.

Index Terms—Reconfigurable intelligent surface, position error bound, Cramér-Rao bound, localization.

# I. INTRODUCTION

Provisioning of radio localization in cellular wireless communication standards was stirred by governmental requirements (Federal Communications Commission and European Commission) on locating the user equipment (UE) by the operators upon receiving an emergency call. Other applications include location-aware communication, geo-advertising, augmented reality, etc [1]. According to 3GPP standards, localization in 4G [2] can be obtained using time-difference-of-arrival measurements between a UE and multiple synchronized base stations (BSs). Since UE is not synchronized to the BSs, at least 4 BSs are needed to solve for the 3D UE position and the clock bias. Hence, cellular localization requires sufficient dense infrastructure and tight inter-BS synchronization. In the context of 5G, it has been shown that mmWave localization with a single BS is possible, provided that UE and BS are equipped with large arrays, and communicate under sufficiently rich multipath conditions [3]. By estimating angles-of-arrival (AOAs) and angles-of-departure (AODs) in addition to delays, localization and synchronization is possible, even when the multipath sources are a priori unknown [4]. Towards 6G, reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RISs) deserve special attention among the technological enablers, since they

can redirect strong signals from a BS towards a UE, thus boosting communication quality among other advantages [5]. In recent years, significant effort has been devoted to study manufacturing, channel models, control, performance gains, and topology design of RIS-aided communication system (see e.g., [6], [7]).

RISs have been considered as one of the main enabling technologies of beyond-5G localization [8], [9] and have been investigated in a number of studies in the localization literature, e.g., [10]-[15]. In [10], [11] localization in the near-field range of a RIS, functioning as a lens, is studied. Cramér Rao bounds (CRBs) have been derived in [12] for a 2D localization in presence of a large intelligent surface equipped with a uniform linear array (ULA). In [13], authors have proposed a joint positioning and beam training algorithm for a wireless system comprising multiple RISs with ULAs. A single-input single-output 2D localization problem with synchronized signaling and multiple RISs (with ULA) has been investigated in [14] by deriving the CRB bounds. In [15], position error bounds (PEBs) have been derived for 3D uplink localization considering both synchronous and asynchronous cases. The authors pointed to the fact that the bounds can be attained by applying a grid search to maximize the log-likelihood function, however, no low-complexity estimation algorithm was presented.

In this paper, we study the 3D downlink localization and synchronization of a UE with a single antenna using the received line-of-sight (LOS) signal from a single-antenna BS and the reflected signal from a RIS with a uniform planar array (UPA). To the best of our knowledge, RIS-aided localization has never been studied in this elemental configuration. The contributions of this paper are:

- We show that both the 3D position and clock bias of the user can be estimated when both the BS and the UE have a single antenna, by the aid of a single RIS, provided that multiple distinct RIS phase profiles are used. As it is known, localization and synchronization would not be possible in the considered scenario without the RIS.  
- We perform the Fisher information matrix (FIM) analysis and calculate the CRB bounds for the user position and channel parameters, showing that the localization is enabled by AOD estimation from the RIS.

# II. SYSTEM AND CHANNEL MODEL

# A. System Setup

We consider a wireless system consisting of a single-antenna BS, a single-antenna UE, and a RIS. In Fig.1a, the system setup is illustrated. The user receives the transmitted signal directly from BS (LOS path) and also from the RIS (reflected path). We assume that the position of the BS and the RIS  $(\pmb{p}_{\mathrm{b}}$  and  $\pmb{p}_{\mathrm{r}}$ , respectively according to some general coordinate system) as well as the rotation matrix corresponding to the RIS coordinate system

$$
\boldsymbol {R} \triangleq [ \boldsymbol {v} _ {1}, \boldsymbol {v} _ {2}, \boldsymbol {v} _ {3} ] ^ {\top} \tag {1}
$$

are known. On the other hand, the UE position  $(\pmb{p})$  is unknown and should be estimated. In addition, we consider an asynchronous scenario, where the user clock bias  $\Delta_{\mathrm{t}}$  should also be estimated. Fig. 1b presents the definition of azimuth and elevation of a general angle in the RIS coordinate system.

We assume that RIS is an  $M_{\mathrm{r}}$  by  $M_{\mathrm{c}}$  UPA with inter-element distance equal to  $d$ . In this case, the element in the  $\ell$ th row  $(\ell \in \{0,\dots ,M_{\mathrm{r}} - 1\})$  and  $m$ th column  $(m\in \{0,\dots ,M_{\mathrm{c}} - 1\})$  has the position  $\pmb{q}_{\ell,m} = [d\ell -d(M_{\mathrm{r}} - 1) / 2),0,dm - d(M_{\mathrm{c}} - 1) / 2]$  in the RIS coordinate system. We assume that the RIS modulates the phase of the incident wave at time  $t$  according to the matrix  $\Gamma_t\in \mathbb{C}^{M_\mathrm{r}\times M_\mathrm{c}}$ , where  $|[T_t]_{\ell ,m}| = 1$  for all  $\ell$  and  $m$ .

# B. Signal Transmission

In this paper, we consider the transmission of  $T$  OFDM symbols with  $N$  subcarriers for one localization occasion. For simplicity, we assume that all the transmitted pilot symbols are equal to  $\sqrt{E_{\mathrm{s}}}$ . Then the received signal can be expressed as the  $N \times T$  matrix

$$
\boldsymbol {Y} = g _ {\mathrm {b}} \sqrt {E _ {\mathrm {s}}} \boldsymbol {d} \left(\tau_ {\mathrm {b}}\right) \mathbf {1} _ {T} ^ {\top} + g _ {\mathrm {r}} \sqrt {E _ {\mathrm {s}}} \boldsymbol {d} \left(\tau_ {\mathrm {r}}\right) \boldsymbol {u} (\phi) ^ {\top} + \boldsymbol {N}, \tag {2}
$$

where  $\mathbf{Y} = [\pmb{y}_0, \dots, \pmb{y}_{T-1}]$ , and  $\pmb{y}_t \in \mathbb{C}^N$  corresponds to the  $t$ th OFDM symbol. The noise matrix  $\pmb{N} \in \mathbb{C}^{N \times T}$  comprises zero-mean circularly-symmetric independent and identically distributed Gaussian elements with variance  $\sigma^2$ . The complex channel gain for the LOS path and the reflected path are indicated by  $g_{\mathrm{b}}$  and  $g_{\mathrm{r}}$ , respectively. The delays  $\tau_{\mathrm{b}} = \| \pmb{p} - \pmb{p}_{\mathrm{b}} \| / c + \Delta_{\mathrm{t}}$  and  $\tau_{\mathrm{r}} = \| \pmb{p} - \pmb{p}_{\mathrm{r}} \| / c + \| \pmb{p}_{\mathrm{b}} - \pmb{p}_{\mathrm{r}} \| / c + \Delta_{\mathrm{t}}$  account for the propagation distance LOS and reflected path delays, respectively, including the clock offset  $\Delta_t$ . The effect of these delays on the signal is captured by the vectors  $\pmb{d}(\tau_{\mathrm{b}})$  and  $\pmb{d}(\tau_{\mathrm{r}})$ , respectively, where we have defined

$$
\boldsymbol {d} (\tau) = \left[ 1, e ^ {- j 2 \pi \tau \Delta f}, \dots , e ^ {- j 2 \pi \tau (N - 1) \Delta f} \right] ^ {\top} \tag {3}
$$

with  $\Delta f$  being the subcarrier spacing.

Furthermore, we have

$$
\begin{array}{l} [ \boldsymbol {u} (\phi) ] _ {t} = (\boldsymbol {a} (\boldsymbol {\theta})) ^ {\top} \operatorname {d i a g} (\boldsymbol {\gamma} _ {t}) \boldsymbol {a} (\boldsymbol {\phi}) \\ = \left(\boldsymbol {\gamma} _ {t} ^ {\top} \odot (\boldsymbol {a} (\boldsymbol {\theta})) ^ {\top}\right) \boldsymbol {a} (\phi) \tag {4} \\ \end{array}
$$

where,  $\pmb{\theta} = [\theta_{\mathrm{az}},\theta_{\mathrm{el}}]^{\top}$  represents the known AOA in azimuth and elevation from the BS to the RIS, and  $\phi = [\phi_{\mathrm{az}},\phi_{\mathrm{el}}]^{\top}$  denotes the AOD from the RIS to the UE, with  $\phi_{\mathrm{az}} = \mathrm{atan2}([s]_2,[s]_1)$  and  $\phi_{\mathrm{el}} = \arccos ([s]_3 / \| p - p_r\|)$ , where  $s = R(p - p_r)$ .

Moreover, we have  $\gamma_{t} = \operatorname{vec}(\Gamma_{t})$  and the response vector of the RIS for the angle  $\psi \in \{\phi, \theta\}$  is defined as  $a(\psi) = a_{\mathrm{r}}(\psi) \otimes a_{\mathrm{c}}(\psi)$  and

$$
\boldsymbol {a} _ {\mathrm {r}} (\psi) = e ^ {j \beta_ {\mathrm {r}}} \left[ 1, e ^ {- j [ \boldsymbol {k} (\psi) ] _ {1} d}, \dots , e ^ {- j [ \boldsymbol {k} (\psi) ] _ {1} (M _ {\mathrm {r}} - 1) d} \right] ^ {\top} \tag {5}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol {a} _ {\mathrm {c}} (\psi) = e ^ {j \beta_ {\mathrm {c}}} [ 1, e ^ {- j [ \boldsymbol {k} (\psi) ] _ {3} d}, \dots , e ^ {- j [ \boldsymbol {k} (\psi) ] _ {3} (M _ {\mathrm {c}} - 1) d} ] ^ {\top} \tag {6}
$$

where  $\beta_{\mathrm{r}} = [\pmb{k}(\pmb{\psi})]_1(M_{\mathrm{r}} - 1)d / 2$  and  $\beta_{\mathrm{c}} = [\pmb{k}(\pmb{\psi})]_3(M_{\mathrm{c}} - 1)d / 2$ . Here,

$$
\boldsymbol {k} (\psi) = - \frac {2 \pi}{\lambda} \left[ \sin \psi_ {\mathrm {e l}} \cos \psi_ {\mathrm {a z}}, \sin \psi_ {\mathrm {e l}} \sin \psi_ {\mathrm {a z}}, \cos \psi_ {\mathrm {e l}} \right] ^ {\top} \tag {7}
$$

is the wavenumber vector and  $\lambda$  is the carrier wavelength. Here, we assume that the wavelength remains relatively constant within the transmission bandwidth.

# C. Goal

Our goal is to estimate the UE location  $\pmb{p}$  and its clock bias  $\Delta_{\mathrm{t}}$  from the observation  $Y$ , given the RIS phase profiles, the transmitted signals, as well as location and orientation of the BS and RIS.

# III. FISHER INFORMATION ANALYSIS

# A. FIM Derivation

In this section, we calculate the PEB, which is a lower bound on the root mean square error (RMSE) of any unbiased estimator of the position upon observing  $\mathbf{Y}$ . The PEB can be calculated as

$$
\mathrm {P E B} = \sqrt {\operatorname {t r} \left(\left[ \boldsymbol {F} _ {\mathrm {p o}} ^ {- 1} \right] _ {1 : 3 , 1 : 3}\right)} \tag {8}
$$

where  $F_{\mathrm{po}}$  is the FIM of positional parameters

$$
\boldsymbol {\zeta} _ {\mathrm {p o}} = \left[ \boldsymbol {p} ^ {\top}, \Delta_ {\mathrm {t}}, g _ {\mathrm {b}, \mathrm {i}}, g _ {\mathrm {b}, \mathrm {r}}, g _ {\mathrm {r}, \mathrm {i}}, g _ {\mathrm {r}, \mathrm {r}} \right] ^ {\top}. \tag {9}
$$

Here, the subindices  $r$  and  $i$  indicate the real and imaginary parts, respectively, of the path gains  $g_{\mathrm{b}}$  and  $g_{r}$ . We can calculate  $F_{\mathrm{po}}$  based on the Fisher information of the channel parameters  $F_{\mathrm{ch}}$  as  $F_{\mathrm{po}} = J^{\top}F_{\mathrm{ch}}J$ . The Jacobian matrix  $J \in \mathbb{R}^{8 \times 8}$  is defined as

$$
\boldsymbol {J} _ {\ell , s} = \frac {\partial [ \boldsymbol {\zeta} _ {\mathrm {c h}} ] _ {\ell}}{\partial [ \boldsymbol {\zeta} _ {\mathrm {p o}} ] _ {s}} \tag {10}
$$

where  $\zeta_{\mathrm{ch}} = [\tau_{\mathrm{b}},\tau_{\mathrm{r}},\phi_{\mathrm{az}},\phi_{\mathrm{el}},g_{\mathrm{b,i}},g_{\mathrm{b,r}},g_{\mathrm{r,i}},g_{\mathrm{r,r}}]^{\top}$  is the set of channel parameters.  $\pmb{F}_{\mathrm{ch}}$  can be written as

$$
\boldsymbol {F} _ {\mathrm {c h}} = \frac {2}{\sigma^ {2}} \sum_ {t = 0} ^ {T - 1} \sum_ {n = 0} ^ {N - 1} \operatorname {R e} \left\{\frac {\partial [ \boldsymbol {E} ] _ {n , t}}{\partial \boldsymbol {\zeta} _ {\mathrm {c h}}} \left(\frac {\partial [ \boldsymbol {E} ] _ {n , t}}{\partial \boldsymbol {\zeta} _ {\mathrm {c h}}}\right) ^ {\mathrm {H}} \right\} \tag {11}
$$

where  $\pmb{E} \in \mathbb{C}^{N \times T}$  is the noiseless part of the received signal in (2). The matrices  $\pmb{F}_{\mathrm{ch}}$  and  $\pmb{J}$  needed to calculate the PEB are derived in Appendix A and Appendix B, respectively.

The CRB bound can be calculated also for  $\Delta_{\mathrm{t}}$  and the channel parameters. As an example, the CRB bound for  $\Delta_{\mathrm{t}}$  can be represented as

$$
\sqrt {\mathbb {E} \left[ \left(\Delta_ {\mathrm {t}} - \tilde {\Delta} _ {t}\right) ^ {2} \right]} \geq \sqrt {\left[ F _ {\mathrm {p o}} ^ {- 1} \right] _ {4 , 4}} \tag {12}
$$

where  $\tilde{\Delta}_t$  indicates the estimate of the true time bias  $\Delta_{\mathrm{t}}$  and  $\mathbb{E}$  represents the expectation over noise.

# B. FIM Interpretation

We observe that the FIM involves a sum of rank-2 matrices over  $T$  transmissions and  $N$  subcarriers. As the dimension of  $\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ch}}$  is  $8 \times 8$ , it immediately follows that  $TN \geq 4$  is a necessary condition for the problem to be identifiable. On the other hand, setting  $N > 1$  is required to be able to estimate any of the delays, while  $T > 1$  is needed to estimate the AODs. From [17], it is known that in order for the AOD to be identifiable, we need that the RIS phase profile vectors  $\gamma_{t}$ 's at different transmission instances are not all parallel.

From a geometric point of view, it can be shown that the four channel geometric parameters (i.e., the two delays and the two AODs for azimuth and elevation) determine the four positional parameters (the 3D localization as the intersection between the AOD line and one-half of a two-sheet hyperboloid, and the clock bias). There is a one-to-one mapping between the channel geometric and the positional parameters, which is equivalent to the fact that the Jacobian  $J$  is an invertible matrix.

# IV. LOW-COMPLEXITY ESTIMATION

In this section, we develop an estimator to determine the channel parameters in  $\zeta_{\mathrm{ch}}$  based on the received signal  $\mathbf{Y}$  and then give an estimation of the UE position and clock bias based on the channel parameters. We first estimate  $\tau_{\mathrm{b}}$  and  $\tau_{\mathrm{r}}$  using IFFT of the received signal and then  $\phi$  based on the 2D-IFFT of the RIS phase profile matrices. At the end of each step, a quasi-Newton algorithm is used to refine the estimation.

# A. Estimation of  $\tau_{\mathrm{b}}$

We estimate  $\tau_{\mathfrak{b}}$  based on the sum of the columns of  $\pmb{Y}$ ,  $\pmb{y}_{\mathrm{c}} = \sum_{t}\pmb{y}_{t}$ , with

$$
\begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {y} _ {\mathrm {c}} = g _ {\mathrm {b}} \sqrt {E _ {\mathrm {s}}} \boldsymbol {d} \left(\tau_ {\mathrm {b}}\right) \mathbf {1} _ {T} ^ {\top} \mathbf {1} _ {T} + g _ {\mathrm {r}} \sqrt {E _ {\mathrm {s}}} \boldsymbol {d} \left(\tau_ {\mathrm {r}}\right) \boldsymbol {u} \left(\phi\right) ^ {\top} \mathbf {1} _ {T} + \boldsymbol {N} \mathbf {1} _ {T}, \\ \approx T g _ {\mathrm {b}} \sqrt {E _ {\mathrm {s}}} \boldsymbol {d} \left(\tau_ {\mathrm {b}}\right) + \sum_ {t} \boldsymbol {n} _ {t} \tag {13} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\boldsymbol{n}_t$  represents the  $t$ th column of matrix  $N$ . This operation adds coherently to the  $T$  transmissions for the LOS path, whereas this does not happen for the signal reflected by the RIS. We assume that  $T$  times the received power from the BS is much greater than the received power from RIS, which is a relevant assumption, especially when both BS and UE are in the far-field of RIS.

Next, we calculate the IFFT of the vector  $\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{c}}$ , that is  $\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{\mathrm{c}} = \mathbf{F}\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{c}}$ , where  $\mathbf{F} \in \mathbb{C}^{N_{\mathrm{F}} \times N}$  is the IFFT matrix with elements

$$
\boldsymbol {F} _ {\ell , m} = \frac {1}{N _ {F}} e ^ {2 j \pi \ell m / N _ {F}} \tag {14}
$$

where  $N_{\mathrm{F}}$  determines the length of the IFFT vector. If

$$
\tau_ {\mathrm {b}} = \ell / \left(N _ {F} \Delta f\right) \tag {15}
$$

for some integer  $\ell$ , the absolute value of the elements of  $\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{\mathrm{c}}$  will likely have a maximum around  $\ell$ . However, with probability one, this assumption does not hold. Therefore, to obtain a more accurate estimation of  $\tau_{\mathrm{b}}$  we induce some artificial delay  $\delta$  by calculating  $\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{c}}(\delta) \triangleq \mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{c}} \odot \mathbf{d}(\delta)$ . First, we set  $\delta = 0$  and calculate  $\tilde{k} = \arg \max_{k} |e_{k}^{\top} \mathbf{F} \mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{c}}(0)|$ , where  $\mathbf{e}_{k}$  is vector comprising all zeros, except with a 1 in the  $k$ th entry. Next, we refine our estimation by calculating

$$
\tilde {\delta} = \arg \max  _ {\delta \in [ 0, 1 / (N _ {F} \Delta f) ]} \left| e _ {k} ^ {\top} F y _ {c} (\delta) \right|. \tag {16}
$$

Then  $\tau_{\mathrm{b}}$  can be estimated as

$$
\tilde {\tau} _ {b} = \tilde {k} / \left(N _ {F} \Delta f\right) - \tilde {\delta}. \tag {17}
$$

Based on our numerical observation, the optimization over  $\delta$  can be performed by a quasi-Newton method using 0 as starting point. We note that the purpose of auxiliary parameter  $\delta$  is to add an artificial delay to the signal such that  $\tau_{\mathrm{b}} + \delta$  satisfies the condition (15), therefore, it is sufficient to constrain the range of  $\delta$  in (16) to  $[0,1 / (N_F\Delta f)]$ .

# B. Estimation of  $\tau_{\mathrm{r}}$

To estimate  $\tau_{\mathrm{r}}$ , we first remove the BS contribution from the received signal  $\mathbf{Y}$  by computing

$$
\begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {Y} _ {\mathrm {r}} = \boldsymbol {Y} - \tilde {g} _ {\mathrm {b}} \boldsymbol {d} \left(\tilde {\tau} _ {\mathrm {b}}\right) \mathbf {1} _ {T} ^ {\top} (18) \\ \approx g _ {\mathrm {r}} \sqrt {E _ {\mathrm {s}}} \boldsymbol {d} \left(\tau_ {\mathrm {r}}\right) \boldsymbol {u} \left(\phi\right) ^ {\top} + \boldsymbol {N}, (19) \\ \end{array}
$$

where

$$
\tilde {g} _ {\mathrm {b}} = \frac {1}{T N} \sum_ {n = 0} ^ {N - 1} \left[ \boldsymbol {y} _ {\mathrm {c}} \odot \boldsymbol {d} \left(- \tilde {\tau} _ {\mathrm {b}}\right) \right] _ {n} \tag {20}
$$

Then, similarly as in Section IV-A, we first perform the optimization

$$
\tilde {k} = \arg \max  _ {k} \left\| e _ {k} ^ {\top} \left(\boldsymbol {F} \boldsymbol {Y} _ {r}\right) \right\| \tag {21}
$$

where  $e_k^\top (F\mathbf{Y}_r)$  extracts the  $k$  -th row from the IFFT of  $\mathbf{Y}_r$ . To refine the estimate, we calculate

$$
\tilde {\delta} = \arg \max  _ {\delta \in [ 0, 1 / \left(N _ {F} \Delta f\right) ]} \left\| \boldsymbol {e} _ {k} ^ {\top} \boldsymbol {F} \left(\boldsymbol {Y} _ {\mathrm {r}} \odot \boldsymbol {d} (\delta) \mathbf {1} _ {T} ^ {\top}\right) \right\|. \tag {22}
$$

Finally similarly as in (17), we have  $\tilde{\tau}_{\mathrm{r}} = \tilde{k} / (N_{F}\Delta f) - \tilde{\delta}$ .

# C. Estimation of  $\phi$

To estimate  $\phi$ , we first remove the delay effects of the reflected path from the signal  $\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{r}}$  by calculating

$$
\mathbf {Y} _ {\phi} = \mathbf {Y} _ {r} \odot d (- \tilde {\tau} _ {r}) \mathbf {1} _ {T} ^ {\top}. \tag {23}
$$

Next, we sum over all the rows of  $\mathbf{Y}_{\phi}$  to obtain  $\pmb{y}_{\phi} \in \mathbb{C}^{T \times 1}$ . One can see from (2) that

$$
\begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {y} _ {\phi} = \boldsymbol {Y} _ {\phi} ^ {\top} \mathbf {1} _ {N} (24) \\ \approx N g _ {\mathrm {r}} \sqrt {E _ {\mathrm {s}}} \boldsymbol {u} (\phi) + \boldsymbol {N} ^ {\top} \mathbf {1} _ {N}. (25) \\ \end{array}
$$

To estimate  $\phi$  based on  $y_{\phi}$ , we use the properties of the Kronecker product and vec operator [18, Eq. (520)], and based on (4), we obtain

$$
\left[ \boldsymbol {u} (\phi) \right] _ {t} = e ^ {- j \left(\beta_ {\mathrm {r}} + \beta_ {\mathrm {c}}\right)} \boldsymbol {a} _ {\mathrm {c}} (\phi) ^ {\top} \left(\boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {t} \odot \boldsymbol {A} (\boldsymbol {\theta})\right) \boldsymbol {a} _ {\mathrm {r}} (\phi) \tag {26}
$$

where  $\mathbf{A}(\pmb {\theta}) = \mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{c}}(\pmb {\theta})\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{r}}(\pmb {\theta})^{\top}$ . Motivated by (26), we search for  $\phi$  through taking 2D IFFT of the known matrices  $\Gamma_t\odot A(\pmb {\theta})$  as

$$
\bar {\Gamma} _ {t} = \boldsymbol {F} _ {\mathrm {r}} \left(\boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {t} \odot \boldsymbol {A} (\boldsymbol {\theta})\right) \boldsymbol {F} _ {\mathrm {c}} ^ {\top}. \tag {27}
$$

The matrices  $\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{r}} \in \mathbb{C}^{N_{\mathrm{Fr}} \times M_{\mathrm{r}}}$  and  $\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{c}} \in \mathbb{C}^{N_{\mathrm{Fc}} \times M_{\mathrm{c}}}$  are defined similarly as in (14), where  $N_{\mathrm{Fr}}$  and  $N_{\mathrm{Fc}}$  determine the dimensions of the 2D IFFT transforms. We note that matrices  $\bar{\Gamma}_t$  do not depend on the received signal and can be calculated offline.

Now, let's assume that for some integers  $0 \leq \ell \leq N_{\mathrm{F_r}} - 1$  and  $0 \leq m \leq N_{\mathrm{F_c}} - 1$ , we have

$$
\left[ \boldsymbol {k} (\phi) \right] _ {1} d \equiv - 2 \pi \ell / N _ {\mathrm {F r}} \quad \mod 2 \pi \tag {28}
$$

$$
[ \boldsymbol {k} (\phi) ] _ {3} d \equiv - 2 \pi m / N _ {\mathrm {F} _ {\mathrm {c}}} \quad \mathrm {m o d} 2 \pi . \tag {29}
$$

Then, by comparing (26) and (27), one can see that for some complex scalar  $\xi$  we have

$$
\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {\ell , m} \triangleq \left[ \left[ \bar {\Gamma} _ {0} \right] _ {\ell , m}, \dots , \left[ \bar {\Gamma} _ {T - 1} \right] _ {\ell , m} \right] ^ {\top} = \xi \boldsymbol {u} (\phi). \tag {30}
$$

w _ {\ell , m} \triangleq \left\| \boldsymbol {y} _ {\phi} - h \left(\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {\ell , m}\right) \boldsymbol {\eta} _ {\ell , m} \right\| ^ {2} \tag {31}
$$

where function  $h(\pmb{\eta}_{\ell,m})$  returns a scalar that minimizes the RHS of (31) for a given vector  $\pmb{\eta}_{\ell,m}$  and is defined as

$$
h (\boldsymbol {v}) \triangleq \frac {\boldsymbol {v} ^ {\mathrm {H}} \boldsymbol {y} _ {\phi}}{\boldsymbol {v} ^ {\mathrm {H}} \boldsymbol {v}}. \tag {32}
$$

Next, we use a quadratic interpolation to obtain continuous (and more accurate) values for  $\tilde{\ell},\tilde{m}$ , i.e., we calculate

$$
\ell_ {\mathrm {q}} = \tilde {\ell} + \frac {w _ {\ell - 1 , m} - w _ {\ell + 1 , m}}{2 \left(w _ {\ell - 1 , m} + w _ {\ell + 1 , m} - 2 w _ {\ell , m}\right)}. \tag {33}
$$

Also, we calculate  $m_{\mathrm{q}}$  similarly to (33). Next, based on  $\ell_{\mathrm{q}}$  and  $m_{\mathrm{q}}$ , we estimate  $[\pmb{k}(\phi)]_1$  and  $[\pmb{k}(\phi)]_3$  using (28)-(29) and calculate an estimation of  $\phi$  using the following relations

$$
[ \boldsymbol {k} (\phi) ] _ {2} = - \sqrt {4 \pi^ {2} / \lambda^ {2} - ([ \boldsymbol {k} (\phi) ] _ {1}) ^ {2} - ([ \boldsymbol {k} (\phi) ] _ {3}) ^ {2}} \tag {34}
$$

$$
\phi_ {\mathrm {a z}} = \operatorname {a t a n 2} \left(- [ \boldsymbol {k} (\phi) ] _ {2}, - [ \boldsymbol {k} (\phi) ] _ {1}\right) \tag {35}
$$

$$
\phi_ {\mathrm {e l}} = \operatorname {a c o s} (- \lambda [ \boldsymbol {k} (\phi) ] _ {3} / 2 \pi). \tag {36}
$$

Finally, we refine our estimation by performing the minimization

$$
\tilde {\phi} = \arg \min  _ {\boldsymbol {\psi}} \left\| \boldsymbol {y} _ {\phi} - h (\boldsymbol {u} (\boldsymbol {\psi})) \boldsymbol {u} (\boldsymbol {\psi}) \right\| \tag {37}
$$

where  $\pmb{u}(\psi)$  is defined in (4). We solve (37) by applying the quasi-Newton algorithm, using (35) and (36) as the initial point.

# D. Estimation of UE position and clock bias

The UE position is calculated as  $\tilde{\pmb{p}} = \pmb{p}_{\mathrm{r}} + \tilde{\kappa}\pmb{R}^{\top}\pmb{k}(\tilde{\phi})$ , where  $\pmb{k}(\tilde{\phi})$  is defined in (7) and

$$
\begin{array}{l} \tilde {\kappa} = \arg \min  _ {\kappa} \left(\| \kappa \boldsymbol {R} ^ {\top} \boldsymbol {k} (\tilde {\phi}) \| + \| \boldsymbol {p} _ {\mathrm {b}} - \boldsymbol {p} _ {\mathrm {r}} \|\right. \\ - \left\| \boldsymbol {p} _ {\mathrm {b}} - \boldsymbol {p} _ {\mathrm {r}} - \kappa \boldsymbol {R} ^ {\top} \boldsymbol {k} (\tilde {\phi}) \right\| - \left(\tilde {\tau} _ {\mathrm {r}} - \tilde {\tau} _ {\mathrm {b}}\right) c ^ {2} \tag {38} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $c$  is the speed of the light. The value of  $\tilde{\kappa}$  can be found numerically via a gradient-descent algorithm. Then we estimate the clock bias as

$$
\tilde {\Delta} _ {t} = \tilde {\tau} _ {\mathrm {b}} - \| \tilde {\boldsymbol {p}} - \boldsymbol {p} _ {\mathrm {b}} \| / c. \tag {39}
$$

# V. SIMULATION RESULTS

In this section, we evaluate the CRB presented in Sec. III and compare them to the RMSE of the estimator proposed in Sec. IV. We study also the effect of the number of RIS elements on PEB. To evaluate the estimator performance, we average over 1000 noise realizations. For each noise realization,  $\Delta_{\mathrm{t}}$  is set to a random number uniformly drawn from the interval  $[0,1 / \Delta f)$ . It is assumed that the RIS and the BS are at the same height, while UE is placed 10 meters below them. The absolute coordinate system is set to be aligned with the RIS coordinates, i.e.,  $R$  is an identity matrix and  $\pmb{p}_{\mathrm{r}} = [0,0,0]$ . All the phase shifts of the RIS elements are drawn from a uniform distribution over  $[0,2\pi)$ , independently. The amplitude of the channel gains  $g_{\mathrm{b}}$  and  $g_{\mathrm{r}}$  are calculated based on Friis' formula (using unit directivity for BS, UE, and RIS elements) and their phase are set randomly between  $[0,2\pi)$ . The rest of the parameters are presented in Table I.

Fig. 2 illustrates the CRB and the estimation errors for the UE position, clock bias, and channel parameters along the direction  $[-r / \sqrt{2}, r / \sqrt{2}, -10]$ . As can be seen, positioning with submeter accuracy is possible for  $r < 30$ . One can see that the proposed estimator is operating in close vicinity of the theoretical bounds. With low values of  $r$ , the UE is positioned almost beneath RIS and a relatively poor angle estimation is obtained. With  $r > 10\mathrm{m}$  the estimation performance deteriorates with  $r$  for all the parameters as the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) decreases. As can be seen, the estimation error of  $\Delta_{\mathrm{t}}$  is close to the PEB bound. This can be explained based on (39) and the fact that the error in  $\tau_{\mathrm{b}}$  is much less than the position error.

# VI. CONCLUSION

We have investigated localization and synchronization in a wireless system with a single-antenna UE, a single-antenna BS, and a RIS. We have calculated the Cramér-Rao bounds and derived a low-complexity estimator to determine the AOD from the RIS, and the delays of the direct and reflected signals. Then based on these parameters, we have estimated the user position and its clock bias. By comparing the estimator's error to the CRBs we have shown that our estimator is efficient and that 3D localization and synchronization is possible for the considered system. Our results point to the potential of RIS in enabling radio localization for simple wireless networks. A future research direction is to investigate the multi-user scenario and to study the effects of employing multi-antenna BS, different RIS phase profiles, and different pilots' energy levels on the localization performance in the considered setup.
