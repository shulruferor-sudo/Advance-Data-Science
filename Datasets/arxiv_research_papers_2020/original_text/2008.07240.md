# Model-Reference Reinforcement Learning for Collision-Free Tracking Control of Autonomous Surface Vehicles

Qingrui Zhang $^{1,2}$ , Wei Pan $^{2}$ , and Vasso Reppa $^{1}$

Abstract—This paper presents a novel model-reference reinforcement learning algorithm for the intelligent tracking control of uncertain autonomous surface vehicles with collision avoidance. The proposed control algorithm combines a conventional control method with reinforcement learning to enhance control accuracy and intelligence. In the proposed control design, a nominal system is considered for the design of a baseline tracking controller using a conventional control approach. The nominal system also defines the desired behaviour of uncertain autonomous surface vehicles in an obstacle-free environment. Thanks to reinforcement learning, the overall tracking controller is capable of compensating for model uncertainties and achieving collision avoidance at the same time in environments with obstacles. In comparison to traditional deep reinforcement learning methods, our proposed learning-based control can provide stability guarantees and better sample efficiency. We demonstrate the performance of the new algorithm using an example of autonomous surface vehicles.

Index Terms—Reinforcement learning, collision avoidance, tracking control, autonomous surface vehicle.

# I. INTRODUCTION

Autonomous surface vehicles (ASVs) have attracted extensive research attention, due to their advantages in many applications, such as environmental monitoring [1], resource exploration [2], enhancing the efficiency and safety of waterborne transportation [3], [4], and many more [5], [6]. Successful launch of ASVs in real life requires avoiding collisions with obstacles [7] and accurate tracking along a desired trajectory [8]. Both collision avoidance and tracking control are the major research topics for ASVs in the maritime engineering [4], [9]–[15]. However, accurate tracking control for ASVs in the presence of obstacles is challenging, as ASVs are subject to uncertain nonlinear hydrodynamics and unknown environmental disturbances [16]. Due to the complexity of the problem, collision avoidance and tracking control are mostly studied in a separate manner.

Collision avoidance methods for ASVs are categorized into path/motion planning approaches [17]–[20] and optimization-based algorithms [14], [21]–[24]. In the path/motion planning approaches, a collision-free reference trajectory or motion is generated based on either off-line or on-line methods, e.g.,  $\mathrm{A}^*$  [17],  $\mathrm{RRT}^*$  [18], [20], and potential field methods [25], etc. It is assumed that the generated collision-free reference trajectory can be tracked with high accuracy by the ASV based on a well-designed control module. Thus, collision avoidance following the path/motion planning approaches may fail for uncertain systems that lack valid tracking controllers. Due

to the two-module design feature, there always exists a time delay for the ASV to apply collision avoidance actions, as the inner-loop controller needs time to react to changes in the reference trajectories. Such a time delay will also downgrade the performance of the path/motion planning approaches in environments with fast-moving obstacles.

The optimization-based algorithms can directly find a control law with collision avoidance by optimizing a certain objective function, e.g. model predictive control (MPC) [14], [22] and reinforcement learning (RL) [23]. They potentially have a better performance than the path/motion planning approaches in dynamic environments. However, collision avoidance algorithms based on MPC suffer from high computational complexity and rely on accurate modeling of ASV systems [14], [22]. They will, therefore, experience dramatic degradation in performances for uncertain ASVs. In comparison to MPC, RL can learn an intelligent collision avoidance law from data samples [26], [27], which can significantly reduce the dependence on modeling efforts and thus make RL very suitable for uncertain ASVs.

Tracking control algorithms for uncertain systems including ASVs mainly lie in four categories: 1) robust control that is the "worst-case" design for bounded uncertainties and disturbances [28]; 2) adaptive control that estimates uncertainty parameters [8], [29], [30]; 3) disturbance observer (DO)-based control that compensates uncertainties and disturbances in terms of the observation technique [31]–[33]; and 4) reinforcement learning (RL) that learns a control law from data samples [13], [34]. In robust control, uncertainties and disturbances are assumed to be bounded with known boundaries [35]. As a consequence, robust control will lead to conservative high-gain control laws that might degrade the control performances (i.e., overshoot, settling time, and stability margins) [36]. Adaptive control can handle varying uncertainties with unknown boundaries, but system uncertainties are assumed to be linearly parameterized with known structure and unknown parameters [37]–[39]. DO-based control can adapt to both uncertainties and disturbances with unknown structures [40], [41]. However, the frequency information of uncertainty and disturbance signals are necessary in the DO-based control for the choice of proper control gains, otherwise, it is highly possible to end up with a high-gain control law [41], [42]. In general, comprehensive modeling and analysis of systems are essential for all model-based methods.

In comparison to existing model-based methods, RL is capable of learning a complex tracking control law with collision avoidance from data samples using much less model information [27]. It is, therefore, more promising in controlling systems subject to massive uncertainties and disturbances as ASVs [13], [34] and meanwhile achieving collision avoidance [23], given the sufficiency and good quality of collected data. With the consideration of the merits and limitations of existing RL methods, we propose a novel learning-based control algorithm for uncertain ASVs with collision avoidance by combining a conventional control method with deep RL in this paper. The proposed learning-based control design, therefore, consists of two components: a baseline control law that stabilizes a nominal ASV system and a deep RL control law that compensates for system uncertainties and also achieves intelligent collision avoidance. Such a design structure has several advantages over both conventional model-based methods and pure deep RL methods. First of all, in relation to the "model-free" feature of deep RL, we can learn from data samples a control law that directly compensates for system uncertainties without exploiting their structures, boundaries, or frequencies [46]. Intelligent collision avoidance can also be learned by the deep RL. Second, closed-loop stability is guaranteed by the overall learned control law for the tracking control in obstacle-free environments, if the baseline control law is able to stabilize the ASV system at least locally. Without introducing a restrictive Lyapunov constraint into the objective function of the policy improvement in RL as in [45], we can avoid exploiting the Lipschitz constant of the overall system and potentially produce less conservative results. Lastly, the proposed design is potentially more sample efficient than an RL algorithm learning from scratch – that is, fewer data samples are needed for training. In RL, a system learns from mistakes, demanding a lot of trials and errors. In our design, the baseline control that can stabilize the overall system, can help to exclude unnecessary mistakes. Hence, it provides a good starting point for the RL training. A similar idea is used in [47] for the control of quadrotors. The baseline control in [47] is constructed based on the full accurate model of a quadrotor system, but stability analysis is missing. The design in [47] is deployed as the inner-loop control to stabilize the attitude of quadrotors, but it is not designed for tracking control with collision avoidance. # II. AUTONOMOUS SURFACE VEHICLE DYNAMICS

The full dynamics of autonomous surface vehicles (ASVs) have six degrees of freedom (DOF), including three linear motions and three rotational motions [16]. In most scenarios, we are interested in controlling the horizontal motions of (ASVs), ignoring the vertical, rolling, and pitching motions [49].

Let  $x_{p} \in \mathbb{R}$  and  $y_{p} \in \mathbb{R}$  be the horizontal position coordinates of an ASV in the inertial frame and  $\psi_{p} \in \mathbb{R}$  the heading angle as shown in Figure 1. In the body frame,  $u_{p} \in \mathbb{R}$  and  $v_{p} \in \mathbb{R}$  to represent the linear velocities in surge (x-axis) and sway (y-axis), respectively. The heading angular rate is denoted by  $r_{p} \in \mathbb{R}$ . The general 3-DOF nonlinear dynamics of an ASV are described by

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{r c l} \dot {\eta} & = & R (\eta) \nu \\ M \dot {\nu} + (C (\nu) + D (\nu)) \nu + G (\nu) & = & \tau \end{array} \right. \tag {1}
$$

where  $\pmb{\eta} = [x_p, y_p, \psi_p]^T \in \mathbb{R}^3$  is a generalized coordinate vector,  $\pmb{\nu} = [u_p, v_p, r_p]^T \in \mathbb{R}^3$  is the speed vector,  $M$  is the inertia matrix,  $\pmb{C}(\pmb{\nu})$  denotes the matrix of Coriolis and centripetal terms,  $\pmb{D}(\pmb{\nu})$  is the damping matrix,  $\pmb{\tau} = [\tau_u, \tau_v, \tau_r] \in \mathbb{R}^3$  represents the control forces and moments,  $\pmb{G}(\pmb{\nu}) = [\pmb{g}_1(\pmb{\nu}), \pmb{g}_2(\pmb{\nu}), \pmb{g}_3(\pmb{\nu})]^T \in \mathbb{R}^3$  denotes unmodeled dynamics due to gravitational and buoyancy forces and moments [16], and  $\pmb{R}$  is a rotation matrix given by

$$
\boldsymbol {R} \left(\boldsymbol {\eta}\right) = \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} \cos \psi & - \sin \psi & 0 \\ \sin \psi & \cos \psi & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right]
$$

The inertia matrix  $M = M^T > 0$  is

$$
\boldsymbol {M} = \left[ M _ {i j} \right] = \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} M _ {1 1} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & M _ {2 2} & M _ {2 3} \\ 0 & M _ {3 2} & M _ {3 3} \end{array} \right] \tag {2}
$$

where  $M_{11} = m - X_{\dot{u}}$ ,  $M_{22} = m - Y_{\dot{v}}$ ,  $M_{33} = I_z - N_{\dot{r}}$ , and  $M_{32} = M_{23} = mx_g - Y_{\dot{r}}$ . The matrix  $C(\pmb{\nu}) = -C^T(\pmb{\nu})$  is

$$
\boldsymbol {C} = \left[ C _ {i j} \right] = \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} 0 & 0 & C _ {1 3} (\boldsymbol {\nu}) \\ 0 & 0 & C _ {2 3} (\boldsymbol {\nu}) \\ - C _ {1 3} (\boldsymbol {\nu}) & - C _ {2 3} (\boldsymbol {\nu}) & 0 \end{array} \right] \tag {3}
$$

where  $C_{13}(\pmb {\nu}) = -M_{22}v - M_{23}r$ ,  $C_{23}(\pmb {\nu}) = M_{11}u$ . The damping matrix  $\pmb{D}(\pmb {\nu})$  is

$$
\boldsymbol {D} (\boldsymbol {\nu}) = \left[ D _ {i j} \right] = \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} D _ {1 1} (\boldsymbol {\nu}) & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & D _ {2 2} (\boldsymbol {\nu}) & D _ {2 3} (\boldsymbol {\nu}) \\ 0 & D _ {3 2} (\boldsymbol {\nu}) & D _ {3 3} (\boldsymbol {\nu}) \end{array} \right] \tag {4}
$$

where  $D_{11}(\pmb {\nu}) = -X_u - X_{|u|u}|u| - X_{uuu}u^2$ ,  $D_{22}(\pmb {\nu}) = -Y_v - Y_{|v|v}|v| - Y_{|r|v}|r|$ ,  $D_{23}(\pmb {\nu}) = -Y_r - Y_{|v|r}|v| - Y_{|r|r}|r|$ ,  $D_{32}(\pmb {\nu}) = -N_v - N_{|v|v}|v| - N_{|r|v}|r|$ ,  $D_{33}(\pmb {\nu}) = -N_r - N_{|v|r}|v| - N_{|r|r}|r|$ , and  $X_{(\cdot)},Y_{(\cdot)}$ , and  $N_{(\cdot)}$  are hydrodynamic coefficients whose definitions can be found in [16]. Accurate numerical models of the nonlinear dynamics (1) are rarely available. Major uncertainty sources come from  $M$ ,  $C(\pmb {\nu})$ , and  $\pmb{D}(\pmb{\nu})$  due to hydrodynamics, and  $\pmb{G}(\pmb{\nu})$  due to gravitational and buoyancy forces and moments.

# III. PROBLEM FORMULATION

In this section, we will formulate the model-reference control structure, introduce the reinforcement learning theory, and define reward functions for reinforcement learning.

# A. Model-reference control formulation

Let  $\pmb{x} = \left[\pmb{\eta}^T, \pmb{\nu}^T\right]^T$  and  $\pmb{u} = \pmb{\tau}$ , so (1) can be rewritten as

$$
\dot {\boldsymbol {x}} = \left[ \begin{array}{l l} 0 & \boldsymbol {R} (\boldsymbol {\eta}) \\ 0 & \boldsymbol {A} (\boldsymbol {\nu}) \end{array} \right] \boldsymbol {x} + \left[ \begin{array}{l} 0 \\ \boldsymbol {B} \end{array} \right] \boldsymbol {u} + \left[ \begin{array}{c} 0 \\ \boldsymbol {M} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {G} (\boldsymbol {\nu}) \end{array} \right] \tag {5}
$$

where  $A(\nu) = M^{-1}(C(\nu) + D(\nu))$ , and  $B = M^{-1}$ . Assuming that an accurate model (5) is not available, it is possible to get a nominal model expressed as

$$
\dot {\boldsymbol {x}} _ {m} = \left[ \begin{array}{l l} 0 & \boldsymbol {R} (\boldsymbol {\eta}) \\ 0 & \boldsymbol {A} _ {m} \end{array} \right] \boldsymbol {x} _ {m} + \left[ \begin{array}{c} 0 \\ \boldsymbol {B} _ {m} \end{array} \right] \boldsymbol {u} _ {m} \tag {6}
$$

where  $A_{m}$  and  $B_{m}$  are the known system matrices, and the unmodelled dynamics  $G(\nu)$  ignored. Note that  $A_{m}$  and  $B_{m}$

are different from  $A(\nu)$  and  $B$ , respectively. In  $A_{m}$  and  $B_{m}$ , we will ignore all unknown nonlinear terms, and obtain a linear nominal model. Assume that there exists a control law  $\pmb{u}_{m}$  allowing the states of the nominal system (6) to converge to a reference signal  $\pmb{x}_{r}$ , i.e.,  $\| \pmb{x}_{m} - \pmb{x}_{r}\|_{2}\to 0$  as  $t\to \infty$ .

The objective of the work in this paper is to design a controller allowing the state  $\pmb{x}$  to track state trajectories of the nominal model (6) and avoid collisions with obstacles having known states  $\pmb{x}_{o_i}$ , where  $i\in \{1,\dots ,N_o\}$  indicates the  $i$ -th obstacle. As shown in Figure 2, the overall control structure for the ASV system (5) is

$$
\boldsymbol {u} = \boldsymbol {u} _ {b} + \boldsymbol {u} _ {l} \tag {7}
$$

where  $\pmb{u}_b$  is a baseline control designed based on (6), and  $\pmb{u}_l$  is a control law from the deep RL module whose design is provided in Section IV-B.

Remark 1. The baseline control  $\mathbf{u}_b$  is employed to ensure the basic tracking performance without obstacles, (i.e., local stability of the tracking control), while  $\mathbf{u}_l$  is introduced to compensate for all system uncertainties and achieve collision avoidance. The baseline control  $\mathbf{u}_b$  in (7) can be designed using any existing method based on the nominal model (6). One potential choice for the design of  $\mathbf{u}_b$  is the nonlinear backstepping control [40]. Hence, we ignore the design process of  $\mathbf{u}_b$ , and focus on the development of  $\mathbf{u}_l$  with RL.

# B. Markov decision process

For the formulation of RL, the ASV dynamics (5) and (6) are characterized using another mathematical model called Markov decision process that is denoted by a tuple  $\mathcal{MDP} \coloneqq \langle S, \mathcal{U}, \mathcal{P}, R, \gamma \rangle$ , where  $\mathcal{S}$  is the state space,  $\mathcal{U}$  specifies the action/input space,  $\mathcal{P}: S \times \mathcal{U} \times S \to \mathbb{R}$  defines a transition probability,  $R: S \times \mathcal{U} \to \mathbb{R}$  is a reward function, and  $\gamma \in [0, 1)$  is a discount factor. Note that the state vector  $s \in S$  contains all available signals affecting the learned control  $u_l$ . In this paper, such signals include  $x$ ,  $x_m$ ,  $x_r$ ,  $u_b$ , and  $x_{o_i}$ , where  $x_m$  represents the desired behaviour of the system (5) and  $u_b$  is a function of  $x$  and  $x_r$ , and  $x_{o_i}$  are obstacle states in the neighbourhood (e.g., the position and velocity of the  $i$ -th obstacle). Hence,  $s = \left\{\boldsymbol{x}_m, \boldsymbol{x}, u_b, \cup_i^{N_o} x_{o_i}\right\}$ , where  $\cup_i^{N_o} x_{o_i}$  denotes the states of  $N_o$  obstacles detected by the ASV. More details on obstacles will be discussed in Section III-D.

Since RL learns the control policies using data samples, it is assumed that we can sample input and state data from system

(5) at discrete time steps. The sample time step is fixed and denoted by  $\delta t$ . Without loss of generality, let  $\pmb{x}_t$ ,  $\pmb{u}_{b,t}$ , and  $\pmb{u}_{l,t}$  be the ASV state, the baseline control action, and the control action from RL at the time step  $t$ , respectively. The union of obstacles detected by the ASV is characterized by  $\cup_{i}^{N_o}\pmb{x}_{o_i,t}$ . The state signal  $\pmb{s}$  at the time step  $t$  is, therefore, denoted by  $\pmb{s}_t = \{\pmb{x}_{m,t},\pmb{x}_t,\pmb{u}_{b,t},\cup_i^{N_o}\pmb{x}_{o_i,t}\}$ .

# C. Reinforcement learning

For standard RL, the objective is to maximize an expected accumulated return described by a value function  $V_{\pi}(s_t)$  with

$$
V _ {\boldsymbol {\pi}} \left(\boldsymbol {s} _ {t}\right) = \sum_ {t} ^ {\infty} \sum_ {\boldsymbol {u} _ {l, t}} \boldsymbol {\pi} \left(\boldsymbol {u} _ {l, t} \mid \boldsymbol {s} _ {t}\right) \sum_ {\boldsymbol {s} _ {t + 1}} \mathcal {P} _ {t + 1 \mid t} \left(R _ {t} + \gamma V _ {\boldsymbol {\pi}} \left(\boldsymbol {s} _ {t + 1}\right)\right) \tag {8}
$$

where  $\mathcal{P}_{t + 1|t} = \mathcal{P}\left(\boldsymbol{s}_{t + 1}|\boldsymbol{s}_t,\boldsymbol{u}_{l,t}\right)$  is the transition probability of the ASV system,  $R_{t} = R(\boldsymbol{s}_{t},\boldsymbol{u}_{l,t})$  is the reward function,  $\gamma \in [0,1)$  is a constant discount factor, and  $\pi (\boldsymbol{u}_{l,t}|\boldsymbol{s}_t)$  is called control policy in RL. A policy in RL, denoted by  $\pi (\boldsymbol{u}_{l,t}|\boldsymbol{s}_t)$ , is the probability of choosing an action  $\boldsymbol{u}_{l,t}\in \mathcal{U}$  at a state  $\boldsymbol{s}_t\in S$  [27]. In this paper, a Gaussian policy is used, which is

$$
\pi \left(\boldsymbol {u} _ {l} | s\right) = \mathcal {N} \left(\boldsymbol {u} _ {l} (s), \sigma\right) \tag {9}
$$

where  $\mathcal{N}(\cdot, \cdot)$  denotes a Gaussian distribution with  $u_{l}(s)$  as the mean value and  $\sigma$  as the covariance matrix. The covariance matrix  $\sigma$  controls the exploration performance at the learning stage. For the algorithm design, we also introduce an action-value function (a.k.a., Q-function) defined by

$$
Q _ {\boldsymbol {\pi}} \left(\boldsymbol {s} _ {t}, \boldsymbol {u} _ {l, t}\right) = R _ {t} + \gamma \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {s} _ {t + 1}} \left[ V _ {\boldsymbol {\pi}} \left(\boldsymbol {s} _ {t + 1}\right) \right] \tag {10}
$$

where  $\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{s}_{t + 1}}[\cdot ] = \sum_{\boldsymbol{s}_{t + 1}}\mathcal{P}_{t + 1|t}[\cdot ]$  is an expectation operator over the distribution of  $s_{t + 1}$ . Maximizing  $Q_{\pi}(s_t,\pmb {u}_{l,t})$  is equivalent to maximizing  $V_{\pi}(s_{t})$ . In the sequel, we will focus the maximization of  $Q_{\pi}(\pmb {s}_t,\pmb {u}_{l,t})$  instead of  $V_{\pi}(s_t)$ .

In this paper, the deep RL is resolved based on the soft actor-critic (SAC) algorithm which provides both sample efficient learning and convergence [50]. In SAC, an entropy term is added to regulate the exploration performance at the training stage, thus resulting in a modified Q-function in (11).

$$
\begin{array}{l} Q _ {\boldsymbol {\pi}} \left(\boldsymbol {s} _ {t}, \boldsymbol {u} _ {l, t}\right) = R _ {t} + \gamma \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {s} _ {t + 1}} \left[ V _ {\boldsymbol {\pi}} \left(\boldsymbol {s} _ {t + 1}\right) \right. \\ \left. + \alpha \mathcal {H} \left(\boldsymbol {\pi} \left(\boldsymbol {u} _ {l, t + 1} \mid \boldsymbol {s} _ {t + 1}\right)\right) \right] \tag {11} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\mathcal{H}(\pi (\pmb{u}_{l,t}|\pmb{s}_t)) = -\sum_{\pmb{u}_{l,t}}\pi (\pmb{u}_{l,t}|\pmb{s}_t)\ln (\pi (\pmb{u}_{l,t}|\pmb{s}_t)) = -\mathbb{E}_{\pmb{\pi}}[\ln (\pi (\pmb{u}_{l,t}|\pmb{s}_t))]$  is the entropy of the policy, and  $\alpha$  is a temperature parameter [50]. Hence, SAC aims to solve the following optimization problem.

$$
\begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {\pi} ^ {*} = \arg \max  _ {\boldsymbol {\pi} \in \Pi} \left(R _ {t} + \gamma \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {s} _ {t + 1}} \left[ V _ {\boldsymbol {\pi}} \left(\boldsymbol {s} _ {t + 1}\right) \right. \right. \\ \left. + \alpha \mathcal {H} \left(\boldsymbol {\pi} \left(\boldsymbol {u} _ {l, t + 1} \mid \boldsymbol {s} _ {t + 1}\right)\right) \right] \tag {12} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\Pi$  denotes a policy set.

Remark 2. Once the optimization problem (12) is resolved, we will have  $\pi^{*}(\pmb{u}_{l}|\pmb{s}) = \mathcal{N}(\pmb{u}_{l}^{*}(\pmb{s}),\pmb{\sigma}^{*})$  according to (9). The variance  $\pmb{\sigma}^{*}$  will be close to 0. Thus, the stochastic policy will converge to a deterministic one in the end. The mean value function  $\pmb{u}_{l}^{*}(\pmb{s})$  will be the learned optimal control law

that is eventually used to compensate system uncertainties and avoid collisions. Notably, the optimal control law  $\pmb{u}_l^*(s)$  will be learned instead of designed. In the real implementation,  $\pmb{u}_l^*(s)$  are approximated using deep neural networks that will be discussed in Section IV-A. Training/learning process of SAC will repeatedly execute policy evaluation and policy improvement. In the policy evaluation, the Q-value in (11) is computed by applying a Bellman operation  $Q_{\pi}(s_t, \boldsymbol{u}_{l,t}) = \mathcal{T}^{\pi} Q_{\pi}(s_t, \boldsymbol{u}_{l,t})$  where

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathcal {T} ^ {\pi} Q _ {\pi} \left(\boldsymbol {s} _ {t}, \boldsymbol {u} _ {l, t}\right) = R _ {t} + \gamma \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {s} _ {t + 1}} \left\{\mathbb {E} _ {\pi} \left[ Q _ {\pi} \left(\boldsymbol {s} _ {t + 1}, \boldsymbol {u} _ {l, t + 1}\right) \right. \right. \\ \left. - \alpha \ln \left(\pi \left(\boldsymbol {u} _ {l, t + 1} \mid \boldsymbol {s} _ {t + 1}\right)\right) \right] \rbrace \tag {13} \\ \end{array}
$$

In the policy improvement, the policy is updated by

$$
\boldsymbol {\pi} _ {n e w} = \arg \min  _ {\boldsymbol {\pi} ^ {\prime} \in \Pi} \mathcal {D} _ {K L} \left(\boldsymbol {\pi} ^ {\prime} (\cdot | \boldsymbol {s} _ {t}) \right\| Z ^ {\boldsymbol {\pi} _ {o l d}} e ^ {\frac {1}{\alpha} Q ^ {\boldsymbol {\pi} _ {o l d}} (\boldsymbol {s} _ {t}, \cdot)} \tag {14}
$$

where  $\pi_{old}$  denotes the policy from the last update,  $Q^{\pi_{old}}$  is the Q-value of  $\pi_{old}$ ,  $\mathcal{D}_{KL}$  denotes the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, and  $Z^{\pi_{old}}$  is a normalization factor. Via mathematical manipulations, the objective is transformed into

$$
\boldsymbol {\pi} ^ {*} = \arg \min  _ {\boldsymbol {\pi} \in \Pi} \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {\pi}} \left[ \alpha \ln \left(\boldsymbol {\pi} \left(\boldsymbol {u} _ {l, t} | \boldsymbol {s} _ {t}\right)\right) - Q \left(\boldsymbol {s} _ {t}, \boldsymbol {u} _ {l, t}\right) \right] \tag {15}
$$

More details on how (15) is obtained can be found in [50].

# D. Reward functions

In our design, two objectives are defined for the ASV: trajectory tracking and collision avoidance. For the trajectory tracking, we aim to allow system (5) to track the nominal system (6), so the tracking reward  $R_{t,1}$  is defined as

$$
R _ {t, 1} = - \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {t} - \boldsymbol {x} _ {m, t}\right) ^ {T} \boldsymbol {H} _ {1} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {t} - \boldsymbol {x} _ {m, t}\right) - \boldsymbol {u} _ {l, t} ^ {T} \boldsymbol {H} _ {2} \boldsymbol {u} _ {l, t} \tag {16}
$$

where  $H_{1} > 0$  and  $H_{2} > 0$  are positive definite matrices.

The second objective is to avoid obstacles along the trajectory of the ASV. Figure 3 shows variables used for the definition of the reward function for collision avoidance. All obstacles are assumed to be inscribed in a circle. The maximum size of the  $i$ -th obstacle is defined by  $d_{o_i}$  as shown in Figure 3. The size of the ASV is denoted by  $d_a$ . The relative distance between the  $i$ -th obstacle and the ASV is defined as  $d_{a o_i}$ . If  $d_{a o_i} \leq d_d$ , the  $i$ -th obstacle is visible

Note that the obstacles could be either static or moving, so the state vector the  $i$ -th obstacle is written as  $\pmb{x}_{o_i} = \left[\pmb{p}_{o_i}^T, \pmb{v}_{o_i}^T\right]^T$ , where  $\pmb{p}_{o_i}$  is the position of the  $i$ -th obstacle, and  $\pmb{v}_{o_i}$  is the velocity of the  $i$ -th obstacle. Let  $\pmb{p}_a = [x_p, y_p]^T$  and  $\pmb{v}_a = [u_p, v_p]^T$  be the position and velocity of the ASV, respectively. For the  $i$ -th visible obstacle at the time step  $t$ , define the following variable shown in Figure 4.

$$
d _ {i, t} = \frac {\left\| \left(\boldsymbol {v} _ {a} - \boldsymbol {v} _ {o _ {i}}\right) ^ {\times} \left(\boldsymbol {p} _ {o _ {i}} - \boldsymbol {p} _ {a}\right) \right\| _ {2}}{\left\| \boldsymbol {v} _ {a} - \boldsymbol {v} _ {o _ {i}} \right\| _ {2}} \tag {17}
$$

where “ $\times$ ” denotes the cross product operation,  $d_{i,t}$  represents the closest possible distance between the ASV and the obstacle, if the ASV keeps its current moving direction relative to the obstacle. Note that  $d_{i,t}$  is only meaningful, if  $(\pmb{v}_a - \pmb{v}_{o_i})^T (\pmb{p}_{o_i} - \pmb{p}_a) > 0$ . If  $(\pmb{v}_a - \pmb{v}_{o_i})^T (\pmb{p}_{o_i} - \pmb{p}_a) > 0$ , it implies that the ASV moves towards the obstacle, otherwise, the ASV moves away from the obstacle. Therefore, the reward function for collision avoidance is defined to be

$$
R _ {t, 2} = \left\{ \begin{array}{c c} - \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N _ {o}} \frac {q _ {c , i} \mathbb {1} _ {o _ {i}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {o _ {i}} , \boldsymbol {p} _ {a} , \boldsymbol {v} _ {a}\right)}{1 + \exp \left(c _ {i} \left(d _ {i , t} - d _ {s _ {i}}\right)\right)}, & d _ {a o _ {i}} \leq d _ {d} \\ 0, & \text {o t h e r w i s e} \end{array} \right. \tag {18}
$$

where  $q_{c,i} > 0$  is the maximum possible cost for collisions,  $c_{i} > 0$  is a design parameter, and  $\mathbb{1}_{o_i}\left(\pmb{x}_{o_i},\pmb{p}_a,\pmb{v}_a\right)$  is

$$
\mathbb {1} _ {o _ {i}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {o _ {i}}, \boldsymbol {p} _ {a}, \boldsymbol {v} _ {a}\right) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1, & \left(\boldsymbol {v} _ {a} - \boldsymbol {v} _ {o _ {i}}\right) ^ {T} \left(\boldsymbol {p} _ {o _ {i}} - \boldsymbol {p} _ {a}\right) > 0 \\ 0, & \text {o t h e r w i s e} \end{array} \right.
$$

The parameter  $c_{i} > 0$  adjusts the sensitivity of collision avoidance of RL in related to the  $i$ -th obstacle. The influence of  $c_{i} > 0$  on  $R_{t,2}$  is illustrated in Figure 5.

The overall reward function is, therefore, defined to be

$$
R _ {t} = R _ {t, 1} + R _ {t, 2} \tag {19}

# IV. MODEL-REFERENCE DEEP REINFORCEMENT LEARNING DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION

In this section, we will present the design and practical implementation of the model-reference deep RL control.

# A. Deep neural networks

In RL, the value function  $Q_{\pi}(s_t, \boldsymbol{u}_{l,t})$ , which contains future information as shown in (10), is not known in advance. Similarly, the control policy  $\pi$  is unknown as well. Hence, a feasible solution is to approximate both the value function  $Q_{\pi}(s_t, \boldsymbol{u}_{l,t})$  and the control policy  $\pi$  using deep neural networks. In this paper, the deep neural networks used to approximate both the value function  $Q_{\pi}(s_t, \boldsymbol{u}_{l,t})$  and the policy  $\pi (\boldsymbol{u}_{l,t} | s_t)$  are chosen to be fully connected multiple layer perceptrons (MLP) with rectified linear unit (ReLU) nonlinearities as the activation functions. The ReLU nonlinearities are defined by

$$
\underline {{r e l u}} (z) = \max  \{z, 0 \}
$$

The ReLU activation function outperforms other activation functions like sigmoid functions [51]. For a vector  $z = [z_{1}, \ldots, z_{n}]^{T} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ , there exists  $\underline{relu}(z) = [\underline{relu}(z_{1}), \ldots, \underline{relu}(z_{n})]^{T}$ . As an example, a MLP with "ReLU" as the activation functions and two hidden layers is

$$
\underline {{M L P}} _ {\mathrm {w}} ^ {2} (z) = \mathrm {w} _ {2} \left[ \underline {{r e l u}} \left(\mathrm {w} _ {1} \left[ \underline {{r e l u}} \left(\mathrm {w} _ {0} [ z ^ {T}, 1 ]\right) ^ {T}, 1 \right] ^ {T}\right) ^ {T}, 1 \right] ^ {T} \tag {20}
$$

where  $\left[z^T, 1\right]^T$  is a vector composed of  $z$  and a bias  $1$ , the superscript "2" denotes the total number of hidden layers, the subscript "w" denotes the parameter set to be trained in a MLP with  $\mathrm{w} = \{\mathrm{w}_0, \mathrm{w}_1, \mathrm{w}_2\}$ , and  $\mathrm{w}_0, \mathrm{w}_1, \mathrm{w}_2$  are weight matrices with appropriate dimensions.

![](images/73f15f845b661999d73988e43ac3a265abfb0cc2871d04fa0abc535c600e49c0.jpg)  
Fig. 6: Approximation of  $Q_{\theta}$  and  $\pmb{u}_{l,\phi}$  using MLP's

![](images/35cf065f1a6380dbae185d1006a314f1e2a08314cd09ff9d931e4c0d098b43b5.jpg)

If there is a set of inputs  $z = \{z_{1},\dots ,z_{L}\}$  for the MLP in (20) with  $z_{1},\ldots ,z_{L}$  denoting vector signals, we have

$$
\underline {{M L P}} _ {\mathrm {w}} ^ {2} (z) = \underline {{M L P}} _ {\mathrm {w}} ^ {2} \left(\left[ z _ {1} ^ {T}, \dots , z _ {L} ^ {T} \right] ^ {T}\right) \tag {21}
$$

Besides,  $\underline{MLP}_{\mathrm{w}}^{2}(z_{1},z_{2}) = \underline{MLP}_{\mathrm{w}}^{2}\left(\left[z_{1}^{T},z_{2}^{T}\right]^{T}\right)$  for two vector inputs  $z_{1}$  and  $z_{2}$ . If  $z_{1} = \{z_{11},\dots ,z_{1L}\}$  is a set of vectors,  $\underline{MLP}_{\mathrm{w}}^{2}(z_{1},z_{2}) = \underline{MLP}_{\mathrm{w}}^{2}\left(\left[z_{11}^{T},\dots ,z_{1L}^{T},z_{2}^{T}\right]^{T}\right)$ .

Let  $Q_{\theta}(s_t, \boldsymbol{u}_{l,t})$  be the approximated Q-function using a MLP with a set of parameters denoted by  $\theta$ . Following (20) and (21), the Q-function approximation  $Q_{\theta}(s_t, \boldsymbol{u}_{l,t})$  is

$$
Q _ {\theta} \left(\boldsymbol {s} _ {t}, \boldsymbol {u} _ {l, t}\right) = \underline {{M L P}} _ {\theta} ^ {K _ {1}} \left(\boldsymbol {s} _ {t}, \boldsymbol {u} _ {l, t}\right) \tag {22}
$$

where  $\theta = \{\theta_0, \dots, \theta_{K_1}\}$  with  $\theta_i \in \mathbb{R}^{(L) \times (L + 1)}$  for  $0 \leq i \leq K_1$  denoting the weight matrices with proper dimensions. The deep neural network for  $Q_\theta$  is illustrated in Figure 6.

The control law  $\pmb{u}_l$  is also approximated using a MLP. The approximated control law of  $\pmb{u}_l$  with a parameter set  $\phi$  is

$$
\boldsymbol {u} _ {l, \phi} = \underline {{M L P}} _ {\phi} ^ {K _ {2}} \left(\boldsymbol {s} _ {t}\right) \tag {23}
$$

The illustration of  $\pmb{u}_{l,\phi}$  is given in Figure 6. In SAC, there are two outputs for the MLP in (23). One is the control law  $\pmb{u}_{l,\phi}$ , the other one is  $\sigma_{\phi}$  that is the standard deviation of the exploration noise [50]. According to (9), the parameterized policy  $\pi_{\phi}$  in our model-reference RL is

$$
\boldsymbol {\pi} _ {\phi} = \mathcal {N} \left(\boldsymbol {u} _ {l, \phi} \left(\boldsymbol {s} _ {t}\right), \boldsymbol {\sigma} _ {\phi} ^ {2}\right) \tag {24}
$$

The deep neural network for  $Q_{\theta}$  is called critic, while the one for  $\pi_{\phi}$  is called actor.

# B. Algorithm design and implementation

The algorithm training process is illustrated in Figure 7. The whole training process will be offline. We repeatedly run the system (5) under a trajectory tracking task. At each time step  $t + 1$ , we collect data samples, such as an input from the last time step  $\boldsymbol{u}_{l,t}$ , a state from the last time step  $\boldsymbol{s}_t$ , a reward  $R_t$  and a current state  $\boldsymbol{s}_{t+1}$ . Those historical data will be stored as a tuple  $(\boldsymbol{s}_t, \boldsymbol{u}_{l,t}, R_t, \boldsymbol{s}_{t+1})$  at a replay memory  $\mathcal{D}$  [52]. At each policy evaluation or improvement step, we randomly sample a batch of historical data,  $\mathcal{B}$ , from the replay memory  $\mathcal{D}$  for the training of the parameters  $\theta$  and  $\phi$ . Starting the training, we apply the baseline control policy  $\boldsymbol{u}_b$  to an ASV system to collect the initial data  $\mathcal{D}_0$  as shown in Algorithm 1. The initial data set  $\mathcal{D}_0$  is used for the initial fitting of Q-value functions. When the initialization is over, we execute both  $\boldsymbol{u}_b$  and the latest updated RL policy  $\pi_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{u}_{l,t} | \boldsymbol{s}_t)$  to run the ASV system.

At the policy evaluation step, the parameters  $\theta$  are trained to minimize the following Bellman residual.

$$
J _ {Q} (\theta) = \mathbb {E} _ {\left(\boldsymbol {s} _ {t}, \boldsymbol {u} _ {l, t}\right) \sim \mathcal {D}} \left[ \frac {1}{2} \left(Q _ {\theta} \left(\boldsymbol {s} _ {t}, \boldsymbol {u} _ {l, t}\right) - Y _ {\text {t a r g e t}}\right) ^ {2} \right] \tag {25}
$$

where  $(\pmb{s}_t,\pmb{u}_{l,t})\sim \mathcal{D}$  implies that we randomly pick data samples  $(s_{t},u_{l,t})$  from a replay memory  $\mathcal{D}$ , and

$$
Y _ {t a r g e t} = R _ {t} + \gamma \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {s} _ {t + 1}} \left[ \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {\pi}} \left[ Q _ {\bar {\theta}} \left(\boldsymbol {s} _ {t + 1}, \boldsymbol {u} _ {l, t + 1}\right) - \alpha \ln (\boldsymbol {\pi} _ {\phi}) \right] \right]
$$

# B. Stability of the tracking control

In this subsection, we will show the closed-loop stability of the overall control law (baseline control  $\boldsymbol{u}_b$  plus the learned control  $\boldsymbol{u}_l$ ) for the tracking control without obstacles. The closed-loop stability is analyzed under the general tracking performance without the consideration of collision avoidance, as the tracking control is the fundamental task. Before the closed-loop stability is analyzed, the admissible control concept is introduced in Definition 1, which is similar to the admissible control in adaptive dynamic programming [56]-[58].

Definition 1. A control law  $\mathbf{u}_b$  is said admissible with respect to the system (5), if it can stabilize the system (5) and ensure that the state of (5) is uniformly ultimately bounded under system uncertainties.

Note that the admissible control in [56]-[58] needs to provide the asymptotic stability for the system. However, the admissible

control in Definition 1 doesn't necessarily ensure the system (5) to be asymptotically stable. Hence, the admissible control concept in this paper is less conservative than that in [56]–[58].

Assume that the baseline control  $\boldsymbol{u}_b$  developed using the nominal system (6) of the ASV (5) is an admissible control law for the uncertain system (5). Let  $\Delta(t)$  be the overall uncertainties in (5). Without loss of generality, it is assumed that  $\Delta(t)$  is bounded, namely  $\| \Delta(t) \|_{\mathcal{L}_{\infty}} \leq \tilde{\Delta}$  where  $\| \cdot \|_{\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}$  denotes the  $\mathcal{L}_{\infty}$  norm. In this paper, the objective of the tracking control in obstacle-free environment is to ensure that the ASV system (5) can track its desired behaviour defined by its nominal system (6), namely  $\| \boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol{x}_m \|_2 \to 0$  as  $t \to \infty$ . Let  $\boldsymbol{e}_t = \boldsymbol{x}_t - \boldsymbol{x}_{m,t}$  be the tracking error of the ASV at the time instant  $t$ . The following assumption is made for a admissible baseline control  $\boldsymbol{u}_b$  according to Definition 1 and Theorem 4.18 in [59] (Chapter 4, Page 172).

Assumption 2. The baseline control law  $\mathbf{u}_b$  is feasible with respect to the uncertain system (5), and there exists a continuously differentiable function  $\mathbb{V}(s_t)$  associate with  $\mathbf{u}_b$  such that

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mu_ {1} \left(\| \boldsymbol {e} _ {t} \| _ {2}\right) \leq \mathbb {V} \left(\boldsymbol {e} _ {t}\right) \leq \mu_ {2} \left(\| \boldsymbol {e} _ {t} \| _ {2}\right) \\ \mathbb {V} \left(\boldsymbol {e} _ {t + 1}\right) - \mathbb {V} \left(\boldsymbol {e} _ {t}\right) \leq - \mathbb {W} _ {1} \left(\boldsymbol {e} _ {t}\right) + \mu_ {3} \left(\left\| \Delta \left(\boldsymbol {e} _ {t}\right) \right\| _ {2}\right) \tag {31} \\ \mathbb {W} _ {1} \left(\boldsymbol {e} _ {t}\right) > \mu_ {3} \left(\| \Delta (t) \| _ {2}\right), \forall \| \boldsymbol {e} _ {t} \| _ {2} > c _ {\Delta} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\mu_1(\cdot),\mu_2(\cdot)$  , and  $\mu_{3}(\cdot)$  are class  $\kappa$  functions,  $\mathbb{W}(\pmb {e}_t)$  is a continuous positive definite function, and  $c_{\Delta}$  is a constant related to the upper bound of system uncertainty

Assumption 2 is possible in real world. One can treat the nominal model (6) as a linearized model of the overall ASV system (5) around a certain equilibrium. Assumption 2 presents the basic design requirements for the baseline control law. With a baseline control law satisfying Assumption 1, we could obtain two advantages which makes the RL process more efficient. Firstly, it can ensure that the reward function  $R_{t}$  is bounded, implying that both  $V_{\pi}(s_{t + 1})$  and  $Q(s_{t},\pmb{u}_{l,t})$  are bounded. Secondly, it could provide a "warm" start for the RL process.

With a feasible baseline control, the uncertain ASV system can be ensured to be stable during the entire learning process. In the stability analysis, we ignore the entropy term  $\mathcal{H}(\pi)$ , as it will converge to zero in the end and it is only introduced to regulate the exploration magnitude. # VI. SIMULATION RESULTS

In this section, the proposed learning-based control algorithm is implemented to the trajectory tracking control of a supply ship model presented in [49], [60]. The ASV has two actuators that are a propeller and a rudder in the rear. Hence, we mainly consider two control inputs in the design, which are  $\tau_{u}$  for the

The based-line control law  $u_{b}$  is designed based on a nominal model with the following simplified linear dynamics in terms of the backstepping control method [40], [59].

$$

# VII. CONCLUSIONS

In this paper, we presented a novel learning-based control algorithm for ASV systems with collision avoidance. The proposed control algorithm combined a conventional control method with deep reinforcement learning to provide closed-loop stability guarantee, uncertainty compensation, and collision avoidance. Convergence of the learning algorithm was analyzed. We also presented the stability analysis of the tracking control. The proposed control algorithm shows much better performance in both tracking control and collision avoidance than the RL without baseline control. In the future works, we will further analyze the sample efficiency of the proposed algorithm, and extend the design to the scenario with extensive environment disturbances.

$$
\boldsymbol {u} _ {l} ^ {1} = \min  _ {\boldsymbol {\pi}} \left(- R _ {t} ^ {0} + \gamma \mathbb {V} ^ {0} \left(\boldsymbol {e} _ {t + 1}\right)\right) \tag {A.9}
$$

Note that  $\pmb{u}_l$  is implicitly contained in both  $R_{t}$  and  $\mathbb{V}^0 (e_{t + 1})$  according to (16) and (A.7). In the policy evaluation, the following update is conducted.

$$
\mathbb {V} ^ {1} \left(\boldsymbol {e} _ {t}\right) = - R _ {t} ^ {1} + \gamma \mathbb {V} ^ {0} \left(\boldsymbol {e} _ {t + 1}\right) \tag {A.10}
$$

where  $R_{t}^{1} = R(\pmb{s}_{t},\pmb{u}_{l,t}^{1})$ . Hence, for  $\pmb{u}_l^1$ , there exists

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbb {V} ^ {1} \left(\boldsymbol {e} _ {t + 1}\right) - \mathbb {V} ^ {1} \left(\boldsymbol {e} _ {t}\right) = \mathbb {V} ^ {1} \left(\boldsymbol {e} _ {t + 1}\right) + R _ {t} ^ {1} - \gamma \mathbb {V} ^ {0} \left(\boldsymbol {e} _ {t + 1}\right) \\ = \mathbb {V} ^ {1} \left(\boldsymbol {e} _ {t + 1}\right) - \mathbb {V} ^ {0} \left(\boldsymbol {e} _ {t + 1}\right) + R _ {t} ^ {1} \\ - R _ {t} ^ {0} + R _ {t} ^ {0} + (1 - \gamma) \mathbb {V} ^ {0} \left(\boldsymbol {e} _ {t + 1}\right) \\ \leq - \mathbb {W} _ {1} \left(\boldsymbol {e} _ {t}\right) + \mu_ {3} \left(\| \Delta (t) \| _ {2}\right) + R _ {t} ^ {1} \\ - R _ {t} ^ {0} + \mathbb {V} ^ {1} \left(\boldsymbol {e} _ {t + 1}\right) - \mathbb {V} ^ {0} \left(\boldsymbol {e} _ {t + 1}\right) \\ \end{array}
$$

According to (A.9) and (A.10), one has  $\mathbb{V}^1 (\pmb{e}_{t + 1})\leq \mathbb{V}^0 (\pmb{e}_{t + 1})$  and  $R_{t}^{1}\leq R_{t}^{0}$ . Therefore,  $\mathbb{V}^0 (\pmb{e}_{t + 1}) - \mathbb{V}^1 (\pmb{e}_{t + 1}) + R_t^0 -R_t^1 +$ $\mathbb{W}_1(\pmb {e}_t)\geq \mathbb{W}_1(\pmb {e}_t)$ . As  $\mathbb{W}_1(\pmb {e}_t) > \mu_3(\| \Delta (t)\| _2),\forall \| \pmb {e}_t\| _2 > c_\Delta$  there must exist a new constant  $c_{\Delta}^{1}\leq c_{\Delta}$  such that  $\mathbb{V}^0 (\pmb{e}_{t + 1}) - \mathbb{V}^1 (\pmb{e}_{t + 1}) + R_t^0 -R_t^1 +\mathbb{W}_1(\pmb {e}_t) > \mu_3(\| \Delta (t)\| _2)$ $\forall \| e_t\| _2 > c_\Delta^1$

The new control law  $\boldsymbol{u}_l^1$  can also ensure the closed-loop ASV system to be uniformly ultimately bounded. In the worst case,  $c_{\Delta}^{1} = c_{\Delta}$ , which implies that  $\boldsymbol{u}_l^1$  will have the same control performance with  $\boldsymbol{u}_t^0$ , namely guaranteeing the same ultimate boundaries for the tracking errors. If there exists  $c_{\Delta}^{1} > c_{\Delta}$ , it implies that  $\boldsymbol{u}_l^1$  will result in smaller tracking errors than  $\boldsymbol{u}_l^0$ .

Following the same analysis, we can show that  $\pmb{u}_l^2$  also stabilizes the ASV system (5) in terms of  $\mathbb{V}^1(\pmb{s}_t)$  and replacing  $\pmb{u}_l^0$  in (A.9) and (A.10) with  $\pmb{u}_l^1$ . Repeating (A.9) and (A.10) for all  $i = 1, 2, \ldots$ , we can prove that all  $\pmb{u}_l^i$  can stabilize the ASV system (5), if Assumption 2 holds. It implies that the

ASV system (5) will be stabilized by the overall control law  $\pmb{u}^{i} = \pmb{u}_{b} + \pmb{u}_{l}^{i}$ .
