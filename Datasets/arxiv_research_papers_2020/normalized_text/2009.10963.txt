# terahertz massive mimo with holographic reconfigurable intelligent surfaces

ziwei wan, zhen gao, member, ieee, feifei gao, fellow, ieee, marco di renzo, fellow, ieee, and mohamed-slim alouini, fellow, ieee

abstract—we propose a holographic version of a reconfigurable intelligent surface (ris) and investigate its application to terahertz (thz) massive multiple-input multiple-output systems. capitalizing on the miniaturization of thz electronic components, riss can be implemented by densely packing subwavelength unit cells, so as to realize continuous or quasicontinuous apertures and to enable holographic communications. in this paper, in particular, we derive the beam pattern of a holographic ris. our analysis reveals that the beam pattern of an ideal holographic ris can be well approximated by that of an ultra-dense ris, which has a more practical hardware architecture. in addition, we propose a closed-loop channel estimation (ce) scheme to effectively estimate the broadband channels that characterize thz massive mimo systems aided by holographic riss. the proposed ce scheme includes a downlink coarse ce stage and an uplink finer-grained ce stage. the uplink pilot signals are judiciously designed for obtaining good ce performance. moreover, to reduce the pilot overhead, we introduce a compressive sensing-based ce algorithm, which exploits the dual sparsity of thz mimo channels in both the angular domain and delay domain. simulation results demonstrate the superiority of holographic riss over the non-holographic ones, and the effectiveness of the proposed ce scheme.

index terms—terahertz communications, reconfigurable intelligent surface, massive mimo, holographic communications, compressive sensing (cs), channel estimation.

in spite of the appealing advantages of the thz band, establishing a reliable transmission link at thz frequencies is a non-trivial task. this is because (i) there exist strong atmospheric attenuation and extremely high free-space losses in the thz band; and (ii) the line-of-sight (los) link is very sensitive to blockage effects in the thz band and thus the links are usually intermittent. these disadvantages may negatively affect the communication range and may severely degrade the service coverage of thz communication systems. the deployment of massive [4] or even ultra-massive [5] multiple-input multiple-output (mimo) systems in the context of thz communications may provide considerable beamforming gain in order to overcome the mentioned limitations, but it may result in an unaffordable power consumption and may put an overweight burden on the overall communication system design.

recently, the emerging technology of reconfigurable intelligent surface (ris) has been proposed and applied to wireless communications in order to enhance the communication performance in various scenarios, such as mimo communications, physical layer security, unmanned aerial vehicle communications, simultaneous wireless information and power transfer, cognitive radio systems [8]–[33]. made of passive and metamaterial-based reconfigurable elements, an ris can manipulate both the phase and amplitude of the incident electromagnetic (em) signals so as to reflect them towards the desired directions. more importantly, unlike other transmission technologies such as active relay [16], an ris does not need power-hungry radio frequency chains (rfcs) and power amplifiers, which may be beneficial for developing green and cost-efficient communications. although the application of riss to the mmwave band has been investigated recently [28]–[31], the utilization of ris for thz communications is still at its infancy.

# a. prior work

ris-aided mimo communications have attracted lots of research interest lately. the authors of [20] propose a joint active and passive beamforming scheme based on convex optimization to maximize the signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (sinr) at the receivers. a similar scenario is considered in [21], where the phases of the ris that maximize the sinr are computed via the projected gradient ascent algorithm.

in [22], the advanced deep reinforcement learning (drl) method is considered for the beamforming design in ris-aided multiuser systems. the authors of [23] develop a scalable optimization framework for large riss, based on which a two-stage method, including an offline design and an online optimization, is proposed to optimize the ris. a practical amplitude and phase shift model for the reflecting element of an ris is investigated in [24], where the beamforming optimization is conducted by considering the phase-dependent amplitude variation of each reflecting element. in [25], the authors introduce a new communication model for riss that accounts for the mutual coupling and the interplay between the amplitude and phase response of the scattering elements of the ris.

the designs in [20]–[25] rely on the knowledge of global channel state information (csi). since no active elements are used in riss, channel estimation (ce) is a challenging task and an essential prerequisite in ris-aided mimo systems. in [26] and [27], the authors propose a ce algorithm for ris-aided systems, based on the least square (ls) estimator, for application to frequency-flat fading channels and frequency-selective fading channels, respectively. to further facilitate the ce task when large-size arrays are deployed, compressive sensing (cs)-based ce schemes are investigated in [28], [29], where the inherent sparsity of mmwave channels in the angular domain is exploited in order to reduce the pilot overhead. in [30] and [31], a new architecture of ris for application to mmwave band is proposed, where a few active rfcs are available at the ris in order to learn the channel in real time. based on this architecture, deep learning (dl) techniques are adopted for ce and beamforming.

as far as the application of riss to thz communications is concerned, the authors of [32] present an ris-aided thz mimo communication system for indoor applications. joint ce and data rate maximization schemes are proposed in [32] based on both cs and dl techniques. in [33], the problem of ris-assisted secure transmission in the thz band is investigated. the phase shifts at the ris that maximize the secrecy rate are obtained based on convex optimization. based on the current state of research, we evince that the design of ris-aided thz communication systems is still at an early development stage.

recently, the concept of holographic communication has been proposed as a new paradigm shift in mimo [34]–[36] and ris-aided [37] communications. one of the main features of holographic communications is the integration of very large numbers of tiny and inexpensive antennas or reconfigurable elements into a compact space in order to realize a holographic array with a spatially continuous aperture [34]–[37]. this holographic architecture is easier to realize in the thz band thanks to the miniaturization of thz electronic components. in [32], for example, the size of each graphene-based reflecting element is  $200 \mu \mathrm{m} \times 190 \mu \mathrm{m}$  at a carrier frequency of  $0.22 \mathrm{thz}$  that corresponds to a wavelength  $\lambda \approx 1360 \mu \mathrm{m}$ . therefore, the reflecting elements can be spaced more densely than  $\lambda / 2$  so as to form a spatially continuous surface [37], since the resulting surface is homogenizable [8]. this densely spaced or continuous implementation of riss is

referred to as holographic ris. channel modeling and data transmission schemes based on active holographic surfaces are investigated in [34] and [37], respectively. however, to the best of our knowledge, no current research works have tackled the physical layer transmission design of passive holographic communications, where nearly-passive holographic riss with spatially continuous apertures are deployed.

# b. paper contributions

in this work, we focus our attention on the analysis of holographic riss for application to massive mimo systems in the thz band. in particular, the main contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows:

- we derive the beam pattern of an ris made of discrete elements, and propose an angular-domain beamforming framework. by applying fourier analysis to the reflection coefficients of the elements at the ris, we prove that the beam pattern of an ris with discrete elements can be represented as a weighted integral of dirichlet kernel functions. on this basis, we propose an angular-domain beamforming framework. the weighting factors in the beam pattern are designed and the corresponding reflection coefficients of the ris are reconstructed via the fourier transform of the obtained weighting factors.

- we generalize the analysis and design to (continuous) holographic riss. based on the proposed beamforming framework, we derive and obtain closed-form solutions for the beamforming design in two important cases, i.e., narrow beam steering (nbs) and spatial bandpass filtering (sbf), which play an important role in ris-aided communication systems. we further extend these solutions to holographic riss, in which the elements are closely spaced so as to yield a virtually continuous spatial aperture. the results reveal that the beam pattern of an ideal holographic ris can be well approximated by an ultra-dense ris, which has a practical hardware architecture.

- we propose a closed-loop ce scheme to effectively estimate the broadband channels of thz massive mimo systems based on holographic riss. the proposed approach consists of downlink and uplink transmissions. in the downlink transmission, the holographic ris uses sbf beamforming so that the users can coarsely estimate the range of los angles. in the subsequent uplink transmission, the users with similar los angles are scheduled into the same group, and the coarsely-estimated los angles are exploited to design the uplink pilot signals for the finer-grained uplink ce. to further reduce the uplink pilot overhead, a cs-based ce scheme is introduced, where the dual sparsity of thz mimo channels in both the angular domain and delay domain is leveraged.

# ii. system description and channel model

in this section, we present the system model and the effective baseband channel model of the considered ris-aided thz massive mimo system over frequency-selective fading channels. we consider an ris-aided thz massive mimo system that operates in time division duplex (tdd) mode, as illustrated in fig. 1. the base station (bs) and user equipments (ues) are equipped with half-wavelength spaced uniform planar arrays (upas) that consist of  $m^{\mathrm{b}} = m_x^{\mathrm{b}} \times m_y^{\mathrm{b}}$  and  $m^{\mathrm{u}} = m_x^{\mathrm{u}} \times m_y^{\mathrm{u}}$  antennas, respectively, where  $m_x^{\mathrm{b}}$  ( $m_x^{\mathrm{u}}$ ) and  $m_y^{\mathrm{b}}$  ( $m_y^{\mathrm{u}}$ ) are the numbers of antennas along the azimuth and elevation directions, respectively. an ris of physical size  $a_x \times a_y$  is deployed to enhance the effective coverage of the bs. a low-power ris controller is connected to the ris and is operated by the bs through a control link. the bs carries out beamforming optimization and feeds the results to the ris controller through the control link so that the reflection coefficients of each reflecting element of the ris can be adjusted accordingly. as shown in fig. 1(a), we assume that the los link between the bs and the ue is blocked by an obstacle or by a human body. thus, the

in the following text, we first introduce the physical channel model that is widely considered in previous works on ris-aided mimo systems [28]-[32]. then, we introduce an effective baseband channel model by taking into account the beamforming design at the bs, the ris and the ue.

a) physical channel model with discrete riss: under the assumptions that the ris has  $n$  reflecting elements, the downlink spatial channel  $\mathbf{g} \in \mathbb{c}^{n \times m^{\mathrm{b}}}$  from the bs to the ris can be modeled as

$$
\mathbf {g} = \alpha^ {\mathrm {d l}} \mathbf {a} _ {\mathrm {r}} (\psi^ {\mathrm {r}}) \mathbf {a} _ {\mathrm {b}} ^ {h} (\psi^ {\mathrm {b}}), \tag {1}
$$

where  $\alpha^{\mathrm{dl}}$  is the channel coefficient,  $\psi^{\mathrm{b}} = [\psi_{\mathrm{bs}}^{\mathrm{azi}},\psi_{\mathrm{bs}}^{\mathrm{ele}}]^t$  and  $\psi^{\mathrm{r}} = [\psi_{\mathrm{ris}}^{\mathrm{azi}},\psi_{\mathrm{ris}}^{\mathrm{ele}}]^t$  are the los angle of departure (aod) and los angle of arrival (aoa) of the bs-ris channel, respectively.  $\psi^{\mathrm{b}}(\psi^{\mathrm{r}})$  includes both the azimuth part  $\psi_{\mathrm{bs}}^{\mathrm{azi}}(\psi_{\mathrm{ris}}^{\mathrm{azi}})$  and elevation part  $\psi_{\mathrm{bs}}^{\mathrm{ele}}(\psi_{\mathrm{ris}}^{\mathrm{ele}})$ , which are assumed to be fixed and known as detailed in previous text.  $\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{b}}(\psi^{\mathrm{b}})\in \mathbb{c}^{m^{\mathrm{b}}\times 1}$  and  $\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{r}}(\psi^{\mathrm{r}})\in \mathbb{c}^{n\times 1}$  denote the steering vectors at the bs and the ris, respectively.  $\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{b}}(\psi^{\mathrm{b}})$  is given by

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbf {a} _ {\mathrm {b}} \left(\boldsymbol {\psi} ^ {\mathrm {b}}\right) = \left[ 1, \dots , e ^ {- j d _ {\mathrm {u p a}} \left(m _ {x} \psi_ {\mathrm {b s}} ^ {\mathrm {a z i}} + m _ {y} \psi_ {\mathrm {b s}} ^ {\mathrm {e l e}}\right)}, \dots , \right. \\ \left. e ^ {- j d _ {\mathrm {u p a}} \left[ \left(m _ {x} ^ {\mathrm {b}} - 1\right) \psi_ {\mathrm {b s}} ^ {\mathrm {a z i}} + \left(m _ {y} ^ {\mathrm {b}} - 1\right) \psi_ {\mathrm {b s}} ^ {\mathrm {e l e}} \right]} \right] ^ {t}, \tag {2} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $d_{\mathrm{upa}} = \lambda /2$  is the element spacing of the upa at the bs, and  $0\leq m_x\leq (m_x^{\mathrm{b}} - 1)$ $0\leq m_y\leq (m_y^{\mathrm{b}} - 1)$ $\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{r}}(\psi^{\mathrm{r}})$  can be written by using a similar notation and assumptions.

as for the ris-ue spatial channel  $\mathbf{h} \in \mathbb{c}^{m^{\mathrm{u}} \times n}$ , we consider a rician fading channel model that consists of one los path and  $l$  nlos paths, as shown in fig. 1(b). in particular, we have

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbf {h} = \beta^ {\mathrm {d l , l o s}} \mathbf {a} _ {\mathrm {u}} \left(\boldsymbol {\nu} ^ {\mathrm {l o s}}\right) \mathbf {a} _ {\mathrm {r}} ^ {h} \left(\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {l o s}}\right) \\ + \frac {1}{\sqrt {l k _ {\mathrm {f}}}} \sum_ {l = 1} ^ {l} \beta_ {l} ^ {\mathrm {d l}} \mathbf {a} _ {\mathrm {u}} (\boldsymbol {\nu} _ {l}) \mathbf {a} _ {\mathrm {r}} ^ {h} (\boldsymbol {\mu} _ {l}), \tag {3} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\beta^{\mathrm{dl,los}}$  and  $\beta_l^{\mathrm{dl}}$  are the channel coefficients of the los component and the  $l$ -th nlos component, respectively,  $k_{\mathrm{f}}$  is the rician factor that denotes the ratio of the energy between the los and nlos channels,  $\mu^{\mathrm{los}}$  and  $\nu^{\mathrm{los}}$  are the los aod and the los aoa, respectively,  $\mu_l$  and  $\nu_l$  are the

nlos aod and the nlos aoa of the  $l$ -th nlos path. the steering vector at the ue  $\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{u}}(\cdot) \in \mathbb{c}^{m^{\mathrm{u}} \times 1}$  can be formulated similar to (2).

b) effective baseband channel model: the effective baseband channel is defined as the inner product between the analog beamforming vector at the transceiver and the steering vector of the physical channels. assuming that the  $r$ -th rfc  $(1 \leq r \leq n_{\mathrm{rf}})$  of the bs and the ue use the analog beamforming vectors  $\mathbf{f}_r \in \mathbb{c}^{m^{\mathrm{b}} \times 1}$  and  $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{c}^{m^{\mathrm{u}} \times 1}$ , respectively, and that the ris uses  $\varphi \in \mathbb{c}^{n \times 1}$  as the reflection coefficients, the effective baseband channel  $h_r^{\mathrm{dl,eff}}$  can be written as

$$
\begin{array}{l} h _ {r} ^ {\mathrm {d l , e f f}} = \mathbf {w} ^ {h} \mathbf {h} \boldsymbol {\phi} \mathbf {g} \mathbf {f} _ {r} \\ = \alpha^ {\mathrm {d l}} g _ {r} ^ {\mathrm {b}} (\psi^ {\mathrm {b}}) \left[ \beta^ {\mathrm {d l}, \mathrm {l o s}} g (\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {l o s}}, \boldsymbol {\psi} ^ {\mathrm {r}}) g ^ {\mathrm {u}} (\boldsymbol {\nu} ^ {\mathrm {l o s}}) \right. \\ \left. + \frac {1}{\sqrt {l k _ {\mathrm {f}}}} \sum_ {l = 1} ^ {l} \beta_ {l} ^ {\mathrm {d l}} g \left(\boldsymbol {\mu} _ {l}, \boldsymbol {\psi} ^ {\mathrm {r}}\right) g ^ {\mathrm {u}} \left(\boldsymbol {\nu} _ {l}\right) \right], \tag {4} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\phi = \mathrm{diag}(\varphi) \in \mathbb{c}^{n \times n}$ , and  $g_r^{\mathrm{b}}(\psi^{\mathrm{b}}) = \mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{b}}^h(\psi^{\mathrm{b}})\mathbf{f}_r$ ,  $g(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\psi}^{\mathrm{r}}) = \mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{r}}^h(\boldsymbol{\mu})\phi_{\mathrm{a}_{\mathrm{r}}}\left(\psi^{\mathrm{r}}\right)$ , and  $g^{\mathrm{u}}(\nu) = \mathbf{w}^h\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{u}}(\nu)$  are the beam patterns of the bs, the ris, and the ue, respectively.

the effective baseband channel model in (4) can be generalized to the case of frequency-selective channel. in a frequency-selective channel, in particular, the bs-ris-ue effective baseband channel in the delay domain can be formulated as

$$
h _ {r} ^ {\mathrm {d l}} (\tau) = \underbrace {\alpha^ {\mathrm {d l}} g _ {r} ^ {\mathrm {b}} \left(\psi^ {\mathrm {b}}\right)} _ {\text {b s t o r i s}} \underbrace {\left[ h ^ {\mathrm {d l} , \mathrm {l o s}} (\tau) + h ^ {\mathrm {d l} , \mathrm {n l o s}} (\tau) \right]} _ {\text {r i s t o u e}}, \tag {5}
$$

where

$$
h ^ {\mathrm {d l}, \mathrm {l o s}} (\tau) = \beta^ {\mathrm {d l}, \mathrm {l o s}} g \left(\boldsymbol {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {l o s}}, \psi^ {\mathrm {r}}\right) g ^ {\mathrm {u}} \left(\boldsymbol {\nu} ^ {\mathrm {l o s}}\right) p \left(\tau - \tau^ {\mathrm {d l}, \mathrm {l o s}}\right), \tag {6}
$$

and

$$
h ^ {\mathrm {d l}, \mathrm {n l o s}} (\tau) = \frac {1}{\sqrt {l k _ {\mathrm {f}}}} \sum_ {l = 1} ^ {l} \beta_ {l} ^ {\mathrm {d l}} g \left(\boldsymbol {\mu} _ {l}, \boldsymbol {\psi} ^ {\mathrm {r}}\right) g ^ {\mathrm {u}} \left(\boldsymbol {\nu} _ {l}\right) p \left(\tau - \tau_ {l} ^ {\mathrm {d l}}\right). \tag {7}
$$

in (6) and (7),  $\tau^{\mathrm{dl},\mathrm{los}}$  and  $\tau_l^{\mathrm{dl}}$  denote the delay offsets corresponding to the los path and the  $l$ -th nlos path, respectively, and  $p(\tau)$  is the pulse shaping filter function.

it is worth noting that, in contrast with riss made of discrete elements [20]–[24], [26]–[32], a holographic ris is modeled as an array with a spatially continuous aperture [37] (i.e.,  $n \to \infty$ ). the physical channels associated with a holographic ris cannot be represented in terms of the finite-dimensional matrices in (1) and (3). therefore, in this paper, we utilize the effective baseband channel model in (5)-(7) to describe the channels associated with a holographic ris. the beam patterns in (4) are elaborated in detail in the next section.

since we consider that the system operates in tdd mode, we assume that the channel reciprocity between the uplink and downlink transmissions holds. in particular, the uplink channels can be modeled based on the downlink channels reported in previous text. we omit the details of the uplink channel model for brevity.

distinct differences between the thz and the mmwave bands, as detailed in the following text, may cause different settings of channel parameters and thus deserve more attention. first, the atmospheric attenuation caused by molecular absorption [1], [2] becomes non-negligible in the thz band, thus introducing an extra path loss for link budget. second, thz signals with an extremely short wavelength are likely to undergo diffuse scattering on the scattering surface [3]. this results in a limited number of effective nlos paths (e.g.,  $l = 1$ ) and a large rician factor (e.g.  $k_{\mathrm{f}} = 30$  db). third, the thz are more vulnerable to the presence of blockages, which may result in weak los directive paths. these factors motivate the deployment of riss to provide strong virtual los links for thz communications in the absence of reliable los/nlos links. in addition, due to the large number of reflecting elements, which yield a better controllability of the radio signals and a high beamforming gain, holographic-based riss provide further advantages compared with their non-holographic counterpart. this is further discussed next.

# iii. beamforming design for holegraphic riss

to complete the formulation of the effective baseband channel model in (5)-(7), we first derive and analyze the beam pattern of riss based on discrete planar arrays (dpas). then, we extend the results to riss with spatially continuous apertures, which are referred to as continuous metasurfaces (cmss). in addition, we derive closed-form beamforming solutions in two important cases, i.e., narrow beam steering and spatial bandpass filtering, which both play an essential role in the proposed ce scheme for ris-aided thz massive mimo systems.

# a. riss based on discrete planar arrays

as shown in fig. 2(a) and (b), a dpa-based ris placed on the  $x - y$  plan consists of numerous evenly-spaced reflecting elements. the number of elements along the  $x$ - and  $y$ -directions is  $n_{x} \in \mathbb{z}$  and  $n_{y} \in \mathbb{z}$ , respectively. the distance between two adjacent elements is  $d$ , and we assume  $d \leq \lambda / 2$  due to the nyquist sampling theorem. we define the total physical size of a dpa-based ris as  $a_{x} \times a_{y}$  with  $a_{x} = n_{x} d$  and  $a_{y} = n_{y} d$ , and assume that  $a_{x}$  and  $a_{y}$  remain unchanged unless stated otherwise. let  $(x_{m}, y_{n})$  be the coordinate of the  $(m, n)$ -th reflecting element, then we have

$$
x _ {m} = (m - 1) d, y _ {n} = (n - 1) d, \tag {8}
$$

where  $1 \leq m \leq n_x$ ,  $1 \leq n \leq n_y$ . assume that a narrowband reference signal "1" impinges on the ris with an azimuth aoa  $\theta_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{azi}} \in [0,2\pi)$  and an elevation aoa  $\theta_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{ele}} \in [0,\pi /2]$  (defined in fig. 2(a)). the phase-difference of the incident signal at the  $(m,n)$ -th element (compared to the reference point  $(0,0)$ ) can be written as

$$
\begin{array}{l} a \left(x _ {m}, y _ {n}; \psi_ {\text {i n}}\right) = e ^ {j \frac {2 \pi}{\lambda} \mathbf {p} ^ {t} \mathbf {r}} \\ = e ^ {j \frac {2 \pi}{\lambda} \left(x _ {m} \cos \theta_ {\mathrm {i n}} ^ {\mathrm {a z i}} \sin \theta_ {\mathrm {i n}} ^ {\mathrm {e l e}} + y _ {n} \sin \theta_ {\mathrm {i n}} ^ {\mathrm {a z i}} \sin \theta_ {\mathrm {i n}} ^ {\mathrm {e l e}}\right)} \\ = e ^ {j \left(x _ {m} \psi_ {\mathrm {i n}} ^ {\mathrm {a z i}} + y _ {n} \psi_ {\mathrm {i n}} ^ {\mathrm {e l e}}\right)}, \tag {9} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\mathbf{p} = [x_n,y_m,0]^t$  is the position vector of the  $(m,n)$  th element,  $\mathbf{r} = [\cos \theta_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{azi}}\sin \theta_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{ele}},\sin \theta_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{azi}}\sin \theta_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{ele}},\cos \theta_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{ele}}]^t$  is the vector of the incident direction, and  $\psi_{\mathrm{in}}\triangleq [\psi_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{azi}},\psi_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{ele}}]^t$  with  $\psi_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{azi}}\triangleq \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\cos \theta_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{azi}}\sin \theta_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{ele}},\psi_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{ele}}\triangleq \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\sin \theta_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{azi}}\sin \theta_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{ele}}$  is a 2-tuple variable accounting for the aoa of the incident signal. similarly, for the aod denoted by  $\theta_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{azi}}$  and  $\theta_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{ele}}$  we define  $\psi_{\mathrm{out}}\triangleq [\psi_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{azi}},\psi_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{ele}}]^t$  with  $\psi_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{azi}}\triangleq \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\cos \theta_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{azi}}\sin \theta_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{ele}},$ $\psi_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{ele}}\triangleq \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\sin \theta_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{azi}}\sin \theta_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{ele}}.$  the definitions of  $\psi_{\mathrm{in}}$  and  $\psi_{\mathrm{out}}$  simplify the notation. the received signal along the direction of observation (i.e., reflection)  $\psi_{\mathrm{out}}$  after the incident signal is reflected by the ris is denoted by  $g(\psi_{\mathrm{out}},\psi_{\mathrm{in}})$ , which is given by the superposition of the signals reflected by all the individual elements of the ris

$$
\begin{array}{l} g \left(\psi_ {\text {o u t}}, \psi_ {\text {i n}}\right) = \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {n _ {x}} \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {n _ {y}} a ^ {*} \left(x _ {m}, y _ {n}; \psi_ {\text {o u t}}\right) \phi (m, n) a \left(x _ {m}, y _ {n}; \psi_ {\text {i n}}\right) \\ = \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {n _ {x}} \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {n _ {y}} \phi (m, n) e ^ {- j \left(x _ {m} \delta_ {x} + y _ {n} \delta_ {y}\right)}, \tag {10} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\delta_x = \psi_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{azi}} - \psi_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{azi}}$ ,  $\delta_y = \psi_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{ele}} - \psi_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{ele}}$ , and  $\phi(m,n) \in \mathbb{c}$  is the reflection coefficient of the  $(m,n)$ -th element of the ris, whose amplitude and phase are software-programmable via the ris controller.

we refer to  $g(\psi_{\mathrm{out}}, \psi_{\mathrm{in}})$  as the beam pattern of the dpa-based ris, which is consistent with the definition in (4). the amplitude of  $g(\psi_{\mathrm{out}}, \psi_{\mathrm{in}})$  can be used to evaluate the intensity of the signal along the direction  $\psi_{\mathrm{out}}$  after reflection from the ris. in general terms,  $\phi(m,n)$  can be treated as a two-dimensional discrete signal in the spatial domain

<sup>1</sup>we assume that  $\psi_{\mathrm{in}} = \left[\psi_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{azi}},\psi_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{ele}}\right]^t$  is known and is treated as a constant in the beamforming design. this follows from the assumption that the los angles of the bs-ris channel are fixed and known.

$$
\begin{array}{l} g \left(\psi_ {\text {o u t}}, \psi_ {\text {i n}}\right) = \left(\frac {d}{2 \pi}\right) ^ {2} e ^ {- j \left(x _ {m} \delta_ {x} + y _ {n} \delta_ {y}\right)} \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {n _ {x}} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {n _ {y}} \int_ {\frac {2 \pi}{d}} \int_ {\frac {2 \pi}{d}} \omega (k, l) e ^ {j (d m k + d n l)} \mathrm {d} k \mathrm {d} l \\ = \left(\frac {d}{2 \pi}\right) ^ {2} \int_ {\frac {2 \pi}{d}} \int_ {\frac {2 \pi}{d}} \omega (k, l) \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {n _ {x}} e ^ {j (d m k - x _ {m} \delta_ {x})} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {n _ {y}} e ^ {j (d n l - y _ {n} \delta_ {y})} d k d l \\ = \frac {a _ {x} a _ {y}}{4 \pi^ {2}} e ^ {j \frac {d - a _ {x}}{2} \delta_ {x}} e ^ {j \frac {d - a _ {y}}{2} \delta_ {y}} \int_ {\frac {2 \pi}{d}} \int_ {\frac {2 \pi}{d}} \omega^ {\prime} (k, l) \xi_ {n _ {x}} [ d (k - \delta_ {x}) ] \xi_ {n _ {y}} [ d (l - \delta_ {y}) ] \mathrm {d} k \mathrm {d} l, \tag {13} \\ \end{array}
$$

with spatial sampling period equal to  $d$  in both the azimuth and elevation directions. by using the discrete-time fourier transform (dtft),  $\phi(m,n)$  can be represented as

$$
\phi (m, n) = \left(\frac {d}{2 \pi}\right) ^ {2} \int_ {\frac {2 \pi}{d}} \int_ {\frac {2 \pi}{d}} \omega (k, l) e ^ {j (d m k + d n l)} \mathrm {d} k \mathrm {d} l, \tag {11}
$$

where

$$
\omega (k, l) = \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {n _ {x}} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {n _ {y}} \phi (m, n) e ^ {- j (d m k + d n l)} \tag {12}
$$

is a 2-dimensional periodic function whose period is  $\frac{2\pi}{d}$  with respect to both  $k$  and  $l$ , and  $\int_{\frac{2\pi}{d}} (\cdot) \mathrm{d}(\cdot)$  denotes the integral in an arbitrary interval of length  $\frac{2\pi}{d}$ . substituting (11) into (10), we obtain (13), as shown at the top of the next page, where

$$
\omega^ {\prime} (k, l) = \omega (k, l) e ^ {j \frac {n _ {x} + 1}{2} d k} e ^ {j \frac {n _ {y} + 1}{2} d l}. \tag {14}
$$

equation (13) reveals that the beam pattern  $g(\psi_{\mathrm{out}}, \psi_{\mathrm{in}})$  can be formulated as a weighted integral of dirichlet kernel functions  $\xi(\cdot)$  [38] whose weighting factors are  $\omega'(k, l)$ . this is a generalization of the far-field results in [41]. equations (11)-(13) shed light on the design of angular-domain beamforming for dpa-based ris. in particular, the proposed design is based on two steps: (i) first,  $\omega'(k, l)$  in (13) is optimized in order to design  $g(\psi_{\mathrm{out}}, \psi_{\mathrm{in}})$  that corresponds to the desired direction of observation; and (ii) then, the corresponding reflection coefficients  $\phi(m, n)$  are reconstructed via (11). in the next sub-section, we further explain this procedure by providing the optimal beamforming for two specific cases.

# b. beamforming design for dpa-based ris

in this sub-section, we describe the following two use cases for beamforming design based on the proposed approach.

a) narrow beam steering. given the desired beamforming direction  $\psi_{\mathrm{opt}} = \left[\psi_{\mathrm{opt}}^{\mathrm{azi}},\psi_{\mathrm{opt}}^{\mathrm{ele}}\right]^t$  , the target of nbs is to maximize  $|g(\psi_{\mathrm{out}},\psi_{\mathrm{in}})|$  for  $\psi_{\mathrm{out}} = \psi_{\mathrm{opt}}$  , and to minimize (null)  $|g(\psi_{\mathrm{out}},\psi_{\mathrm{in}})|$  for  $\psi_{\mathrm{out}}\neq \psi_{\mathrm{opt}}$  . since  $|\xi_{n_x}[d(k - \delta_x)]|$  and  $\left|\xi_{ny}\left[d(l - \delta_y)\right]\right|$  attain their maximum if and only if  $k = \frac{2\pi u}{d} +\delta_x$ $u\in \mathbb{z}$  and  $l = \frac{2\pi v}{d} +\delta_y$ $v\in \mathbb{z}$  , respectively, we can impose the following design for the angular-domain coefficients of nbs

$$
\omega_ {\mathrm {n b s}} (k, l; \psi_ {\text {o p t}}) \propto \sum_ {u \in \mathbb {z}} \delta (k - k _ {\text {o p t}, u}) \sum_ {v \in \mathbb {z}} \delta (l - l _ {\text {o p t}, v}), \tag {15}
$$

where  $k_{\mathrm{opt},u} = \frac{2\pi u}{d} + (\psi_{\mathrm{opt}}^{\mathrm{azi}} - \psi_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{azi}})$  and  $l_{\mathrm{opt},v} = \frac{2\pi v}{d} + (\psi_{\mathrm{opt}}^{\mathrm{ele}} - \psi_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{ele}})$ . it can be readily verified that (15) ensures

the required periodicity of  $\frac{2\pi}{d}$  with respect to both  $k$  and  $l$ . the corresponding beam pattern for nbs can be obtained by substituting (15) into (13) and (14), which yields

$$
\begin{array}{l} g _ {\text {n b s}} \left(\psi_ {\text {o u t}}, \psi_ {\text {i n}}; \psi_ {\text {o p t}}\right) \propto e ^ {j \frac {d - a _ {x}}{2} \delta_ {x}} e ^ {j \frac {d - a _ {y}}{2} \delta_ {y}} \\ \times \xi_ {n _ {x}} \left[ d \left(k _ {\mathrm {o p t}} - \delta_ {x}\right) \right] \xi_ {n _ {y}} \left[ d \left(l _ {\mathrm {o p t}} - \delta_ {y}\right) \right], \tag {16} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $k_{\mathrm{opt}} \triangleq k_{\mathrm{opt,0}}$  and  $l_{\mathrm{opt}} \triangleq l_{\mathrm{opt,0}}$ , since in (13) we choose the integral intervals containing  $k_{\mathrm{opt,0}}$  and  $l_{\mathrm{opt,0}}$  for  $k$  and  $l$ , respectively. from (11), the reflection coefficients for nbs can be formulated as follows

$$
\phi_ {\mathrm {n b s}} (m, n; \psi_ {\mathrm {o p t}}) \propto e ^ {j (d m k _ {\mathrm {o p t}} + d n l _ {\mathrm {o p t}})}. \tag {17}
$$

this nbs design results in a narrowest beam pattern towards a certain transmission direction  $\psi_{\mathrm{opt}}$ , and thus provides the optimal beamforming gain in the point-to-point communications.

b) spatial bandpass filtering. as far as sbf is concerned, we aim to design  $|g(\psi_{\mathrm{out}}, \psi_{\mathrm{in}})|$  to be quasi-constant for  $\psi_{\mathrm{min}}^{\mathrm{azi}} \leq \psi_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{azi}} \leq \theta_{\mathrm{max}}^{\mathrm{azi}}$  and  $\psi_{\mathrm{min}}^{\mathrm{ele}} \leq \psi_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{ele}} \leq \theta_{\mathrm{max}}^{\mathrm{ele}}$ , and to be almost zero, i.e.,  $|g(\psi_{\mathrm{out}}, \psi_{\mathrm{in}})| \approx 0$  otherwise, where  $\psi_{\mathrm{min}} = [\psi_{\mathrm{min}}^{\mathrm{azi}}, \psi_{\mathrm{min}}^{\mathrm{ele}}]^t$  and  $\psi_{\mathrm{max}} = [\psi_{\mathrm{max}}^{\mathrm{azi}}, \psi_{\mathrm{max}}^{\mathrm{ele}}]^t$  are referred to as the cut-off angles. to clearly explain the design of sbf, we first decompose the values of  $k$  in one period as follows

$$
k _ {\min } \leq k \leq k _ {\max }, \tag {18}
$$

$$
a <   k <   k _ {\min } \text {o r} k _ {\max } <   k \leq a + \frac {2 \pi}{d}, \tag {19}
$$

where  $k_{\mathrm{min}} = \psi_{\mathrm{min}}^{\mathrm{azi}} - \psi_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{azi}}$ ,  $k_{\mathrm{max}} = \psi_{\mathrm{max}}^{\mathrm{azi}} - \psi_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{azi}}$ , and  $a \in \mathbb{r}$  is an arbitrary number that satisfies the constraints  $a < k_{\mathrm{min}}$  and  $a + \frac{2\pi}{d} > k_{\mathrm{max}}$ . since we assume  $d \leq \lambda / 2$ , the existence of  $a$  is guaranteed. similarly, the values of  $l$  in one period can be decomposed as follows

$$
l _ {\min } \leq l \leq l _ {\max }, \tag {20}
$$

$$
b <   l <   l _ {\min } \text {o r} l _ {\max } <   l \leq b + \frac {2 \pi}{d}, \tag {21}
$$

where  $l_{\mathrm{min}} = \psi_{\mathrm{min}}^{\mathrm{ele}} - \psi_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{ele}}$ ,  $l_{\mathrm{max}} = \psi_{\mathrm{max}}^{\mathrm{ele}} - \psi_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{ele}}$ , and  $b \in \mathbb{r}$  is an arbitrary number that satisfies the constraints  $b < l_{\mathrm{min}}$  and  $b + \frac{2\pi}{d} > l_{\mathrm{max}}$ .

the angular-domain coefficients for sbf (in one period of  $k$  and  $l$ ) can be designed as follows

$$
\begin{array}{l} \omega_ {\mathrm {s b f}} \left(k, l; \psi_ {\min}, \psi_ {\max}\right) \\ \propto \left\{ \begin{array}{c c} e ^ {- j \frac {n _ {x} + 1}{2} d k} e ^ {- j \frac {n _ {y} + 1}{2} d l}, & \text {f o r (1 8) a n d (2 0)} \\ 0, & \text {f o r (1 9) a n d (2 1)} \end{array} \right. \tag {22} \\ \end{array}
$$

![](images/0751d365f9c9ad566231d2df1972b00f857b487c4f8c6078890bb1425323371c.jpg)  
fig. 3. an example of beam pattern of nbs based on (16).  $n_{x} = n_{y} = 64$ ,  $d = \lambda /2$ . the desired angle is  $\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\psi_{\mathrm{opt}} = [0.6, -0.2]^{t}$ .

substituting (22) into (13) and (14), and choosing the intervals of integration equal to  $\left(a, a + \frac{2\pi}{d}\right]$  for  $k$  and equal to  $\left(b, b + \frac{2\pi}{d}\right]$  for  $l$ , we obtain the following beam pattern

$$
\begin{array}{l} g _ {\mathrm {s b f}} \left(\psi_ {\text {o u t}}, \psi_ {\text {i n}}; \psi_ {\text {m i n}}, \psi_ {\text {m a x}}\right) \propto e ^ {j \frac {d - a _ {x}}{2} \delta_ {x}} e ^ {j \frac {d - a _ {y}}{2} \delta_ {y}} \\ \times \int_ {l _ {\min }} ^ {l _ {\max }} \int_ {k _ {\min }} ^ {k _ {\max }} \xi_ {n _ {x}} \left[ d \left(k - \delta_ {x}\right) \right] \xi_ {n _ {y}} \left[ d \left(l - \delta_ {y}\right) \right] \mathrm {d} k \mathrm {d} l. \tag {23} \\ \end{array}
$$

the corresponding reflection coefficients can be calculated from (11), which yields

$$
\begin{array}{l} \phi_ {\mathrm {s b f}} (m, n; \psi_ {\min }, \psi_ {\max }) \\ \propto \int_ {l _ {\min }} ^ {l _ {\max }} \int_ {k _ {\min }} ^ {k _ {\max }} e ^ {- j \frac {n _ {x} + 1}{2} d k} e ^ {- j \frac {n _ {y} + 1}{2} d l} e ^ {j (d m k + d n l)} d k d l \\ = e ^ {- j d \left(\bar {m} \psi_ {\mathrm {i n}} ^ {\mathrm {a z i}} + \bar {n} \psi_ {\mathrm {i n}} ^ {\mathrm {e l e}}\right)} \frac {e ^ {j d \bar {m} \psi_ {\max} ^ {\mathrm {a z i}}} - e ^ {j d \bar {m} \psi_ {\min} ^ {\mathrm {a z i}}}}{d \bar {m}} \frac {e ^ {j d \bar {n} \psi_ {\max} ^ {\mathrm {e l e}}} - e ^ {j d \bar {n} \psi_ {\min} ^ {\mathrm {e l e}}}}{d \bar {n}} \tag {24} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\bar{m} = m - \frac{n_x + 1}{2}$  and  $\bar{n} = n - \frac{n_y + 1}{2}$ .

to validate the proposed designs, we show two realizations of  $|g_{\mathrm{nbs}}(\psi_{\mathrm{out}}, \psi_{\mathrm{in}}; \psi_{\mathrm{opt}})|$  and  $|g_{\mathrm{sbf}}(\psi_{\mathrm{out}}, \psi_{\mathrm{in}}; \psi_{\mathrm{min}}, \psi_{\mathrm{max}})|$  with normalized amplitudes in fig. 3 and fig. 4, respectively. it can be observed that the obtained beam patterns based on the proposed beamforming framework well fulfill the desired design. it is noteworthy that the reflection coefficients in (17) and (24) are given in closed-form and are physically realizable. by direct inspection of fig. 3 and fig. 4, we observe that (i) the nbs design criterion allows one to obtain a small focused region, which can be useful for beamforming applications; and (ii) the sbf design criterion allows one to obtain a wide focused region, which can be useful for broadcasting applications.

# c. extension to holographic riss

as far as the design of dpa-based riss is concerned, we have considered an element spacing equal to  $d = \lambda / 2$ , which is a critical spacing based on the nyquist sampling theorem. this assumption has been, implicitly or explicitly, adopted in previous research works [20]–[24], [26]–[32]. however, the design and optimization of riss based on elements spaced

![](images/91cd43496a72f9a9db226cf4b57b00b963e22c8817791a40940cc19944e4810a.jpg)  
fig. 4. an example of beam pattern of sbf based on (23).  $n_{x} = n_{y} = 64$ ,  $d = \lambda / 2$ . the cut-off angles are  $\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\psi_{\mathrm{min}} = [-0.2, 0.2]^t$  and  $\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\psi_{\mathrm{max}} = [0.2, 0.6]^t$ .

at the critical distance has some inherent drawbacks. in particular, (i) due to the periodicity and the non-negligible sidelobes of the dirichlet kernel functions, a power leakage phenomenon [39] is usually observed, which may result in inter-beam interference; (ii) the energy received and reflected by the ris highly depends on its effective reflection area [41]. more specifically, the use of critically-spaced riss usually degrades the effective reflection area, which may result in a reduced energy efficiency.

since the physical size of an ris is limited by several practical factors, an effective solution to overcome the just mentioned drawbacks is to increase the number of reflecting elements and to reduce their spacing  $(d < \lambda /2)$  while keeping  $a_{x}$  and  $a_{y}$  unchanged, as illustrated in fig. 2(b). a dpa-based ris whose elements have an element spacing  $d < \lambda /2$  is referred to as ultra-dense ris. it is worth mentioning that the analysis reported in the previous sub-section can be applied to critically-spaced and ultra-dense riss by appropriately choosing the value of  $d$ . in figs. 5 and 6, to elucidate these aspects, we report the beam patterns of dpa-based riss for different values of  $d$  (only the beam patterns along the azimuth direction, i.e., with respect to  $\psi_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{azi}}$ , are reported for ease of illustration). it can be observed that the beam patterns of an ultra-dense ris with  $d = \{\lambda /4,\lambda /8\}$  are quite similar to those of a critically-spaced ris  $(d = \lambda /2)$ , but the power leakage is suppressed. by increasing the number of reflecting elements, more importantly, the effective reflection area of an ultra-dense ris is expected to increase as compared with critically-spaced ris, and thus a larger fraction of the energy of the incident em signal can be steered towards the desired direction. this point is elaborated in detail in section v, where the numerical results are presented.

the performance improvement from critically-spaced riss to ultra-dense riss naturally motivates us to ask: what is the performance of dpa-based riss in the asymptotic regime  $d \to 0$  and  $n_x, n_y \to \infty$  (while keeping  $n_x d = a_x$  and  $n_y d = a_y$  fixed)? an ris that is obtained by letting  $d \to 0$  is referred to as cms. in the following text, we show that the beam patterns and the design of the reflection coefficients of a cms can be obtained by extending the analysis of dpa-based riss.

\tilde {\phi} (x, y) \propto \lim  _ {d \rightarrow 0} \phi (m, n) | _ {d m = x, d n = y}, x \in [ 0, a _ {x} ], y \in [ 0, a _ {y} ] \tag {25}

$$

# iv. closed-loop channel estimation scheme

# b. uplink ce stage

after the downlink ce stage, each ue obtains the indices of the optimal groups  $(\hat{g}_x,\hat{g}_y,\hat{n}_x,\hat{n}_y)$ , and this information is fed back to the bs via the control links for ue scheduling. specifically, the bs schedules the ues having the same  $(\hat{g}_x,\hat{g}_y)$  into the same uplink group. each scheduled group performs the finer-grained uplink ce and the subsequent payload data transmission. in this sub-section, therefore, we consider a generic group as follows

$\mathcal{g}_{\hat{g}_x,\hat{g}_y} = \{\mathrm{all~the~ues~that~have~the~same}$

$$
\left(\hat {g} _ {x}, \hat {g} _ {y}\right) \text {o b t a i n e d} \left. \left. \left(\hat {g} _ {x}, \hat {g} _ {y}\right) \right\} \right., \tag {41}
$$

and we assume  $\mathrm{card}(\mathcal{g}_{\hat{g}_x,\hat{g}_y}) = n_{\mathrm{ue}}$ . during the uplink ce and payload data transmission stages, the beamforming design at the  $u$ -th ue ( $1 \leq u \leq n_{\mathrm{ue}}$ ) in  $\mathcal{g}_{\hat{g}_x,\hat{g}_y}$  is chosen as follows

$$

$$

$$

where  $n_{\mathrm{used}} \triangleq n_{\mathrm{cp}} / n_{\mathrm{ue}}$  ( $n_{\mathrm{used}} \in \mathbb{z}$  without loss of generality),  $\mathcal{k}_u \subseteq \{1, \dots, n_{\mathrm{cp}}\}$ ,  $\operatorname{card}(\mathcal{k}_u) = n_{\mathrm{used}}$ , and  $\mathcal{k}_u \cap \mathcal{k}_{u'} = \emptyset$  for  $\forall u \neq u'$ . during the uplink ce stage, the  $u$ -th ue transmits its own pilot signals by using only the  $n_{\mathrm{used}}$  subcarriers indexed by  $\mathcal{k}_u$  out of the  $n_{\mathrm{cp}}$  available subcarriers. on the other hand, no signals are transmitted in other subcarriers. this makes easier to separate the pilot signals of different ues at the bss, since different dscs are used. three examples of the possible structure of an uw with dscs are illustrated in fig. 8.

taking into account the dsc allocation above, the uplink channel in the delay domain (47) can be transformed to the frequency domain, which is similar to (38). in particular, the frequency-domain representation of the channel related to the  $u$ -th ue corresponding to its  $n$ -th dsc in the  $i$ -th time slot can be written as

$$
h _ {i, u, n} ^ {\mathrm {u l}, \mathrm {f d}} = \frac {1}{\sqrt {n _ {\mathrm {c p}}}} \sum_ {d = 0} ^ {n _ {\mathrm {c p}} - 1} h _ {i, u} ^ {\mathrm {u l}} (d t _ {\mathrm {s}}) e ^ {- j \frac {2 \pi}{n _ {\mathrm {c p}}} k _ {u, n}} = \mathbf {w} _ {i} ^ {t} \mathbf {h} _ {u} ^ {\mathrm {a d d d}} \mathbf {f} _ {k _ {u, n}}, \tag {52}
$$

where  $\mathbf{f}_{k_{u,n}}$  is the  $k_{u,n}$ -th column vector of the  $n_{\mathrm{cp}} \times n_{\mathrm{cp}}$  dft matrix  $\mathbf{f}_{n_{\mathrm{cp}}}$ , and

$$
\mathbf {h} _ {u} ^ {\mathrm {a d d d}} = \left[ \mathbf {h} _ {1}, \dots , \mathbf {h} _ {n _ {\mathrm {c p}}} \right] \in \mathbb {c} ^ {b _ {x} b _ {y} \times n _ {\mathrm {c p}}} \tag {53}
$$

with  $\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{h}_u^{\mathrm{ul},\mathrm{los}}p[(t - 1)t_{\mathrm{s}} - \tau_u^{\mathrm{ul},\mathrm{los}}] + \sum_{l = 1}^{l}\mathbf{h}_{u,l}^{\mathrm{ul},\mathrm{nlos}}p[(t - 1)t_{\mathrm{s}} - \tau_{u,l}^{\mathrm{ul}}], t = 1,..,n_{\mathrm{cp}},$  being the effective angular-domain and delay-domain (addd) uplink channel to be estimated.

we assume that each ue transmits the pilot signal  $\sqrt{\frac{p_{\mathrm{tx}}^{\mathrm{ul}}}{n_{\mathrm{used}}}}$  over its dscs during the uplink ce stage, where  $p_{\mathrm{tx}}^{\mathrm{ul}}$  is the total transmit power of the ue. the received pilot signal in the  $i$ -th time slot at the bs can be expressed as

$y_{i,u,n} = \sqrt{\frac{p_{\mathrm{tx}}^{\mathrm{ul}}}{n_{\mathrm{used}}}} h_{i,u,n}^{\mathrm{ul,fd}} + n_{i,u,n} = \sqrt{\frac{p_{\mathrm{tx}}^{\mathrm{ul}}}{n_{\mathrm{used}}}}\mathbf{w}_i^t\mathbf{h}_u^{\mathrm{addd}}\mathbf{f}_{k_{u,n}} + n_{i,u,n},$  where  $n_{i,u,n}\sim \mathcal{cn}(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^{2})$  is the awgn. by collecting the received pilot signals of all the dscs for the  $u$  -th ue  $\{\mathbf{y}_{i,u,n}\}_{n = 1}^{n_{\mathrm{used}}}$  , we have

$$
\mathbf {y} _ {i, u} ^ {t} = \sqrt {\frac {p _ {\mathrm {t x}} ^ {\mathrm {u l}}}{n _ {\mathrm {u s e d}}}} \mathbf {w} _ {i} ^ {t} \mathbf {h} _ {u} ^ {\mathrm {a d d d}} \mathbf {f} _ {u} + \mathbf {n} _ {i, u} ^ {t}, \tag {55}
$$

where  $\mathbf{y}_{i,u} = [y_{i,u,1},\dots,y_{i,u,n_{\mathrm{used}}}]^t\in \mathbb{c}^{n_{\mathrm{used}}\times 1}$ ,  $\mathbf{n}_{i,u} = [n_{i,u,1},\dots,n_{i,u,n_{\mathrm{used}}}]^t\in \mathbb{c}^{n_{\mathrm{used}}\times 1}$ , and  $\mathbf{f}_u = [\mathbf{f}_{n_{\mathrm{cp}}}]_{\mathcal{k}_u}\in$ $\mathbb{c}^{n_{\mathrm{cp}}\times n_{\mathrm{used}}}$  is the partial dft matrix. for  $n_{\mathrm{p}}$  successive time slots, we aggregate the channel observations of the  $u$ -th ue  $\{\mathbf{y}_{i,u}^t\}_{i = 1}^{n_{\mathrm{p}}}$  into the matrix  $\mathbf{y}_u\in \mathbb{c}^{n_{\mathrm{p}}\times n_{\mathrm{used}}}$ , which can be formulated as follows

$$
\mathbf {y} _ {u} = \left[ \mathbf {y} _ {1, u}, \dots , \mathbf {y} _ {n _ {\mathrm {p}}, u} \right] ^ {t} = \mathbf {w} \mathbf {h} _ {u} ^ {\mathrm {a d d d}} \mathbf {f} _ {u} + \mathbf {n} _ {u}, \tag {56}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} h _ {i, u} ^ {\mathrm {u l}, \mathrm {l o s}} (\tau) = \alpha^ {\mathrm {u l}} \beta^ {\mathrm {u l}, \mathrm {l o s}} g ^ {\mathrm {b}} \sum_ {b _ {x} = 1} ^ {b _ {x}} \sum_ {b _ {y} = 1} ^ {b _ {y}} \frac {e ^ {j \theta_ {i , b _ {x} , b _ {y}}}}{\sqrt {b _ {x} b _ {y}}} \tilde {g} _ {\mathrm {n b s}} \left(\boldsymbol {\mu} _ {u} ^ {\mathrm {l o s}}, \boldsymbol {\psi} ^ {\mathrm {r}}; \boldsymbol {\zeta} _ {b _ {x}, b _ {y}}\right) g _ {\mathrm {n b s}} ^ {\mathrm {u}} \left(\boldsymbol {\nu} _ {u} ^ {\mathrm {l o s}}; \boldsymbol {\psi} _ {\text {o p t}, \hat {n} _ {x} ^ {u}, \hat {n} _ {y} ^ {u}}\right) p \left(\tau - \tau_ {u} ^ {\mathrm {u l}, \mathrm {l o s}}\right) \\ = \mathbf {w} _ {i} ^ {t} \mathbf {h} _ {u} ^ {\mathrm {u l}, \mathrm {l o s}} p \left(\tau - \tau_ {u} ^ {\mathrm {u l}, \mathrm {l o s}}\right), \tag {48} \\ \end{array}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbf {h} _ {u} ^ {\mathrm {u l}, \mathrm {l o s}} = \alpha^ {\mathrm {u l}} \beta^ {\mathrm {u l}, \mathrm {l o s}} g ^ {\mathrm {b}} g _ {\mathrm {n b s}} ^ {\mathrm {u}} \left(\boldsymbol {\nu} _ {u} ^ {\mathrm {l o s}}; \boldsymbol {\psi} _ {\mathrm {o p t}, \hat {n} _ {x} ^ {u}, \hat {n} _ {y} ^ {u}}\right) \times \\ \left[ \tilde {g} _ {\mathrm {n b s}} \left(\boldsymbol {\mu} _ {u} ^ {\text {l o s}}, \boldsymbol {\psi} ^ {\mathrm {r}}; \boldsymbol {\zeta} _ {1, 1}\right), \dots , \tilde {g} _ {\mathrm {n b s}} \left(\boldsymbol {\mu} _ {u} ^ {\text {l o s}}, \boldsymbol {\psi} ^ {\mathrm {r}}; \boldsymbol {\zeta} _ {b _ {x}, b _ {y}}\right), \dots , \tilde {g} _ {\mathrm {n b s}} \left(\boldsymbol {\mu} _ {u} ^ {\text {l o s}}, \boldsymbol {\psi} ^ {\mathrm {r}}; \boldsymbol {\zeta} _ {b _ {x}, b _ {y}}\right) \right] ^ {t} \in \mathbb {c} ^ {b _ {x} b _ {y} \times 1}. \tag {49} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\mathbf{w} = \sqrt{\frac{p_{\mathrm{tx}}^{\mathrm{ul}}}{n_{\mathrm{used}}}}\left[\mathbf{w}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{w}_{n_{\mathrm{p}}}\right]^{t}\in \mathbb{c}^{n_{\mathrm{p}}\times b_{x}b_{y}}$  and  $\mathbf{n}_u =$ $[\mathbf{n}_{1,u},\ldots ,\mathbf{n}_{n_{\mathrm{p}},u}]^t\in \mathbb{c}^{n_{\mathrm{p}}\times n_{\mathrm{used}}}$  . the objective of the uplink ce stage is therefore, to estimate  $\mathbf{h}_u^{\mathrm{addd}}$  by exploiting the knowledge of w,  $\mathbf{f}_u$  , and the noisy matrix  $\mathbf{y}_u$  . usually, we have  $n_{\mathrm{p}} <   b_{x}b_{y}$  due to the limited channel coherence time, and  $n_{\mathrm{used}} <   n_{\mathrm{cp}}$  due to the allocation of different dscs among multiple ues. these constraints make (56) an underdetermined system, which is not possible to solve by using traditional estimation techniques such as the ls estimator [26], [27]. fortunately, due to the strong los link between the ue and the ris, the channel matrix  $\mathbf{h}_u^{\mathrm{addd}}$  is expected to be sparse, and, in particular, only the elements whose indices fulfill the conditions  $\mu_u^{\mathrm{los}}\approx \zeta_{b_x,b_y}$ $b_{x}\in \{1,\dots,b_{x}\}$ $b_{y}\in \{1,\dots,b_{y}\}$  , and  $\tau_u^{\mathrm{ul,los}}\approx (t - 1)t_s$ $t\in \{1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{cp}}\}$  have a non-negligible absolute value. on the other hand, the other entries have a much smaller absolute value, which can be safely ignored. this property of  $\mathbf{h}_u^{\mathrm{addd}}$  is referred to as the dual sparsity in both the angular domain and delay domain, which can be exploited to solve the ce problem.

based on these considerations, algorithm 2 reports the details of the proposed uplink ce scheme, where the cs-based orthogonal matching pursuit (omp) algorithm is adopted to recover sparse channels based on (56). once the estimated channel  $\hat{\mathbf{h}}_u^{\mathrm{addd}}$  is obtained, interpolation-based methods can be applied to reconstruct the channels in all  $k$  subcarriers [27].

remark: the proposed closed-loop ce framework can be extended to the case where the los paths between the bs and the ues exist. to this end, the ris can be configured in order to reduce the scattering from it (i.e., absorption state [37]). under this configuration, the ues do not receive the signal reflected by the ris. therefore, the direct channel between the bs and the ues can be estimated by using state-of-the-art ce algorithms (see, e.g., [38], [42]–[45]). it is worth mentioning that the proposed closed-loop ce scheme can be applied to conventional mimo (in the absence of riss) communications in order to estimate the direct channels between the bs and the ues. once the direct link is estimated, it can be removed from the signal received in the presence of the ris, and the proposed ce algorithm can be applied to estimate the channel between the ris and the ues.

# vi. conclusions

motivated by the concept of holographic communications, we studied the physical layer transmission of holographic riss in the thz band, where a large number of sub-wavelength reconfigurable elements are densely integrated into a compact space to approach a spatially continuous aperture. we derived the beam pattern of a holographic ris and proposed two beamforming designs that are formulated in closed-form expressions. based on the proposed beamforming designs, we proposed a closed-loop broadband ce scheme for the ris-aided thz massive mimo systems. the proposed ce scheme encompasses a downlink grouping stage and an uplink finer-grained ce stage. in order to reduce the pilot overhead, a cs-based ce algorithm was proposed that exploits the dual

sparsity of thz mimo channels in both the angular domain and delay domain. simulation results showed that holographic riss are able to outperform traditional designs based on non-holographic riss as well as communication schemes that do not use riss. possible future research directions based on the results obtained in this paper include the practical amplitude and phase shift model of reflecting elements, the analysis of hardware impairments, the development of robust signal processing methods, the analysis of near-field communications, and the proof-of-concept validations and field experiments.

the result in (27) can be obtained by letting  $dm = x$  and  $dn = y$  in (17). as far as (28) is concerned, in particular, we have

$$
\begin{array}{l} \tilde {g} _ {\mathrm {n b s}} \left(\psi_ {\mathrm {o u t}}, \psi_ {\mathrm {i n}}; \psi_ {\mathrm {o p t}}\right) \\ \propto \lim  _ {d \rightarrow 0} g _ {\mathrm {n b s}} \left(\psi_ {\text {o u t}}, \psi_ {\text {i n}}; \psi_ {\text {o p t}}\right) \\ = \lim _ {d \to 0} e ^ {j \frac {d - a _ {x}}{2} \delta_ {x}} e ^ {j \frac {d - a _ {y}}{2} \delta_ {y}} \xi_ {n _ {x}} [ d (k _ {\mathrm {o p t}} - \delta_ {x}) ] \xi_ {n _ {y}} [ d (l _ {\mathrm {o p t}} - \delta_ {y}) ] \\ = e ^ {- j \frac {a _ {x}}{2} \delta_ {x}} e ^ {- j \frac {a _ {y}}{2} \delta_ {y}} \lim  _ {d \rightarrow 0} \xi_ {n _ {x}} [ d (k _ {\text {o p t}} - \delta_ {x}) ] \xi_ {n _ {y}} [ d (l _ {\text {o p t}} - \delta_ {y}) ]. \tag {61} \\ \end{array}
$$

given the symmetry, we only need to prove  $\lim_{d\to 0}\xi_{n_x}[d(k_{\mathrm{opt}} - \delta_x)] = \mathrm{sinc}\left[\frac{a_x}{2} (k_{\mathrm{opt}} - \delta_x)\right]$  in order to obtain (28). when  $k_{\mathrm{opt}}\neq \delta_x$  , based on the definition of the function  $\xi_{n_x}$  , we have

\begin{array}{l} \lim  _ {d \rightarrow 0} \xi_ {n _ {x}} [ d (k _ {\text {o p t}} - \delta_ {x}) ] \\ = \lim _ {d \rightarrow 0} \frac {\sin \left[ \frac {n _ {x} d}{2} \left(k _ {\mathrm {o p t}} - \delta_ {x}\right)\right]}{n _ {x} \sin \left[ \frac {d}{2} \left(k _ {\mathrm {o p t}} - \delta_ {x}\right)\right]} \\ \stackrel {\mathrm {(a)}} {=} \lim  _ {d \rightarrow 0} \frac {d \sin \left[ \frac {a _ {x}}{2} \left(k _ {\text {o p t}} - \delta_ {x}\right)\right]}{a _ {x} \sin \left[ \frac {d}{2} \left(k _ {\text {o p t}} - \delta_ {x}\right)\right]} \\ \stackrel {\mathrm {(b)}} {=} \lim  _ {d \rightarrow 0} \frac {\sin \left[ \frac {a _ {x}}{2} \left(k _ {\mathrm {o p t}} - \delta_ {x}\right)\right]}{\frac {a _ {x}}{2} \left(k _ {\mathrm {o p t}} - \delta_ {x}\right) \cos \left[ \frac {d}{2} \left(k _ {\mathrm {o p t}} - \delta_ {x}\right)\right]} \\ = \frac {\sin \left[ \frac {a _ {x}}{2} \left(k _ {\text {o p t}} - \delta_ {x}\right) \right]}{\frac {a _ {x}}{2} \left(k _ {\text {o p t}} - \delta_ {x}\right)} \\ = \operatorname {s i n c} \left[ \frac {a _ {x}}{2} \left(k _ {\text {o p t}} - \delta_ {x}\right) \right], \tag {62} \\ \end{array}
