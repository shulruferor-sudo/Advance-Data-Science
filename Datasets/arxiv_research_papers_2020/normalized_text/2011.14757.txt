# message passing based structured sparse signal recovery for estimation of otfs channels with fractional doppler shifts

fei liu, zhengdao yuan, qinghua guo, senior member, ieee, zhongyong wang and peng sun

abstract—the orthogonal time frequency space (otfs) modulation has emerged as a promising modulation scheme for high mobility wireless communications. to enable efficient otfs detection in the delay-doppler (dd) domain, the dd domain channels need to be acquired accurately. to achieve the low latency requirement in future wireless communications, the time duration of the otfs block should be small, therefore fractional doppler shifts have to be considered to avoid significant modelling errors due to the assumption of integer doppler shifts. however there lack investigations on the estimation of otfs channels with fractional doppler shifts in the literature. in this work, we develop a channel estimator for otfs with particular attention to fractional doppler shifts, and both bi-orthogonal waveform and rectangular waveform are considered. instead of estimating the dd domain channel directly, we estimate the channel gains and (fractional) doppler shifts that parameterize the dd domain channel. the estimation is formulated as a structured sparse signal recovery problem with a bayesian treatment. based on a factor graph representation of the problem, an efficient message passing algorithm is developed to recover the structured sparse signal (thereby the otfs channel). the cramer-rao lower bound (crlb) for the estimation is developed and the effectiveness of the algorithm is demonstrated through simulations.

index terms—orthogonal time frequency space modulation, message passing, channel estimation, fractional doppler shifts.

# i. introduction

rencently, the orthogonal time frequency space (otfs) modulation has been proposed to achieve reliable communications in high-mobility scenarios [1], [2], [3]. otfs provides both time and frequency diversity because each symbol is spread over the time and frequency domains through the two dimensional inverse symplectic finite fourier transform (isfft) [1], [2]. when the number of channel paths is small, the effective channel in the delay-doppler (dd) domain is sparse, which allows efficient data detection using the message passing techniques [2]. a variety of otfs detection methods

have been proposed in the literature to harvest the time and frequency diversity promised by otfs [4]–[11]. however, all the detection methods assume perfect channel state information, which has to be estimated in practice.

in otfs, the delay shifts and doppler shifts are discretized in the dd domain. in general, a wideband system is able to provide sufficient delay resolution, so that fractional delay shifts do not need to be considered [12]. however, the doppler resolution depends on the time duration of the otfs block. to fulfill the low latency requirement in future wireless communications, the time duration of the otfs block should be relatively small, hence fractional doppler shifts have to be considered to avoid significant modelling errors due to the assumption of integer doppler shifts [2], [13]. therefore factional doppler shifts have to be considered in otfs channel estimation.

a number of channel estimation methods have been proposed in the literature. in [14], an entire otfs block is used to accommodate pilot symbols for channel estimation, and the estimated channel is used for data detection in the subsequent otfs block. this results in a considerable loss in spectrum efficiency and the detection performance may deteriorate due to the channel variation between two otfs blocks. to solve this problem, pilot and data symbols are placed in the same otfs block in [13], where guard interval is used to avoid interference between pilot and data symbols. with this scheme, channel estimation and data detection can be performed for the same otfs block. in [15], channel estimation in massive multiple input and multiple output (mimo)-otfs systems is studied, where the downlink time-varying massive mimo channels are transformed to the delay-doppler-angle domain, and the channel estimation is formulated as a sparse signal recovery problem. in [16], the uplink-aided downlink massive mimo-otfs channel estimation over the delay-doppler-angle domain is studied. however, in these works, only integer doppler shifts are considered, except [13]. in [13], a single pilot symbol is used to facilitate the design of a low complexity thresholding method to acquire the channel in the dd domain. although factional doppler shifts are considered for otfs with the bi-orthogonal waveform in [13], it is not clear how to estimate the channel with fractional doppler shifts for otfs with a more practical waveform, e.g., the rectangular waveform. in addition, the method estimates the dd domain channel directly, and does not provide the estimates of the channel gains and fractional doppler shifts.

in this work, we address the issue of otfs channel esti

mation with particular attention to fractional doppler shifts. as the otfs channel is parameterized by the channel gains and fractional doppler shifts, we estimate these parameters rather than the dd domain channel directly, mainly due to two reasons. firstly, this can lead to much better performance as the number of variables to be estimated is significantly smaller. secondly, the estimates of doppler shifts can be useful, e.g., for estimating the velocity of mobile users. in addition, both the bi-orthogonal waveform and the rectangular waveform are considered. we formulate the otfs channel estimation as a structured signal recovery problem, which is solved using bayesian inference. with a factor graph representation of the problem, a message passing algorithm is developed to estimate the channel gains and fractional doppler shifts jointly. in contrast to [13], we show that using a few pilot symbols rather than a single pilot symbol is more preferable as the peak to average power ratio (papr) of otfs signals can be significantly reduced. our proposed algorithm can work with either single pilot symbol or multiple pilot symbols, and significantly outperforms the state-of-the-art method. the cramer-rao lower bound (crlb) for the channel estimation is derived to verify the performance of the proposed algorithm. the bit error rates (ber) of the otfs system with perfect channel and estimated channel are also compared to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed algorithm.

the main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

- the otfs dd domain channel is acquired by estimating the relevant parameters, i.e., the channel gains and fractional doppler shifts. the estimation of the parameters is formulated as a novel structured sparse signal recovery problem with a bayesian treatment.  
- both the bi-orthogonal waveform and the rectangular waveform are considered in this work. to the best of our knowledge, the investigations on the fractional doppler shift estimation are very limited in the literature. the work in [13], where the dd domain channel is estimated directly, does not provide fractional doppler estimate explicitly, and it is not applicable to the case of the rectangular waveform.  
- due to the uniqueness of the formulated structured sparse signal recovery problem, a dedicated message passing algorithm is proposed to efficiently solve the problem. the algorithm is able to work with either a single pilot symbol or multiple pilot symbols, which can significantly reduce the papr of the otfs signal.  
- to evaluate the performance of the estimator, the crlb is derived. comparison results with existing methods are provided to demonstrate the advantages of the proposed method.

for simplicity, this work is focused on a plain otfs system, but the proposed algorithm can be extended for a more complex system, such as mimo-otfs.

the remainder of the paper is organized as follows. in section ii, we introduce otfs modulation and demodulation, and otfs input-output relation in the dd domain. then we formulate the channel estimation as a structured sparse signal

recovery problem in section iii, and develop the message passing based algorithm in section iv. the clrb for channel estimation is derived in section v. simulation results are provided in section vi, followed by conclusions in section vii.

# ii. otfs system model

# a. system model in the dd domain

as shown in fig. 1, the otfs modulation and demodulation are implemented with 2d inverse sfft (isfft) and sfft at the transmitter and receiver, respectively [1] [17]. a (coded) bit sequence is mapped to symbols  $\{x[k,l], k = 0,\dots ,n-1,l = 0,\dots m - 1\}$  in the dd domain, where  $x[k,l]\in \mathcal{a} = \{\alpha_{1},\ldots \alpha_{|\mathcal{a}|}\}$  with  $|\mathcal{a}|$  being the cardinality of  $\mathcal{a}$ , and  $l$  and  $k$  denote the indices of the delay and doppler shifts, respectively. as shown in fig. 1, isfft is performed to convert the symbols to signals in the time-frequency (tf) domain, i.e.,

$$
x _ {t f} [ n, m ] = \frac {1}{\sqrt {m n}} \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {n - 1} \sum_ {l = 0} ^ {m - 1} x [ k, l ] e ^ {j 2 \pi \left(\frac {n k}{n} - \frac {m l}{m}\right)}. \tag {1}
$$

then  $\{x_{tf}[m,n]\}$  are converted to a continuous-time waveform  $s(t)$  using the heisenberg transform with a transmit waveform  $g_{tx}(t)$ , i.e.,

$$
s (t) = \sum_ {n = 0} ^ {n - 1} \sum_ {m = 0} ^ {m - 1} x _ {t f} [ n, m ] g _ {t x} (t - n t) e ^ {j 2 \pi m \delta f (t - n t)}, \tag {2}
$$

where  $\delta f$  is the subcarrier spacing and  $t = 1 / \delta f$ .

the signal  $s(t)$  is then transmitted through a time-varying channel and the received signal in the time domain can be expressed as

$$
r (t) = \int \int h (\tau , \nu) s (t - \tau) e ^ {j 2 \pi \nu (t - \tau)} d \tau d \nu , \tag {3}
$$

![](images/ac86023905879ba5b5670e2afda3a84003bfed9a1a047d00aba4f75ca0c4d6bd.jpg)  
fig. 1: otfs modulation and demodulation [2].

where  $h(\tau, \nu)$  is the channel impulse response in the (continuous) dd domain. the channel impulse response can be expressed as

$$
h (\tau , \nu) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {p} h _ {i} \delta \left(\tau - \tau_ {i}\right) \delta \left(\nu - \nu_ {i}\right), \tag {4}
$$

where  $\delta(\cdot)$  is the dirac delta function,  $p$  is the number of resolvable propagation paths, and  $h_i$ ,  $\tau_i$  and  $\nu_i$  represent the gain, delay shift and doppler shift associated with the  $i$ th path, respectively. the delay and doppler shift taps for the  $i$ th path are

$$
\tau_ {i} = \frac {l _ {i}}{m \delta f}, \tag {5}
$$

$$
\nu_ {i} = \frac {k _ {i} + \kappa_ {i}}{n t}, \tag {6}
$$

where  $0 \leq l_{i} \leq l_{max}$  and  $-k_{max} \leq k_{i} \leq k_{max}$  are the delay index and doppler index of the  $i$ th path,  $l_{max}$  and  $k_{max}$  represent the largest indices of the delay taps and doppler taps, respectively, and  $\kappa_{i} \in [-0.5, 0.5]$  is the fractional doppler shift associated with the  $i$ th path.

at the receiver side, a receive waveform  $g_{rx}(t)$  is used to transform the received signal  $r(t)$  to the tf domain i.e.,

$$
y (t, f) = \int g _ {r x} ^ {*} \left(t ^ {\prime} - t\right) r \left(t ^ {\prime}\right) e ^ {- j 2 \pi f \left(t ^ {\prime} - t\right)} d t ^ {\prime}, \tag {7}
$$

which is then sampled at  $t = nt$  and  $f = m\delta f$ , yielding  $y[n,m]$ . finally, an sfft is applied to  $\{y[n,m]\}$  to obtain the signal  $y[k,l]$  in the dd domain, i.e.,

$$
y [ k, l ] = \frac {1}{\sqrt {m n}} \sum_ {n = 0} ^ {n - 1} \sum_ {m = 0} ^ {m - 1} y [ n, m ] e ^ {- j 2 \pi \left(\frac {n k}{n} - \frac {m l}{m}\right)}. \tag {8}
$$

if the transmit waveform  $g_{tx}(t)$  and receive waveform  $g_{rx}(t)$  satisfy the bi-orthogonal property [1], the channel input-output relationship in the dd domain can be expressed as [2], [9]

$$
\begin{array}{l} y [ k, l ] = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {p} \sum_ {q = - n _ {i}} ^ {n _ {i}} h _ {i} \frac {1 - e ^ {- j 2 \pi (- q - \kappa_ {i})}}{n - n e ^ {- j \frac {2 \pi}{n} (- q - \kappa_ {i})}} e ^ {- j 2 \pi \frac {l _ {i} (k _ {i} + \kappa_ {i})}{m n}} \\ \times x \left[ \left[ k - k _ {i} + q \right] _ {n}, \left[ l - l _ {i} \right] _ {m} \right] + \omega [ k, l ], \tag {9} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $n_{i}\ll n$  is an integer, and  $\omega [k,l]$  denotes the gaussian noise in the dd domain with mean 0 and variance  $\gamma^{-1}$  (or precision  $\gamma$ ). we can see that for each path, the transmitted signal is circularly shifted, and scaled by a channel gain. we stack  $\{x[k,l]\}$  to form a vector  $\pmb {x}\in \mathbb{c}^{mn\times 1}$ , where the  $j$ th element  $x_{j}$  is  $x[k,l]$  with  $j = km + l$ . similarly, a vector

$\pmb{y} \in \mathbb{c}^{mn \times 1}$  can also be constructed from  $\{y[k, l]\}$ . then (9) can be rewritten in a vector form as

$$
\boldsymbol {y} = \boldsymbol {h} _ {b i} \boldsymbol {x} + \boldsymbol {\omega}, \tag {10}
$$

where  $\omega$  is the corresponding noise vector, and  $h_{bi} \in \mathbb{c}^{mn \times mn}$  represents the effective channel in the dd domain, which can be expressed as

$$
\begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {h} _ {b i} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {p} \sum_ {q = - n _ {i}} ^ {n _ {i}} \boldsymbol {i} _ {n} \left(- \left[ q - k _ {i} \right] _ {n}\right) \otimes \left[ \boldsymbol {i} _ {m} \left(l _ {i}\right) h _ {i} \right. \\ \left. \times \left(\frac {1 - e ^ {- j 2 \pi (- q - \kappa_ {i})}}{n - n e ^ {- j \frac {2 \pi}{n} (- q - \kappa_ {i})}}\right) e ^ {- j 2 \pi \frac {l _ {i} \left(k _ {i} + \kappa_ {i}\right)}{m n}} \right], \tag {11} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\mathbf{i}_n(-[q - k_i]_n)$  denotes an  $n\times n$  matrix obtained by circularly shifting the rows of an identity matrix by  $-[q - k_{i}]_{n}$  and  $\pmb{i}_{m}(l_{i})$  is obtained similarly.

when the rectangular waveform is used for both  $g_{tx}(t)$  and  $g_{rx}(t)$ , the received signal  $y[k,l]$  in the dd domain can be expressed as [2], [9]

$$
\begin{array}{l} y [ k, l ] = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {p} \sum_ {q = - n _ {i}} ^ {n _ {i}} h _ {i} e ^ {j 2 \pi \left(\frac {l - l _ {i}}{m}\right) \left(\frac {k _ {i} + \kappa_ {i}}{n}\right)} \alpha_ {i} (k, l, q) \\ \times x \left[ \left[ k - k _ {i} + q \right] _ {n}, \left[ l - l _ {i} \right] _ {m} \right] + \omega [ k, l ], \tag {12} \\ \end{array}
$$

where

$$
\alpha_ {i} (k, l, q) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \frac {1}{n} \beta_ {i} (q) & l _ {i} \leq l <   m \\ \frac {1}{n} \left(\beta_ {i} (q) - 1\right) e ^ {- j 2 \pi \frac {[ k - k _ {i} + q ] _ {n}}{n}} & 0 \leq l <   l _ {i} \end{array} , \right. \tag {13}
$$

and

$$
\beta_ {i} (q) = \frac {e ^ {- j 2 \pi (- q - \kappa_ {i})} - 1}{e ^ {- j \frac {2 \pi}{n} (- q - \kappa_ {i})} - 1}. \tag {14}
$$

similarly, we can also rewrite (12) in a vector form as

$$
\boldsymbol {y} = \boldsymbol {h} _ {\text {r e c t}} \boldsymbol {x} + \boldsymbol {\omega}, \tag {15}
$$

and the channel matrix  $\pmb{h}_{rect} \in \mathbb{c}^{mn \times mn}$  can be written as

$$
\begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {h} _ {\text {r e c t}} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {p} \sum_ {q = - n _ {i}} ^ {n _ {i}} \boldsymbol {i} _ {n} (- [ q - k _ {i} ] _ {n}) \otimes \\ \left(\boldsymbol {\lambda} \boldsymbol {i} _ {m} \left(l _ {i}\right) h _ {i} e ^ {- j 2 \pi \frac {l _ {i} \left(k _ {i} + \kappa_ {i}\right)}{m n}}\right) \cdot \boldsymbol {\delta} ^ {- [ q - k _ {i} ] _ {n}}, \tag {16} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\lambda \in \mathbb{c}^{m\times m}$  is a diagonal matrix and the  $l$ th diagonal element  $\lambda_{ll}$  can be expressed as

$$
\boldsymbol {\lambda} _ {l l} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} e ^ {j 2 \pi \frac {l \left(k _ {i} + \kappa_ {i}\right)}{m n}} \beta_ {i} (q) / n & l _ {i} \leq l <   m \\ e ^ {j 2 \pi \frac {l \left(k _ {i} + \kappa_ {i}\right)}{m n}} \left(\beta_ {i} (q) - 1\right) / n & 0 \leq l <   l _ {i} \end{array} \right.. \tag {17}
$$

note that  $\delta^{-[q - k_i]_n}$  is an  $mn\times mn$  block matrix, which is obtained by circularly shifting the blocks in a matrix  $\pmb{\delta}$  by  $-[q - k_{i}]_{n}$ . the matrix  $\pmb{\delta}$  is a block diagonal matrix

$$
\boldsymbol {\delta} = \left( \begin{array}{c c c c} \boldsymbol {\delta} _ {0} & \mathbf {0} & \dots & \mathbf {0} \\ \mathbf {0} & \boldsymbol {\delta} _ {1} & \dots & \mathbf {0} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \mathbf {0} & \mathbf {0} & \dots & \boldsymbol {\delta} _ {n - 1} \end{array} \right), \tag {18}

$$
\psi_ {m m} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 & l _ {i} \leq m <   m \\ e ^ {- j 2 \pi m / n} & 0 \leq m <   l _ {i} \end{array} \right.. \tag {19}
$$

# b. data and pilot symbols arrangement

in this paper, we use the scheme in [13] and [15], where pilot symbols are inserted to the dd plane with guard interval to avoid the interference between data and pilot symbols, as shown in fig. 2. this allows that channel estimation and data detection are carried out for the same otfs block.

as shown in fig. 2, the size of the pilot symbol block is  $m_{p} \times n_{p}$ . to avoid the interference between pilot and data symbols, the size of guard interval should be at least  $2(k_{max} + \hat{n})$  along the doppler dimension and  $l_{max}$  along the delay dimension, where we assume  $\{n_{i}, i = 1,2,\dots ,p\}$  take a same value  $\hat{n}$ . with this scheme, the overhead due to pilot symbols and guard interval is  $(2l_{max} + m_p)(4k_{max} + 4\hat{n} + n_p) / mn$ . the received signal in the dd domain is shown in

# iii. structured sparse signal recovery for otfs channel estimation

the otfs channel can be estimated using the pilot symbols and the received signals in the orange area shown in fig. 3. it can be seen from (9) and (25) that the otfs channel is parameterized by the nonzero channel gains  $\{h_i\}$ , the fractional doppler shifts  $\{\kappa_i\}$  and their indices  $\{l_i, k_i\}$ ,  $i = 1, \dots, p$ . these parameters will be estimated, based on which the otfs channel in the dd domain can be constructed.

# a. bi-orthogonal waveform

model (9) involves a number of unknown variables, including  $h_i$ ,  $\kappa_i$ ,  $l_i$  and  $k_i$  for  $i = 1, \dots, p$ . in addition,  $p$  is unknown as well. these make the estimation very difficult. to overcome this, we define two variables  $t$  and  $d$  to represent the indices of delay shifts and doppler shifts, respectively. as only the pilot symbols need to be considered, we have  $t \in [0, l_{max}]$  and  $d \in [-k_{max}, k_{max}]$ . then (9) can be rewritten as

$$
y [ k, l ] = \sum_ {t = 0} ^ {l _ {m a x}} \sum_ {d = - k _ {m a x}} ^ {k _ {m a x}} h _ {t, d} e ^ {- j 2 \pi \frac {t (d + \kappa_ {d})}{m n}} \sum_ {q = - \widehat {n}} ^ {\widehat {n}} f (q, \kappa_ {d})
$$

$$
x \left[ [ k - d + q ] _ {n}, [ l - t ] _ {m} \right] + \omega [ k, l ], \tag {20}
$$

where

$$
f (q, \kappa_ {d}) = \frac {1 - e ^ {- j 2 \pi (- q - \kappa_ {d})}}{n - n e ^ {- j \frac {2 \pi}{n} (- q - \kappa_ {d})}}. \tag {21}
$$

the received signals  $\{y[k,l]\}$  in (20) corresponding to the pilot symbols are in the orange area in fig. 3. our aim is to find the non-zero elements in  $\{h_{t,d},\kappa_d,t\in [0,l_{max}],d\in [-k_{max},k_{max}]\}$  based on  $\{y[k,l]\}$ . therefore, the estimation can be formulated as a sparse signal recovery problem.

to facilitate the design of the estimation algorithm, we rewrite (20) in a matrix form as

$$
\boldsymbol {y} = \boldsymbol {x} _ {b i} \boldsymbol {c} + \boldsymbol {\omega}, \tag {22}
$$

where  $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{c}^{z \times 1}$ ,  $z = (l_{max} + m_p)(n_p + 2k_{max} + 2\widehat{n})$  is formed by stacking  $\{y[k,l]\}$  as a vector,  $\mathbf{x}_{bi} \in \mathbb{c}^{z \times (l_{max} + 1)(2k_{max} + 1)(2\widehat{n} + 1)}$  is constructed based on the pilot

symbols,  $\omega \in \mathbb{c}^{z\times 1}$  is obtained by stacking  $\{\omega [k,l]\}$ , and  $\pmb {c}\in \mathbb{c}^{(l_{max} + 1)(2k_{max} + 1)b\times 1}$  with  $b = 2\widehat{n} +1$  can be expressed as

\boldsymbol {c} = \left[ \boldsymbol {c} _ {0, - k _ {m a x}} ^ {t}, \boldsymbol {c} _ {1, - k _ {m a x}} ^ {t}, \dots , \boldsymbol {c} _ {t, d} ^ {t}, \dots , \boldsymbol {c} _ {l _ {m a x}, k _ {m a x}} ^ {t} \right] ^ {t}, \tag {23}
$$

where  $\pmb{c}_{t,d} \in \mathbb{c}^{b \times 1}$  is given as

$$
\boldsymbol {c} _ {t, d} = h _ {t, d} e ^ {- j 2 \pi \frac {(d + \kappa_ {d}) t}{m n}}.
$$

\left[ f \left(- \widehat {n}, \kappa_ {d}\right), f \left(- \widehat {n} + 1, \kappa_ {d}\right), \dots , f \left(\widehat {n}, \kappa_ {d}\right) \right] ^ {t}. (2 4)
$$

we can see that the vector  $\pmb{c}$  has a structure, i.e.,  $\pmb{c}$  is block sparse, and for a non-zero block (subvector)  $\pmb{c}_{t,d}$ , it is parameterized by only two parameters  $h_{t,d}$  and  $\kappa_d$ . we will recover the structured sparse vector  $\pmb{c}$  and obtain the estimates of the non-zero channel gains and fractional doppler shifts.

# b. rectangular waveform

to facilitate channel estimation, we place the pilot symbols to ensure that their delay index  $l \geq l_{max}$ , so that (12) is reduced to

$$
\begin{array}{l} y [ k, l ] = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {p} \sum_ {q = - n _ {i}} ^ {n _ {i}} h _ {i} \frac {1 - e ^ {- j 2 \pi (- q - \kappa_ {i})}}{n - n e ^ {- j \frac {2 \pi}{n} (- q - \kappa_ {i})}} e ^ {j 2 \pi \frac {(l - l _ {i}) (k _ {i} + \kappa_ {i})}{m n}} \\ \times x \left[ \left[ k - k _ {i} + q \right] _ {n}, \left[ l - l _ {i} \right] _ {m} \right] + \omega [ k, l ]. \tag {25} \\ \end{array}
$$

comparing (25) to (9), we can find that their difference lies in that each transmitted symbol in (25) is rotated by an additional phase  $e^{j2\pi \frac{l(k_i + \kappa_i)}{mn}}$ .

similar to the case of bi-orthogonal waveform, model (25) can be rewritten in a matrix form

$$
\boldsymbol {y} = \boldsymbol {x} _ {\text {r e c t}} \boldsymbol {c} + \boldsymbol {\omega}, \tag {26}
$$

where  $\pmb{c}$  is the structured sparse vector to be recovered, and the  $(z,n)$ th element of matrix  $x_{rect}$  is given as

$$
x _ {r e c t} ^ {z, n} = e ^ {j 2 \pi \frac {l _ {z} \left(d _ {n} + \kappa_ {n}\right)}{m n}} x _ {b i} ^ {z, n}, \tag {27}
$$

where  $l_{z}$  is the delay index corresponding to the  $z$ th element of  $\mathbf{y}$ ,  $d_{n}$  and  $\kappa_{n}$  are the parameters  $d$  and  $\kappa_{d}$  of the  $n$ th element of vector  $\mathbf{c}$  (refer to the definition of vector  $\mathbf{c}$  in (23), and  $x_{bi}^{z,n}$  denotes the the  $(z,n)$ th element of matrix  $x_{bi}$ ). it is noted that when  $z$  and  $n$  are given,  $l_{z}$  and  $d_{n}$  are known, but  $\kappa_{n}$  is unknown. so it is different from the case of the biorthogonal waveform in (22) that the matrix  $x_{rect}$  depends on the fractional doppler shifts, which are unknown. later we will show that this can be solved by using an iterative estimation strategy.

as discussed above, the channel estimation problem can be formulated as structured sparse signal recovery. next, we will develop a bayesian method to recover the block sparse vector  $\mathbf{c}$  and obtain the estimates of  $\{h_{t,d}\}$  and  $\{\kappa_d\}$ . in particular, the factor graph techniques [18] are used, based on which an efficient message passing algorithm is developed.

# iv. bayesian approach andmessage passing algorithm

in this section, we first focus on otfs with the biorthogonal waveform, and then extend our discussion to the

case of the rectangular waveform.

for the convenience of notation, we define  $j = (l_{max} + 1)(k_{max} + d) + t + 1$ . as  $t \in [0, l_{max}]$  and  $d \in [-k_{max}, k_{max}]$ , there is one-to-one map between the index  $j$  and the index pair  $(t, d)$ . then we define

$$
\boldsymbol {c} _ {j} = \boldsymbol {c} _ {t, d}, h _ {j} = h _ {t, d}, \kappa_ {j} = \kappa_ {d}, \tag {28}
$$

which will be used hereafter.

inspired by the sparse bayesian learning [19], we assume a gaussian prior with mean 0 and precision  $\lambda_{j}$  for  $h_j$  to promote sparsity, i.e.,

$$
p \left(h _ {j} \mid \lambda_ {j}\right) = \mathcal {c n} \left(h _ {j}; 0, \lambda_ {j} ^ {- 1}\right), \tag {29}
$$

where the hyperparameter  $\lambda_{j}$  is gamma distributed, i.e.,  $p(\lambda_j) = ga(\lambda_j;\epsilon_j,\eta_j)$ . the hyper-parameters  $\epsilon_{j}$  and  $\eta_{j}$  are respectively set to be 1 and 0, leading to a uniform or noninformative distribution for  $\lambda_{j}$  [20]. as the noise precision  $\gamma$  is normally unknown, it will also be estimated with an improper prior  $p(\gamma)$  [19].

to facilitate the algorithm design, we introduce an auxiliary vector

$$
\boldsymbol {g} _ {j} \triangleq \left[ f (- \widehat {n}, \kappa_ {j}), f (- \widehat {n} + 1, \kappa_ {j}), \dots , f (\widehat {n}, \kappa_ {j}) \right] ^ {t} e ^ {- j 2 \pi \frac {t (d + \kappa_ {j})}{m n}}
$$

$$
\triangleq \left[ \phi \left(- \widehat {n}, \kappa_ {j}\right), \dots , \phi \left(q, \kappa_ {j}\right), \dots , \phi \left(\widehat {n}, \kappa_ {j}\right) \right] ^ {t}, \tag {30}
$$

where

$$
\phi (q, \kappa_ {j}) = f (q, \kappa_ {j}) e ^ {- j 2 \pi \frac {t (d + \kappa_ {j})}{m n}}. \tag {31}
$$

then we have

$$
\boldsymbol {c} _ {j} = h _ {j} \boldsymbol {g} _ {j}. \tag {32}
$$

define  $\pmb{h} = [h_1, \dots, h_j]^t$ ,  $\pmb{g} = [\pmb{g}_1^t, \dots, \pmb{g}_j^t]^t$ ,  $\pmb{\kappa} = [\kappa_1, \dots, \kappa_j]^t$  and  $\pmb{\lambda} = [\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_j]^t$ , where  $j = (l_{\text{max}} + 1)(2k_{\text{max}} + 1)$ . then the joint conditional distribution of the unknown variables can be factorized as

$$
\begin{array}{l} p (\boldsymbol {c}, \boldsymbol {h}, \boldsymbol {g}, \kappa , \lambda , \gamma | \boldsymbol {y}) \\ \propto p (\boldsymbol {y} \mid \boldsymbol {c}, \gamma) p (\boldsymbol {c} \mid \boldsymbol {h}, \boldsymbol {g}) p (\boldsymbol {h} \mid \lambda) p (\lambda) p (\boldsymbol {g} \mid \kappa) p (\kappa) p (\gamma) \\ = p (\gamma) p (\boldsymbol {y} | \boldsymbol {c}, \gamma) \prod_ {j, b} p (c _ {j b} | h _ {j}, g _ {j b}) p (h _ {j} | \lambda_ {j}) p (\lambda_ {j}) \\ \times p \left(g _ {j b} \mid \kappa_ {j}\right) p \left(\kappa_ {j}\right) \\ \end{array}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \triangleq f _ {\gamma} (\gamma) f _ {\boldsymbol {y}} (\boldsymbol {c}, \gamma) \prod_ {j, b} f _ {c _ {j b}} \left(c _ {j b}, h _ {j}, g _ {j b}\right) f _ {h _ {j}} \left(h _ {j}, \lambda_ {j}\right) f _ {\lambda_ {j}} \left(\lambda_ {j}\right) \\ \times f _ {g _ {j b}} \left(g _ {j b}, \kappa_ {j}\right) f _ {\kappa_ {j}} \left(\kappa_ {j}\right), \tag {33} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $1 \leq j \leq j$ ,  $1 \leq b \leq b$ , and  $c_{jb}$  and  $g_{jb}$  represent the  $b$ th element of  $c_{j}$  and  $g_{j}$ , respectively. the correspondence between the factors and distributions (and their detailed forms) are listed in table ii. we aim to obtain the approximate marginals of the parameters (thereby their estimates) by performing approximate inference. in particular, we use the factor graph techniques, and the variational message passing (vmp) [21], [22] and belief propagation (bp) message passing [18] are combined to achieve efficient approximate inference. we note that variational inference has been used for channel estimation, e.g., the works in [23] and [24].

# a. message computations in part i

assume that the belief of  $\pmb{c}$  is known, which turns out to be gaussian, i.e.,  $b(\pmb{c}) \propto \mathcal{cn}(\pmb{c};\pmb{c}^p,\pmb{v}_c^p)$ , as given later in (39) and (40). the vmp or mean field (mf) is used at the function node  $f_{\pmb{y}}(\pmb{c},\gamma)$ , and the message from  $f_{\pmb{y}}(\pmb{c},\gamma)$  to the variable

node  $\gamma$  can be computed as

$$
\begin{array}{l} m _ {f _ {\boldsymbol {y}} \rightarrow \gamma} (\gamma) \propto \exp \int \ln f _ {\boldsymbol {y}} (\boldsymbol {c}, \gamma) b (\boldsymbol {c}) d \boldsymbol {c} \\ \propto \gamma^ {z} \exp \{- \gamma [ (\boldsymbol {y} - \boldsymbol {x} _ {b i} \boldsymbol {c} ^ {p}) ^ {h} (\boldsymbol {y} - \boldsymbol {x} _ {b i} \boldsymbol {c} ^ {p}) \\ + t r \left\{\boldsymbol {x} _ {b i} \boldsymbol {v} _ {c} ^ {p} \boldsymbol {x} _ {b i} ^ {h} \right\} \rbrack \}. \tag {34} \\ \end{array}
$$

with the prior  $f_{\gamma}(\gamma) \propto 1 / \gamma$ , the belief  $b(\gamma)$  can be expressed as

$$
b (\gamma) \propto \gamma^ {- 1} m _ {f _ {\mathcal {y}} \rightarrow \gamma} (\gamma). \tag {35}
$$

then, with the mf rule and the belief  $b(\gamma)$ , we can compute the outgoing message  $m_{f_y \to c_{jb}}(c_{jb})$  as

$$
\begin{array}{l} m _ {f _ {\mathcal {y}} \rightarrow c _ {j b}} (c _ {j b}) \\ \propto \exp \int \ln f _ {\boldsymbol {y}} (\boldsymbol {c}, \gamma) b (\gamma) b \left(\boldsymbol {c} _ {\sim c _ {j b}}\right) d \gamma d \boldsymbol {c} _ {\sim c _ {j b}}, \tag {36} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $c_{\sim c_{jb}}$  denotes a vector obtained by removing  $c_{jb}$  from  $c$ . however, the computation of the message involves high complexity. an efficient way is to first compute the belief of  $c_{jb}$ , then compute the outgoing extrinsic message. according to the mf rule, the message  $m_{f_y \to c}(c)$  can be expressed as

$$
\begin{array}{l} m _ {f _ {\boldsymbol {y}} \rightarrow \boldsymbol {c}} (\boldsymbol {c}) \\ \propto \exp \int \ln f _ {\boldsymbol {y}} (\boldsymbol {c}, \gamma) b (\gamma) d \gamma \\ \propto \mathcal {c n} \left(\boldsymbol {c}; \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {b i} ^ {h} \boldsymbol {x} _ {b i}\right) ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {x} _ {b i} ^ {h} \boldsymbol {y}, \hat {\gamma} ^ {- 1} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {b i} ^ {h} \boldsymbol {x} _ {b i}\right) ^ {- 1}\right), \tag {37} \\ \end{array}
$$

where

$$
\begin{array}{l} \hat {\gamma} = \int \gamma b (\gamma) d \gamma \\ = \frac {z}{\left(\boldsymbol {y} - \boldsymbol {x} _ {b i} \boldsymbol {c} ^ {p}\right) ^ {h} \left(\boldsymbol {y} - \boldsymbol {x} _ {b i} c ^ {p}\right) + t r \left\{\boldsymbol {x} _ {b i} v _ {c} ^ {p} \boldsymbol {x} _ {b i} ^ {h} \right\}}. \tag {38} \\ \end{array}
$$

as the incoming message  $m_{f_{c_{jb}} \to c_{jb}}(c_{jb}) \propto \mathcal{cn}(c_{jb}; \stackrel{\leftarrow}{c_{jb}}, \stackrel{\leftarrow}{\nu}_{c_{jb}})$ , the belief of  $c$  is gaussian with covariance matrix  $v_{c}^{p}$  and mean vector  $c^{p}$ , which can be computed as

$$
\boldsymbol {v} _ {c} ^ {p} = \left(\boldsymbol {v} _ {c} ^ {- 1} + \hat {\gamma} \boldsymbol {x} _ {b i} ^ {h} \boldsymbol {x} _ {b i}\right) ^ {- 1}, \tag {39}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol {c} ^ {p} = \boldsymbol {v} _ {c} ^ {p} \left(\boldsymbol {v} _ {c} ^ {- 1} \overleftarrow {\boldsymbol {c}} + \hat {\gamma} \boldsymbol {x} _ {b i} ^ {h} \boldsymbol {y}\right), \tag {40}
$$

where  $v_{c}$  is a diagonal matrix with the diagonal elements given by  $\{\overleftarrow{\nu}_{cjb}\}$  and  $\overleftarrow{c}$  is a column vector that consists of  $\{\overleftarrow{c}_{jb}\}$ . the computations of the mean  $\overleftarrow{c}_{jb}$  and the variance  $\overline{\nu}_{cjb}$  are delayed to (86) and (87). hence the outgoing message  $m_{f_y\rightarrow c_{jb}}(c_{jb})$  is also gaussian, and can be expressed as [25]

$$
m _ {f _ {\mathbf {y}} \rightarrow c _ {j b}} (c _ {j b}) = \mathcal {c n} \left(c _ {j b}; \vec {c} _ {j b}, \vec {\nu} _ {c _ {j b}}\right) \tag {41}
$$

with

$$
\vec {\nu} _ {c _ {j b}} = \left(\frac {1}{\nu_ {c _ {j b}} ^ {p}} - \frac {1}{\overleftarrow {\nu} _ {c _ {j b}}}\right) ^ {- 1}, \tag {42}
$$

$$
\vec {c} _ {j b} = \vec {\nu} _ {c _ {j b}} \left(\frac {c _ {j b} ^ {p}}{\nu_ {c _ {j b}} ^ {p}} - \frac {\stackrel {\leftarrow} {c} _ {j b}}{\stackrel {\leftarrow} {\nu} _ {c _ {j b}}}\right), \tag {43}
$$

where  $c_{jb}^p$  is the  $b$ th element of the  $j$ th block of  $c^p$ , and  $\nu_{c_{jb}}^p$  is the  $b$ th element of the  $j$ th block of the vector that consists of the diagonal elements of  $v_c^p$ .

# b. message computations in part ii

1) forward message passing: with the incoming message from part i  $n_{c_{jb} \to f_{c_{jb}}} (c_{jb}) = m_{f_{y} \to c_{jb}} (c_{jb}) = \mathcal{cn}(c_{jb}; \vec{c}_{jb}, \vec{\nu}_{c_{jb}})$  and the factor  $f_{c_{jb}} (c_{jb}, h_j, g_{jb}) = \delta (c_{jb} - h_j g_{jb})$ , we can obtain an intermediate function node  $\tilde{f}_{c_{jb}} (h_j, g_{jb})$  according to bp [18], i.e.,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \tilde {f} _ {c _ {j b}} \left(h _ {j}, g _ {j b}\right) = \int f _ {c _ {j b}} \left(c _ {j b}, h _ {j}, g _ {j b}\right) n _ {c _ {j b} \rightarrow f _ {c _ {j b}}} \left(c _ {j b}\right) d c _ {j b} \\ = \mathcal {c n} \left(h _ {j} g _ {j b}; \vec {c} _ {j b}, \vec {\nu} _ {c _ {j b}}\right). \tag {44} \\ \end{array}
$$

with the local function  $\tilde{f}_{c_{jb}}(h_j,g_{jb})$ , we can compute the message from  $f_{c_{jb}}$  to  $h_j$  by treating  $g_{jb}$  as a constant, i.e.,

$$
m _ {f _ {c _ {j b}} \rightarrow h _ {j}} (h _ {j}) = \mathcal {c n} \left(h _ {j}; \vec {h} _ {j b}, \vec {\nu} _ {h _ {j b}}\right), \tag {45}
$$

where

$$
\vec {h} _ {j b} = \frac {\vec {c} _ {j b}}{\hat {g} _ {j b}}, \tag {46}
$$

$$
\vec {\nu} _ {h _ {j b}} = \frac {\vec {\nu} _ {c _ {j b}}}{| \hat {g} _ {j b} | ^ {2}} \tag {47}
$$

with  $\hat{g}_{jb}$  being the mean of the gaussian belief of  $g_{jb}$ , which is computed in (85). the product of the gaussian messages  $\{m_{f_{c_{jb}}\to h_j}(h_j),\forall b\}$  is still gaussian [25], i.e.,

$$
q _ {j} \left(h _ {j}\right) = \prod_ {b} m _ {f _ {c _ {j b}} \rightarrow h _ {j}} \left(h _ {j}\right) \propto \mathcal {c n} \left(h _ {j}; \hat {q} _ {j}, v _ {q _ {j}}\right), \tag {48}
$$

where

$$
\nu_ {q _ {j}} = \left(\sum_ {b} \frac {1}{\vec {\nu} _ {h _ {j b}}}\right) ^ {- 1}, \tag {49}
$$

$$
\hat {q} _ {j} = \nu_ {q _ {j}} \sum_ {b} \frac {\vec {h} _ {j b}}{\vec {\nu} _ {h _ {j b}}}. \tag {50}
$$

with the message  $m_{f_{h_j}\to h_j}(h_j)\propto \mathcal{cn}(h_j;0,\widehat{\lambda}_j^{-1})$ , which is given later in (73), the belief  $b(h_{j})$  of  $h_j$  is obtained as

$$
b \left(h _ {j}\right) = q _ {j} \left(h _ {j}\right) m _ {f _ {h _ {j}} \rightarrow h _ {j}} \left(h _ {j}\right) \propto \mathcal {c n} \left(h _ {j}; \hat {h} _ {j}, v _ {h _ {j}}\right), \tag {51}
$$

where

$$
\hat {h} _ {j} = \frac {\hat {q} _ {j}}{1 + \nu_ {q _ {j}} \widehat {\lambda} _ {j}}, \tag {52}
$$

$$
\nu_ {h _ {j}} = \left(\frac {1}{\nu_ {q _ {j}}} + \widehat {\lambda} _ {j}\right) ^ {- 1}. \tag {53}
$$

the message  $m_{f_{h_j} \to \lambda_j}(\lambda_j)$  is then computed by using the mf rule, i.e.,

$$
\begin{array}{l} m _ {f _ {h _ {j}} \rightarrow \lambda_ {j}} (\lambda_ {j}) \propto e x p \left\{\int \ln f _ {h _ {j}} (h _ {j}, \lambda_ {j}) b (h _ {j}) d h _ {j} \right\} \\ \propto \lambda_ {j} e x p \{- \lambda_ {j} \left(\left| \hat {h} _ {j} \right| ^ {2} + \nu_ {h _ {j}}\right) \}, \tag {54} \\ \end{array}
$$

so the belief  $b(\lambda_j)$  of the hyperparameter  $\lambda_j$  is given as

$$
\begin{array}{l} b \left(\lambda_ {j}\right) = m _ {f _ {h _ {j}} \rightarrow \lambda_ {j}} \left(\lambda_ {j}\right) f _ {\lambda_ {j}} \left(\lambda_ {j}\right) \\ \propto \lambda_ {j} ^ {\epsilon_ {j}} \exp \left\{- \lambda_ {j} \left(\eta_ {j} + | \hat {h} _ {j} | ^ {2} + \nu_ {h _ {j}}\right) \right\}. \tag {55} \\ \end{array}
$$

the message from  $f_{c_{jb}}$  to  $g_{jb}$  is computed with the mf rule, and i.e.,

$$
\begin{array}{l} m _ {f _ {c _ {j b}} \rightarrow g _ {j b}} (g _ {j b}) \propto e x p \left\{\int \ln \tilde {f} _ {c _ {j b}} (h _ {j}, g _ {j b}) b (h _ {j}) d h _ {j} \right\} \\ = \mathcal {c n} \left(g _ {j b}; \vec {g} _ {j b}, \vec {\nu} _ {g _ {j b}}\right), \tag {56} \\ \end{array}
$$

where

$$
\vec {g} _ {j b} = \frac {\vec {c} _ {j b} \hat {h} _ {j} ^ {*}}{| \hat {h} _ {j} | ^ {2} + \nu_ {h _ {j}}}, \tag {57}
$$

$$
\vec {\nu} _ {g _ {j b}} = \frac {\vec {\nu} _ {c _ {j b}}}{| \hat {h} _ {j} | ^ {2} + \nu_ {h _ {j}}}. \tag {58}
$$

it is noted that the local function node  $f_{g_j b}$  includes a nonlinear function  $\phi(q, \kappa_j)$ , which makes the message computation about  $\kappa_j$  intractable. inspired by the extended kalman filter [26], to solve this problem,  $\phi(q, \kappa_j)$  is linearized by using the first order taylor expansion with the estimate of  $\kappa_j$  in last iteration, i.e.,

$$
\phi (q, \kappa_ {j}) \approx \phi (q, \hat {\kappa} _ {j} ^ {\prime}) + \phi^ {\prime} (q, \hat {\kappa} _ {j} ^ {\prime}) (\kappa_ {j} - \hat {\kappa} _ {j} ^ {\prime}), \tag {59}
$$

with

$$
\begin{array}{l} \phi^ {\prime} (q, \hat {\kappa} _ {j} ^ {'}) = \left(\frac {- j 2 \pi t}{m n}\right) \phi (q, \hat {\kappa} _ {j} ^ {'}) + \\ e ^ {- j 2 \pi \frac {t \left(d + \hat {\kappa} _ {j} ^ {\prime}\right)}{m n}} \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {n - 1} j \frac {2 n \pi}{n} e ^ {j \frac {2 n \pi}{n} \left(q + \hat {\kappa} _ {j} ^ {\prime}\right)}, \tag {60} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\hat{\kappa}_j^\prime$  denotes the estimates of  $\kappa_{j}$  in last iteration. then, with the bp rule and the approximation in (59), the message  $m_{f_{g_{jb}}\to \kappa_j}(\kappa_j)$  can be expressed as

$$
m _ {f _ {g _ {j b}} \rightarrow \kappa_ {j}} (\kappa_ {j}) = \int f _ {g _ {j b}} (g _ {j b}, \kappa_ {j}) m _ {f _ {c _ {j b}} \rightarrow g _ {j b}} (g _ {j b}) d g _ {j b}. \tag {61}
$$

note that  $\kappa_{j}$  is a real valued variable. to ensure that the message  $m_{f_{g_{jb}}\to \kappa_j}(\kappa_j)$  is real, we rewrite the function  $f_{g_{jb}}(g_{jb},\kappa_j)$  as

$$
\begin{array}{l} f _ {g _ {j b}} \left(g _ {j b}, \kappa_ {j}\right) \\ = \delta (\mathcal {r} [ g _ {j b} ] - \mathcal {r} [ \phi (q, \kappa_ {j}) ]) \delta (\mathcal {i} [ g _ {j b} ] - \mathcal {i} [ \phi (q, \kappa_ {j}) ]), \tag {62} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $q = -\widehat{n} + b - 1$ . then the message  $m_{f_{g_{jb}} \to \kappa_j}(\kappa_j)$  in (61) can be obtained as

$$
\begin{array}{l} m _ {f _ {g _ {j b}} \rightarrow \kappa_ {j}} (\kappa_ {j}) \propto \mathcal {n} (\kappa_ {j}; \vec {\kappa} _ {j b} ^ {\mathcal {r}}, \vec {\nu} _ {\kappa_ {j b}} ^ {\mathcal {r}}) \mathcal {n} (\kappa_ {j}; \vec {\kappa} _ {j b} ^ {\mathcal {i}}, \vec {\nu} _ {\kappa_ {j b}} ^ {\mathcal {i}}) \\ \propto \mathcal {n} \left(\kappa_ {j}; \vec {\kappa} _ {j b}, \vec {\nu} _ {\kappa_ {j b}}\right), \tag {63} \\ \end{array}
$$

where

$$
\begin{array}{l} \vec {\kappa} _ {j b} ^ {\mathcal {r}} = \frac {\mathcal {r} [ \vec {g} _ {j b} ] - \mathcal {r} [ \phi (q , \hat {\kappa} _ {j} ^ {\prime}) ] + \mathcal {r} [ \phi^ {\prime} (q , \hat {\kappa} _ {j} ^ {\prime}) ] \hat {\kappa} _ {j} ^ {\prime}}{\mathcal {r} [ \phi^ {\prime} (q , \hat {\kappa} _ {j} ^ {\prime}) ]}, (64) \\ \vec {\nu} _ {\kappa_ {j b}} ^ {\mathcal {r}} = \frac {\vec {\nu} _ {g _ {j b}}}{2 | \mathcal {r} [ \phi^ {\prime} (q , \hat {\kappa} _ {j} ^ {\prime}) ] | ^ {2}}, (65) \\ \vec {\kappa} _ {j b} ^ {\mathcal {i}} = \frac {\mathcal {i} [ \vec {g} _ {j b} ] - \mathcal {i} [ \phi (q , \hat {\kappa} _ {j} ^ {\prime}) ] + \mathcal {i} [ \phi^ {\prime} (q , \hat {\kappa} _ {j} ^ {\prime}) ] \hat {\kappa} _ {j} ^ {\prime}}{\mathcal {i} [ \phi^ {\prime} (q , \hat {\kappa} _ {j} ^ {\prime}) ]}, (66) \\ \vec {\nu} _ {\kappa_ {j b}} ^ {\mathcal {i}} = \frac {\vec {\nu} _ {g _ {j b}}}{2 | \mathcal {i} [ \phi^ {\prime} (q , \hat {\kappa} _ {j} ^ {\prime}) ] | ^ {2}}, (67) \\ \end{array}
$$

$$
\vec {\nu} _ {\kappa_ {j b}} = \left(\frac {1}{\vec {\nu} _ {\kappa_ {j b}} ^ {\mathcal {r}}} + \frac {1}{\vec {\nu} _ {\kappa_ {j b}} ^ {\mathcal {i}}}\right) ^ {- 1}, \tag {68}
$$

$$
\vec {\kappa} _ {j b} = \vec {\nu} _ {\kappa_ {j b}} \left( \begin{array}{c} \vec {\kappa} _ {j b} ^ {\mathcal {r}} \\ \frac {\vec {\kappa} _ {j b} ^ {\mathcal {r}}}{\vec {\nu} _ {\kappa_ {j b}}} + \frac {\vec {\kappa} _ {j b} ^ {\mathcal {i}}}{\vec {\nu} _ {\kappa_ {j b}}} \end{array} \right). \tag {69}
$$

the prior of  $\kappa_{j}$  is a uniform distribution over  $[-0.5, 0.5]$ , i.e.,  $-0.5 \leq \kappa_{j} \leq 0.5$ . to simplify the computation of the belief  $b(\kappa_{j})$ , we simply carry out the clipping operation, i.e.,

$$
b \left(\kappa_ {j}\right) \propto \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \mathcal {n} \left(\kappa_ {j}; - 0. 5, 0\right); & \hat {\kappa} _ {j} \leq - 0. 5 \\ \mathcal {n} \left(\kappa_ {j}; 0. 5, 0\right); & \hat {\kappa} _ {j} \geq 0. 5 \\ \mathcal {n} \left(\kappa_ {j}; \hat {\kappa} _ {j}, \nu_ {\kappa_ {j}}\right); & o t h e r w i s e \end{array} \right. \tag {70}
$$

where

$$
\nu_ {\kappa_ {j}} = \left(\sum_ {b} \frac {1}{\overrightarrow {\nu} _ {\kappa_ {j b}}}\right) ^ {- 1}, \tag {71}
$$

$$
\hat {\kappa} _ {j} = \nu_ {\kappa_ {j}} \sum_ {b} \frac {\vec {\kappa} _ {j b}}{\vec {\nu} _ {\kappa_ {j b}}}. \tag {72}
$$

2) backward message passing: we firstly compute the message  $m_{f_{h_j} \to h_j}(h_j)$  from the function node  $f_{h_j}$  to  $h_j$  by using the mf rule as follows

$$
\begin{array}{l} m _ {f _ {h _ {j}} \rightarrow h _ {j}} (h _ {j}) \propto e x p \left\{\int \ln f _ {h _ {j}} (h _ {j}, \lambda_ {j}) b (\lambda_ {j}) d \lambda_ {j} \right\} \\ \propto \mathcal {c n} \left(h _ {j}; 0, \hat {\lambda} _ {j} ^ {- 1}\right), \tag {73} \\ \end{array}
$$

where

$$
\hat {\lambda} _ {j} = \int \lambda_ {j} b \left(\lambda_ {j}\right) d \lambda_ {j} = \frac {\epsilon_ {j} + 1}{\eta_ {j} + \left| \hat {h} _ {j} \right| ^ {2} + \nu_ {h _ {j}}}. \tag {74}
$$

then the message  $n_{h_j\to f_{c_{jb}}}(h_j)$  from variable node  $h_j$  to  $f_{c_{jb}}$  is updated by the bp rule, i.e.,

$$
n _ {h _ {j} \rightarrow f _ {c _ {j b}}} (h _ {j}) = \frac {b (h _ {j})}{m _ {f _ {c _ {j b}} \rightarrow h _ {j}}} \propto \mathcal {c n} \left(h _ {j}; \overleftarrow {h} _ {j b}, \overleftarrow {\nu} _ {h _ {j b}}\right), \tag {75}
$$

where

$$
\stackrel {\leftarrow} {\nu} _ {h _ {j b}} = \left(\frac {1}{\nu_ {h _ {j}}} - \frac {1}{\stackrel {\rightarrow} {\nu} _ {h _ {j b}}}\right) ^ {- 1}, \tag {76}
$$

$$
\overleftarrow {h} _ {j b} = \overleftarrow {\nu} _ {h _ {j b}} \left(\frac {\hat {h} _ {j}}{\nu_ {h _ {j}}} - \frac {\vec {h} _ {j b}}{\vec {\nu} _ {h _ {j b}}}\right). \tag {77}
$$

the backward message  $n_{\kappa_j \to f_{g_{jb}}(\kappa_j)}$  is gaussian with mean  $\stackrel{\leftarrow}{\kappa}_{jb}$  and variance  $\stackrel{\leftarrow}{\nu}_{\kappa_{jb}}$  and can be calculated as

$$
\stackrel {\leftarrow} {\nu} _ {\kappa_ {j b}} = \left(1 / \nu_ {\kappa_ {j}} - 1 / \vec {\nu} _ {\kappa_ {j b}}\right) ^ {- 1}, \tag {78}
$$

$$
\stackrel {\leftarrow} {\kappa} _ {j b} = \stackrel {\leftarrow} {\nu} _ {\kappa_ {j b}} \left(\hat {\kappa} _ {j} / \nu_ {\kappa_ {j}} - \vec {\kappa} _ {j b} / \vec {\nu} _ {\kappa_ {j b}}\right). \tag {79}
$$

for the cases that  $\hat{\kappa}_j = 0.5$  or  $-0.5$  and  $\nu_{\kappa_j} = 0$  in (70), we set

$$
\stackrel {\leftarrow} {\nu} _ {\kappa_ {j b}} = \nu_ {\kappa_ {j}}, \quad \stackrel {\leftarrow} {\kappa} _ {j b} = \hat {\kappa} _ {j}. \tag {80}
$$

then the message  $m_{f_{g_{jb}}\to g_{jb}}(g_{jb})$  is calculated by using the bp rule, i.e.,

$$
\begin{array}{l} m _ {f _ {g _ {j b}} \rightarrow g _ {j b}} (g _ {j b}) = \int f _ {g _ {j b}} (g _ {j b}, \kappa_ {j}) n _ {\kappa_ {j} \rightarrow f _ {g _ {j b}}} (\kappa_ {j}) d \kappa_ {j} \\ \propto \mathcal {c n} \left(g _ {j b}; \stackrel {\leftarrow} {g} _ {j b}, \stackrel {\leftarrow} {\nu} _ {g _ {j b}}\right), \tag {81} \\ \end{array}
$$

where

$$
\overleftarrow {g} _ {j b} = \phi \left(q, \hat {\kappa} _ {j} ^ {\prime}\right) + \phi^ {\prime} \left(q, \hat {\kappa} _ {j} ^ {\prime}\right) \left(\bar {\kappa} _ {j b} - \hat {\kappa} _ {j} ^ {\prime}\right), \tag {82}
$$

$$
\overleftarrow {\nu} _ {g _ {j b}} = \overleftarrow {\nu} _ {\kappa_ {j b}} \left| \phi^ {\prime} \left(q, \hat {\kappa} _ {j} ^ {\prime}\right) \right| ^ {2}. \tag {83}
$$

hence we can obtain the belief  $b(g_{jb}) \propto \mathcal{cn}(g_{jb};\hat{g}_{jb},\nu_{g_{jb}})$  with

$$
\nu_ {g _ {j b}} = \left(\frac {1}{\overleftarrow {\nu} _ {g _ {j b}}} + \frac {1}{\overrightarrow {\nu} _ {g _ {j b}}}\right) ^ {- 1}, \tag {84}
$$

$$
\hat {g} _ {j b} = \nu_ {g _ {j b}} \left(\frac {\overleftarrow {g} _ {j b}}{\overleftarrow {\nu} _ {g _ {j b}}} + \frac {\overrightarrow {g} _ {j b}}{\overrightarrow {\nu} _ {g _ {j b}}}\right). \tag {85}
$$

finally, by combining the incoming message  $n_{g_{jb} \to f_{c_{jb}}} (g_{jb}) = m_{f_{g_{jb}} \to g_{jb}} (g_{jb})$  and  $n_{h_j \to f_{c_{jb}}} (h_j) \propto \mathcal{cn}(h_j; \overleftarrow{h}_{jb}, \overleftarrow{\nu}_{h_{jb}})$ , the message  $m_{f_{c_{jb}} \to c_{jb}} (c_{jb})$  is gaussian with mean  $\overleftarrow{c}_{jb}$  and variance  $\overleftarrow{\nu}_{c_{jb}}$ , which are computed as [27]

$$
\overleftarrow {c} _ {j b} = \overleftarrow {h} _ {j b} \overleftarrow {g} _ {j b}, \tag {86}
$$

$$
\stackrel {\leftarrow} {\nu} _ {c _ {j b}} = \left| \stackrel {\leftarrow} {h} _ {j b} \right| ^ {2} + \left| \stackrel {\leftarrow} {g} _ {j b} \right| ^ {2} \stackrel {\leftarrow} {\nu} _ {h _ {j b}} + \stackrel {\leftarrow} {\nu} _ {h _ {j b}}. \tag {87}
$$

the message passing algorithm is summarized in algorithm 1. the algorithm can be terminated when it reaches a maximum number of iteration or the difference between the estimates of parameters of two consecutive iterations is less than a threshold.

# c. extension to rectangular waveform

the derivation of algorithm 1 in the above is for otfs with the bi-orthogonal waveform. thanks to the iterative process of the algorithm, it can be readily extended for otfs with the rectangular waveform. as shown in (27), the difference between  $\mathbf{x}_{bi}$  and  $\mathbf{x}_{rect}$  is that there is a doppler shift-dependent factor for each of the elements in  $\mathbf{x}_{rect}$ , and the parameter  $\kappa_{n}$  is unknown. here we note that when  $z$  and  $n$  are given, the values of  $l_{z}$  and  $d_{n}$  are known.

this problem can be solved by taking advantage of the iterative estimation strategy. we can start the iterative process with initialization  $\kappa_{n} = 0$  and treat  $x_{rect}$  as a known matrix with the estimated doppler shifts in last iteration plugged in (27) (here we abuse the use of the notation  $x_{rect}$ , which is actually an estimate of the true  $x_{rect}$  with the estimate of  $\kappa_{n}$ ). then the algorithm developed for the otfs with the bi-orthogonal waveform can be used to recover  $\pmb{c}$ , i.e.,  $\kappa_{d}$  is estimated, so that  $\kappa_{n}$  can be found based on the corresponding  $\kappa_{d}$ . then with the estimated  $\kappa_{n}$ ,  $x_{rect}$  is updated for the next round iteration. hence, we only need to add an extra step after step 11 of algorithm 1 to update  $x_{rect}$  for otfs with the rectangular waveform.

# v. cramer-rao lower bound

to evaluate the performance of the proposed algorithm, we derive the crlb for the channel gain and doppler shift estimation. we assume that the locations of the nonzero elements in  $c$  are known to facilitate the derivation of the crlb. hence, the crlb derived in this section is a loose performance bound for the estimation.

we firstly rewrite (9) and (25) as

$$
y [ k, l ] = u [ k, l ] + \omega [ k, l ], \tag {88}
$$

where

$$
\begin{array}{l} u [ k, l ] = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {p} \sum_ {q = - \hat {n}} ^ {\hat {n}} h _ {i} f (q, \kappa_ {i}) e ^ {- j 2 \pi \frac {l _ {i} \left(k _ {i} + \kappa_ {i}\right)}{m n}} \\ \times x ([ k - k _ {i} + q ] _ {n}, [ l - l _ {i} ] _ {m}) \tag {89} \\ \end{array}
$$

for the bi-orthogonal waveform, and

$$
\begin{array}{l} u [ k, l ] = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {p} \sum_ {q = - \hat {n}} ^ {\hat {n}} h _ {i} f (q, \kappa_ {i}) e ^ {j 2 \pi \frac {(l - l _ {i}) (k _ {i} + \kappa_ {i})}{m n}} \\ \times x ([ k - k _ {i} + q ] _ {n}, [ l - l _ {i} ] _ {m}) \tag {90} \\ \end{array}
$$

for the rectangular waveform. in order to derive the crlb, we define  $\pmb{\theta} = [h_1,h_p,\dots ,h_p,\kappa_1,\kappa_p,\dots ,\kappa_p]^t$  . according to [28], the crlb of the  $j$  th element in  $\pmb{\theta}$  is the  $j$  th diagonal

element of the inverse of the fisher information matrix, i.e.,

$$
\theta_ {j} ^ {c r l b} = \left[ \boldsymbol {i} ^ {- 1} (\boldsymbol {\theta}) \right] _ {j j}, \tag {91}
$$

where the fisher information matrix  $\pmb{i}(\pmb{\theta})$  has a size of  $2p\times 2p$  with the  $(i,j)$ th element given as

$$
\left[ \boldsymbol {i} (\boldsymbol {\theta}) \right] _ {i j} = - \mathbb {e} \left[ \frac {\partial^ {2} \ln p (\boldsymbol {y} ; \boldsymbol {\theta})}{\partial \theta_ {i} \partial \theta_ {j}} \right] \tag {92}
$$

for  $i = 1,2,\dots ,2p$ $j = 1,2,\dots ,2p$  , and the expectation is taken with respect to  $p(\pmb {y};\pmb {\theta})$  . the logarithm of the likelihood function  $\ln p(\pmb {y};\pmb {\theta})$  can be expressed as

$$
\ln p (\boldsymbol {y}; \boldsymbol {\theta}) = - z \ln \left(\pi \gamma^ {- 1}\right) - \gamma \sum_ {z = 0} ^ {z - 1} \left| y _ {z} - u _ {z} \right| ^ {2}, \tag {93}
$$

where  $y_{z}$  denotes the  $z$ th element in  $\pmb{y}$  and  $u_{z}$  denotes the corresponding  $u[k,l]$ . then, (92) can be rewritten as

$$
[ \boldsymbol {i} (\boldsymbol {\theta}) ] _ {i j} = \gamma \sum_ {z = 0} ^ {z - 1} \left[ - \frac {\partial u _ {z}}{\partial \theta_ {i}} \frac {\partial u _ {z} ^ {*}}{\partial \theta_ {j}} - \frac {\partial u _ {z} ^ {*}}{\partial \theta_ {i}} \frac {\partial u _ {z}}{\partial \theta_ {j}} \right]. \tag {94}
$$

for simplicity, we define  $k' \triangleq [k - k_p + q]_n$  and  $l' \triangleq [l - l_p]_m$ . when  $1 \leq p \leq p$  ( $p$  represents  $i$  or  $j$ ), the derivative  $\frac{\partial u_z}{\partial \theta_p}$  is about the channel gain  $h_p$ . for the bi-orthogonal waveform, we have

$$
\frac {\partial u _ {z}}{\partial h _ {p}} = \sum_ {q = - \widehat {n}} ^ {\widehat {n}} f (q, \kappa_ {p}) e ^ {- j 2 \pi \frac {l _ {p} \left(k _ {p} + \kappa_ {p}\right)}{m n}} x \left[ k ^ {\prime}, l ^ {\prime} \right], \tag {95}
$$

while for the rectangular waveform we have

$$
\frac {\partial u _ {z}}{\partial h _ {p}} = \sum_ {q = - \widehat {n}} ^ {\widehat {n}} f (q, \kappa_ {p}) e ^ {j 2 \pi \frac {(l - l _ {p}) (k _ {p} + \kappa_ {p})}{m n}} x \left[ k ^ {\prime}, l ^ {\prime} \right]. \tag {96}
$$

when  $p < p' \leq 2p$  ( $p'$  represents  $i$  or  $j$ ), the derivative  $\frac{\partial u_z}{\partial \theta_{p'}}$  is about  $\kappa_p$ , where  $p = p' - p$ . for the bi-orthogonal waveform, we have

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {\partial u _ {z}}{\partial \kappa_ {p}} = \sum_ {q = - \widehat {n}} ^ {\widehat {n}} h _ {p} \left[ \left(\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {n - 1} j \frac {2 n \pi}{n} e ^ {j \frac {2 n \pi}{n} (q + \kappa_ {p})}\right) e ^ {- j 2 \pi \frac {l _ {p} (k _ {p} + \kappa_ {p})}{m n}} \right. \\ \left. + f (q, \kappa_ {p}) \left(e ^ {- j 2 \pi \frac {l _ {p} \left(k _ {i} + \kappa_ {p}\right)}{m n}} - \frac {j 2 \pi l _ {p}}{m n}\right) \right] x [ k ^ {\prime}, l ^ {\prime} ], \tag {97} \\ \end{array}
$$

while for the rectangular waveform

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {\partial u _ {z}}{\partial \kappa_ {p}} = \sum_ {q = - \widehat {n}} ^ {\widehat {n}} h _ {p} \left[ \left(\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {n - 1} j \frac {2 n \pi}{n} e ^ {j \frac {2 n \pi}{n} (q + \kappa_ {p})}\right) e ^ {j 2 \pi \frac {(l - l _ {p}) (k _ {p} + \kappa_ {p})}{m n}} \right. \\ \left. + f (q, \kappa_ {p}) \left(e ^ {j 2 \pi \frac {(l - l _ {p}) (k _ {p} + \kappa_ {p})}{m n}} \frac {j 2 \pi (l - l _ {p})}{m n}\right) \right] x \left[ k ^ {\prime}, l ^ {\prime} \right]. \tag {98} \\ \end{array}
$$

to evaluate the performance of the proposed algorithm in terms of the normalized mean squared error (nmse), we use the average normalized crlb for the estimation of channel gains and fractional doppler shifts, which are defined as

$$
\hat {\boldsymbol {h}} _ {\text {b o u n d}} = \frac {\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {p} \theta_ {j} ^ {c r l b}}{| | \boldsymbol {h} _ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} | | _ {2} ^ {2}}, \tag {99}
$$

$$
\hat {\boldsymbol {\kappa}} _ {\text {b o u n d}} = \frac {\sum_ {j = p + 1} ^ {2 p} \theta_ {j} ^ {c r l b}}{| | \boldsymbol {\kappa} _ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} | | _ {2} ^ {2}}, \tag {100}
$$

where  $\pmb{h}_{\pmb{\theta}} = (h_{1}, h_{2}, \dots, h_{p})^{t}$  and  $\pmb{\kappa}_{\pmb{\theta}} = (\kappa_{1}, \kappa_{2}, \dots, \kappa_{p})^{t}$ .

# vi. simulation results

in this section, we evaluate the performance of the proposed message passing algorithm in terms of the nmse of the estimated channel parameters  $\hat{\pmb{h}}$  and  $\hat{\kappa}$ , and the reconstructed channel matrices  $\widehat{h}_{bi}$  and  $\widehat{h}_{rect}$ . the nmse is defined as

$$
n m s e (\boldsymbol {x}) = \frac {1 / l \sum_ {l = 1} ^ {l} \left\| \hat {\boldsymbol {x}} _ {l} - \boldsymbol {x} \right\| _ {2} ^ {2}}{\left\| \boldsymbol {x} \right\| _ {2} ^ {2}}, \tag {101}
$$

where  $\hat{x}_l$  denotes the estimate of a variable  $x$  in the  $l$ th trial, and  $l$  is the number of trials. in addition, the bit error rate (ber) of data detection based on the reconstructed channel matrix is also evaluated. we set  $m = 128$  and  $n = 32$ , i.e., there are 32 time slots and 128 subcarriers in the tf domain. the carrier frequency is  $3\mathrm{ghz}$ , the subcarrier spacing is  $2\mathrm{khz}$ , and the quadrature phase shift keying (qpsk) is adopted for modulation. the velocity of the mobile user is set to be  $v = 120km / h$ , leading to a maximum doppler frequency shift index  $k_{max} = 4$ . we assume that the maximum delay index  $l_{max} = 10$ , the doppler index of the  $i$ th path is uniformly distributed over  $[-k_{max}, k_{max}]$ , and the delay index is uniformly distributed over  $[1, l_{max}]$ , excluding the first path  $(l_i = 0)$ . as mentioned before, the fractional doppler  $\kappa_i$  has a uniform distribution over  $[-0.5, 0.5]$ . the channel path gains  $\{h_j\}$  are independently drawn from the complex gaussian distribution  $\mathcal{n}(0, 1 / p)$ . furthermore, we define the snr of pilot and data as

$$
\mathrm {s n r p} = 1 0 \log 1 0 \left(\frac {\mathcal {p} _ {p}}{\gamma^ {- 1}}\right), \tag {102}
$$

$$
\mathrm {s n r d} = 1 0 \log 1 0 \left(\frac {\mathbb {e} \left| x _ {d} \right| ^ {2}}{\gamma^ {- 1}}\right), \tag {103}
$$

respectively, where  $\mathcal{p}_p$  denotes the average power of pilot symbols.
