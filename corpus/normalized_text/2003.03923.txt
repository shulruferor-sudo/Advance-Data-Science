# deconfounded image captioning: a causal retrospect

xu yang, hanwang zhang, and jianfei cai

abstract—dataset bias in vision-language tasks is becoming one of the main problems which hinders the progress of our community. existing solutions lack a principled analysis about why modern image captioners easily collapse into dataset bias. in this paper, we present a novel perspective: deconfounded image captioning (dic), to find out the answer of this question, then retrospect modern neural image captioners, and finally propose a dic framework: dicv1.0 to alleviate the negative effects brought by dataset bias. dic is based on causal inference, whose two principles: the backdoor and front-door adjustments, help us review previous studies and design new effective models. in particular, we showcase that dicv1.0 can strengthen two prevailing captioning models and can achieve a single-model 131.1 cider-d and 128.4 c40 cider-d on karpathy split and online split of the challenging ms coco dataset, respectively. interestingly, dicv1.0 is a natural derivation from our causal retrospect, which opens promising directions for image captioning.

index terms—image captioning, causality, deconfounding, the backdoor adjustment, the front-door adjustment.

# 1 introduction

recently, our vision-language community has paid more attention to dataset bias. on one hand, it shows that we have already achieved impressive or even super-human performances on many benchmark leader-boards. on the other hand, it also shows that we do not actually believe in these "super-human" systems due to the obvious dataset bias, e.g., image captioners are likely to imply gender discrimination [1] (e.g., generating "a man" instead of "a woman" even through the image actually shows "a woman is snowboarding") and hallucinate what they do not see [2] (e.g., generating "sitting on the bench" when only seeing people talking on the phone); vqa agents usually answer "yes" when being asked "are the apples red?" even without a look at the image [3].

the ideal solution to eliminate dataset bias is to collect a perfect and totally balanced dataset. however, since humans are living in a world filled with various biases, e.g., long-tailed concept distributions [4], [5], reporting bias [6], [7], and language bias [8], [9], we are easily trapped in the loop: "making a dataset"-it's biased"-“making a new one”<sup>1</sup>. promisingly, by observing that our biological vision-language system works well even though the around real word is biased, we are prompted to evolve from diagnosis to treatment, by changing from collecting new datasets [3], [10], [11], [12] to designing unbiased models [1], [13] for confronting the bias. however, recent studies still lack a principled analysis about why a vision-language learning system easily collapses into dataset bias. in this paper, we exploit the

extending this toy example to the ic case, with a similar basic causal graph shown in fig. 2, we denote  $\mathcal{d}$  as a confounder due to dataset biases,  $\mathcal{x}$  as image features to be learned and  $\mathcal{l}$  as the resultant caption. in particular, we can treat the set of some frequently appearing concepts  $\mathcal{d}$  in the training dataset as the confounder. on one hand, the more images of these concepts that a network is trained with, the more the learned representation  $\mathcal{x}$  is affected. thus, we have the causal effect  $\mathcal{d} \rightarrow \mathcal{x}$ . on the other hand, these concepts affect the frequency of some related words in the captions  $\mathcal{l}$ , e.g., the most preferred concept  $id_{\text{person}}$  in ms coco largely increases the frequency of the words "woman", "man", and some human related actions "sit", "stand". thus, these concepts affect the captions and we have  $\mathcal{d} \rightarrow \mathcal{l}$ . when we use the dataset to train a captioner, although we hope to learn the true causal effect  $\mathcal{x} \rightarrow \mathcal{l}$ , the captioner is likely to learn some spurious correlation between  $\mathcal{x}$  and  $\mathcal{l}$  due to the confounder  $\mathcal{d}$ , i.e., overexploiting the co-occurrence between the recognized concepts and some words to generate the biased captions. for example, as in fig. 2 (c), after recognizing the concept  $id_{\text{board}}$  through  $\mathcal{x} \leftarrow \mathcal{d}$ , the captioner will exploit the higher co-occurrence between  $id_{\text{board}}$  with "man" through  $\mathcal{d} \rightarrow \mathcal{l}$  to generate a wrong word.

so far, we have shown that  $\mathcal{d}$  is a confounder and in section 2.1 we will further show that if the observational probability  $p(\mathcal{l}|\mathcal{x})$  is used as the training target, the captioner is likely to learn the spurious correlation. the rest is how to deconfound it — deconfounded image captioning (dic) — which is our goal in this paper. a principled solution is to apply a new

# 2 preliminaries: deconfounding

we hope to train an image captioner to learn the true causal effect  $\mathcal{x} \to \mathcal{l}$ : the captioner should reason the caption  $\mathcal{l}$  from the image features  $\mathcal{x}$  instead of exploiting the spurious correlations induced by the confounder  $\mathcal{d}$ . in this section, we review two main deconfounding techniques which help us achieve this goal: the backdoor and front-door adjustments [14], [15].

# 2.1 the backdoor adjustment

we first use the digit classifier example to illustrate that the observational probability  $p(\mathcal{l}|\mathcal{x})$  often encourages the classifier to learn spurious correlation. by bayes' theorem, we have

$$
p (\mathcal {l} | \mathcal {x}) = \sum_ {p} p (\mathcal {l} | \mathcal {x}, p) p (p | \mathcal {x}), \tag {1}
$$

where  $p$  denotes the position of the black pixels, which is either "left" or "right". now assuming that  $\mathcal{x}_0$  denotes the feature of an image containing "0" with label  $\mathcal{l}_0$  and, by eq. (1), we obtain:

$$
\begin{array}{l} p (\mathcal {l} _ {0} | \mathcal {x} _ {0}) \\ = p \left(\mathcal {l} _ {0} \mid \mathcal {x} _ {0}, \text {l e f t}\right) p \left(\text {l e f t} \mid \mathcal {x} _ {0}\right) + p \left(\mathcal {l} _ {0} \mid \mathcal {x} _ {0}, \text {r i g h t}\right) p \left(\text {r i g h t} \mid \mathcal {x} _ {0}\right). \tag {2} \\ \end{array}
$$

due to the position bias in the dataset, we have  $p(left|\mathcal{x}_0) \gg p(right|\mathcal{x}_0)$  and thus

$$
p \left(\mathcal {l} _ {0} \mid \mathcal {x} _ {0}\right) \approx p \left(\mathcal {l} _ {0} \mid \mathcal {x} _ {0}, l e f t\right) p (l e f t \mid \mathcal {x} _ {0}) \approx p \left(\mathcal {l} _ {0} \mid l e f t\right) p (l e f t \mid \mathcal {x} _ {0}). \tag {3}
$$

we have the second approximation in eq. (3) because  $\mathcal{x}_0$  is learnable and the connection between "left" and  $\mathcal{l}_0$  is strong. maximizing  $p(\mathcal{l}_0|\mathcal{x}_0)$  over the training set are likely to encourage  $\mathcal{x}_0$  to converge to the position feature, since recognizing the position is easier than recognizing the digit shape, which is also referred as short-cut learning [26]. in this way, the classifier learns the spurious correlation induced by the backdoor path  $\mathcal{x} \gets \mathcal{p} \rightarrow \mathcal{l}$ .

the backdoor adjustment calculates the interventional distribution  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{x}))$  by revising the observational distribution in eq. (1) as:

$$
p (\mathcal {l} | d o (\mathcal {x})) = \sum_ {p} p (\mathcal {l} | \mathcal {x}, p) p (p). \tag {4}
$$

by comparing eq. (1) and eq. (4), we can find that  $p(p|\mathcal{x})$  becomes  $p(p)$ , which means that the position is not affected by  $\mathcal{x}$  while being intervened to be "left" or "right" regardless of  $\mathcal{x}$ . assuming  $p(p = left) = p(p = right) = 0.5$ , for the input  $\mathcal{x}_0$ , eq. (4) becomes

$$
p \left(\mathcal {l} _ {0} | d o \left(\mathcal {x} _ {0}\right)\right) = \frac {1}{2} \sum_ {p} p \left(\mathcal {l} _ {0} \mid \mathcal {x} _ {0}, p\right). \tag {5}
$$

although the probability of having  $(\mathcal{x}_0, p = right)$  in the training set (say  $10\%$  or less) is much smaller than that of  $(\mathcal{x}_0, p = left)$ , it will not be ignored in eq. (5) and maximizing eq. (5) will encourage  $\mathcal{x}_0$  to learn something that is different from the position feature and can lead to  $\mathcal{l}_0$ .

in the ic case, almost all the modern captioners are trained by the observational distribution  $p(\mathcal{l}|\mathcal{x})$ , where  $\mathcal{x}$  are usually based the features extracted from visual backbones [27]. various spurious correlations have been observed in the existing captioners as shown in fig. 2. treating the frequently appearing concepts as the confounder  $\mathcal{d}$ , we can decompose  $p(\mathcal{l}|\mathcal{x})$  as:

$$
p (\mathcal {l} | \mathcal {x}) = \sum_ {d} p (\mathcal {l} | \mathcal {x}, d) p (d | \mathcal {x}), \tag {6}
$$

where  $d$  is a concept id in  $\mathcal{d}$ , e.g.,  $id_{\text{apple}}$  or  $id_{\text{banana}}$ . since a captioning dataset usually contains more samples about certain concepts than the others, e.g., ms coco contains 80 frequently appearing objects [28], [29] and thus these objects are the more preferred concepts than the others, especially the attribute or relation concepts like  $id_{\text{green}}$ ,  $id_{\text{small}}$ ,  $id_{\text{sit}}$ , or  $id_{\text{ride}}$ .

then during training, it is much easier for the visual encoders to recognize the objects and neglect the objects' attributes or relations. after recognizing these objects, the captioner might only learn to predict the attributes or relations by their co-occurrences with those preferred concepts instead of really reasoning from the images. for example, if a dataset contains much more "red apple" than "green apple", after recognizing  $id_{\text{apple}}$ , which means that  $p(id_{\text{apple}}|\mathcal{x}) \approx 1$ , eq. (6) could degrade to  $p(\mathcal{l}|id_{\text{apple}})$  to make the captioner build strong connection between the colour "red" with the concept  $id_{\text{apple}}$  without seeing the real colour. fig. 2 shows three examples, where  $\mathcal{x} \gets \mathcal{d}$  in the causal graph denotes the recognizing of some preferred concepts and  $\mathcal{d} \to \mathcal{l}$  denotes the learning or exploiting of the co-occurrence.

to deconfound the image captioning, we can also use the backdoor adjustment as eq. (4) to split  $\mathcal{d}$  into different concepts and calculate the average causal effects at these concepts:

$$
p (\mathcal {l} | d o (\mathcal {x})) = \sum_ {d} p (\mathcal {l} | \mathcal {x}, d) p (d). \tag {7}
$$

then if we train our captioner by maximizing  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{x}))$ , the

![](images/426d456ecd7cfd7795a86e3ccd0e53ce1b2d11e731becb53400cd48a0c1f303d.jpg)  
fig. 3. the toy example of using the front-door adjustment to solve the biased 0-vs-1 problem. the front-door graph requires an additional mediator  $\mathcal{z}$  to divide the classification process into two parts: a mediator extractor  $\mathcal{x} \to \mathcal{z}$  and a mediator classifier  $\mathcal{z} \to \mathcal{l}$ . to deconfound this front-door based digit classifier, we should chain together the causal effects of two parts:  $p(\mathcal{z}|do(\mathcal{x}))$  and  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{z}))$  by blocking two backdoor paths:  $\mathcal{x} \gets \mathcal{p} \to \mathcal{l} \gets \mathcal{z}$  and  $\mathcal{z} \gets \mathcal{x} \gets \mathcal{p} \to \mathcal{l}$ , respectively. the first one is already blocked due to the collider at  $\mathcal{l}$  and the second one can be blocked by cutting off the link  $\mathcal{x} \to \mathcal{z}$ .

captioner will learn to maximize  $p(\mathcal{l}|\mathcal{x},d)$  at every concept instead of just one  $id_{\mathrm{apple}}$ , e.g., learning  $\mathcal{l} = \text{green}$  from  $id_{\mathrm{banana}}$  as well. in this way, the captioner is encouraged to build more connections between  $\mathcal{x}$  and  $\mathcal{l}$ , i.e., reasoning the caption from the image.

# 2.2 the front-door adjustment

in eq. (7), we have seen that the backdoor adjustment requires us to split  $\mathcal{d}$  into different levels. in other words, we need to know what the confounder is in advance in order to apply the backdoor adjustment. however, in real situations, dataset biases could be very complex and it is hard to know and disentangle different types of confounders. for example, in ic, it is an oversimplification to split  $\mathcal{d}$  into individual concepts since dataset biases are not only brought by each single concept independently but more likely by the combinations of different concepts, e.g., relation words are affected by both subject and object concepts, for which a sufficient split requires the combinational complexity. more seriously, since researchers usually throw away the pre-training dataset and only use the pre-trained model, they have no idea which concepts appeared more frequently. thus, it is almost impossible for us to get a reasonable split of  $\mathcal{d}$  to apply the backdoor adjustment.

fortunately, the front-door adjustment [15] can also be used to calculate  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{x}))$  when we cannot split  $\mathcal{d}$ . here we still use the position-biased digit classification in fig. 1 as the toy example to demonstrate the basic ideas of the front-door adjustment, and then briefly discuss our ic case. as shown in fig. 3(a), to apply the front-door adjustment, an additional mediator  $\mathcal{z}$  should be inserted between  $\mathcal{x}$  and  $\mathcal{l}$  to construct a front-door path  $\mathcal{x} \to \mathcal{z} \to \mathcal{l}$  for helping transmit knowledge.

here we treat "the shape" as the mediator  $\mathcal{z}$  in this digit classifier and then this classifier is divided into two parts: a shape extractor  $\mathcal{x} \to \mathcal{z}$  and a shape classifier  $\mathcal{z} \to \mathcal{l}$ . then for the conditional probability  $p(\mathcal{l}|\mathcal{x})$ , we can decompose it by chaining together two parts' conditional probabilities:

$$
p (\mathcal {l} | \mathcal {x}) = \sum_ {z} p (\mathcal {z} = z | \mathcal {x}) p (\mathcal {l} | \mathcal {z} = z). \tag {8}
$$

similarly, we can decompose the interventional probability  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{x}))$  as:

$$
p (\mathcal {l} | d o (\mathcal {x})) = \sum_ {\boldsymbol {z}} p (\mathcal {z} = \boldsymbol {z} | d o (\mathcal {x})) p (\mathcal {l} | d o (\mathcal {z} = \boldsymbol {z})). \tag {9}
$$

next, we compare the conditional and interventional probabilities of these two parts to see whether they are the same or not and why the biases are induced.

shape extractor  $\mathcal{x}\rightarrow \mathcal{z}$ . as shown in the blue part of fig. 3(b), for the causal link  $\mathcal{x}\rightarrow \mathcal{z}$ , due to the collider at  $\mathcal{l}$ , the backdoor path between  $\mathcal{x}$  and  $\mathcal{z}$ :  $\mathcal{x}\gets \mathcal{p}\to \mathcal{l}\gets \mathcal{z}$  is already blocked. thus the interventional probability is equal to the conditional one:

$$
p (\mathcal {z} = \boldsymbol {z} | d o (\mathcal {x})) = p (\mathcal {z} = \boldsymbol {z} | \mathcal {x}). \tag {10}
$$

the details about why the collider at  $\mathcal{l}$  blocks this backdoor path is given in section a.2.3 of the supplementary material. for simplicity, we assume this extractor is perfect that it extracts the shape "circle" from digit "0" and "line" from "1".

shape classifier  $\mathcal{z} \to \mathcal{l}$ . as shown in the green part of fig. 3(b), the backdoor path between  $\mathcal{z}$  and  $\mathcal{l}$ :  $\mathcal{z} \gets \mathcal{x} \gets \mathcal{p} \to \mathcal{l}$  is not naturally blocked, which means the shape classifier is affected by the confounder  $\mathcal{p}$ . to clearly see how  $\mathcal{p}$  affects this classifier, we should expand its conditional distribution  $p(\mathcal{l}|\mathcal{z})$  as:

$$
\begin{array}{l} p (\mathcal {l} | \mathcal {z} = z) = \sum_ {\boldsymbol {x}} \sum_ {p} p (\mathcal {l}, \boldsymbol {x}, p | z) \\ = \sum_ {\boldsymbol {x}} \sum_ {p} p (\mathcal {l} | \boldsymbol {x}, p, z) p (\boldsymbol {x}, p | z) \tag {11} \\ = \sum_ {\boldsymbol {x}} \sum_ {p} p (\mathcal {l} | \boldsymbol {x}, p, z) p (p | \boldsymbol {x}, z) p (\boldsymbol {x} | z) \\ = \sum_ {\boldsymbol {x}} \sum_ {p} p (\mathcal {l} | p, z) p (p | \boldsymbol {x}) p (\boldsymbol {x} | z). \\ \end{array}
$$

now assuming the shape is  $\mathcal{z} =$  circle and the corresponding label is  $\mathcal{l} = \mathcal{l}_0$  ..

$$
p \left(\mathcal {l} _ {0} \mid \text {c i r c l e}\right) = \sum_ {\boldsymbol {x}} \sum_ {p} p \left(\mathcal {l} _ {0} \mid p, \text {c i r c l e}\right) p (p \mid \boldsymbol {x}) p (\boldsymbol {x} \mid \text {c i r c l e}). \tag {12}
$$

assuming the perfect situation,  $p(\mathcal{x}_0|\mathrm{circle}) = 1$  and  $p(\mathcal{x}_1|\mathrm{circle}) = 0$ , with the position bias,  $p(\mathrm{left}|\mathcal{x}_0) \gg p(\mathrm{right}|\mathcal{x}_0)$ , we have:

$$
\begin{array}{l} p \left(\mathcal {l} _ {0} \mid \mathcal {z} = \text {c i r c l e}\right) \\ \approx \sum_ {\boldsymbol {x} \in \mathcal {x} _ {0}} p \left(\mathcal {l} _ {0} | \text {l e f t}, \text {c i r c l e}\right) p (\text {l e f t} | \boldsymbol {x}) p (\boldsymbol {x} | \text {c i r c l e}) \tag {13} \\ \approx p \left(\mathcal {l} _ {0} | \text {l e f t}, \text {c i r c l e}\right). \\ \end{array}
$$

for convenience, we use  $\mathcal{x}_0$  to denote the feature space of images with "0" and  $x \in \mathcal{x}_0$  to denote the feature  $x$  from this space. with this shape extractor, the classifier will predict from both "left" and "circle", which is more robust than the one without this shape mediator (eq. (3)). however, we still run into a similar situation as eq. (3) that if the classifier is trained by maximizing eq. (13), it does not know whether "circle" or "left" causes the digit "0". as a result, this classifier may be polluted by the position bias as the backdoor case. moreover, the shape extractor is also imperfect in practice. if the followed classifier learns the position bias, the ahead extractor could also be polluted. as a result, the whole system following  $\mathcal{x} \to \mathcal{z} \to \mathcal{l}$  might learn the position bias instead of the causal effect.

to address this, we can cut off  $\mathcal{z} \gets \mathcal{x}$  to block the backdoor path  $\mathcal{z} \gets \mathcal{x} \gets \mathcal{p} \to \mathcal{l}$ , as shown in the green part of fig. 3(b). then, similar to eq. (7), we have:

$$
p (\mathcal {l} | d o (\mathcal {z} = z)) = \sum_ {\boldsymbol {x}} p (\mathcal {l} | \boldsymbol {z}, \boldsymbol {x}) p (\boldsymbol {x}), \tag {14}
$$

where  $\pmb{x}$  represents all the possible representations in the feature space. in eq. (14), since  $\pmb{x}$  keeps changing while the shape knowledge does not, the classifier will more easily learn the causal effect, e.g., "circle" causes "0". once this classifier knows that "circle" is the true cause, the former extractor is also encouraged to extract "circle" from the images with "0". as a result, both extractor and classifier will learn the true causal effects.

4. given the observed  $\mathcal{p} = p$  and  $\mathcal{z} = z$ ,  $\mathcal{l}$  and  $\mathcal{x}$  are conditional independent:  $\mathcal{l} \perp \mathcal{x}|\mathcal{p}, \mathcal{z}$ , thus we have  $p(\mathcal{l}|\boldsymbol{x}, p, z) = p(\mathcal{l}|p, z)$ . also, since  $\mathcal{z} \perp \mathcal{p}|\mathcal{x}$ , we have  $p(p|\boldsymbol{x}, z) = p(p|\boldsymbol{x})$ . both of them establish the forth equation.

to sum up, by applying eq. (10) and (14) into eq. (9), we have the front-door adjustment as:

$$
p (\mathcal {l} | d o (\mathcal {x})) = \sum_ {\boldsymbol {z}} p (\boldsymbol {z} | \mathcal {x}) \sum_ {\boldsymbol {x}} p (\mathcal {l} | \boldsymbol {z}, \boldsymbol {x}) p (\boldsymbol {x}). \tag {15}
$$

in our ic case, as we will see in section 3.1, researchers have also built some front-door based captioners with different mediators, e.g., the prevailing visual attention mechanism [30] treats the features extracted from different positions as the mediator.

# 3 related work

# 3.1 a causal retrospect

we follow fig. 4 to retrospect the milestone image captioners proposed in recent six years from the causal view, which provides a novel perspective of understanding their contributions.

show & tell [21] (fig. 4(a)). compared with earlier template-based captioners like baby talk [31], show & tell is where we start to deploy deep neural network based encoder-decoder for captioning. also, since show & tell, almost all the following captioners are built on the pre-trained model to narrow the gap between vision and language, while also intensifying the "preferred concepts" problem and encouraging the model to learn the spurious correlation.

large-scale training (fig. 4(b)). a straightforward way to deconfound is to turn the confounder  $\mathcal{d}$  into a non-confounder, i.e., constructing an infinite and balanced dataset. in this way, the captioner can learn every possible visual-semantic pairs from the abundant training samples and as a result, the captioner will no longer collapse into biases. we can approximate this by scaling up the training data. many studies fall into this large-scale training strategy, e.g., up-down [24] exploited visual genome [32] with a larger label space, hip [33] used more fine-grained segmentation annotations, and vision-language bert frameworks [34], [35] trained their network by 3 million image-caption pairs from conceptual captions [12]. after training or pre-training with those large-scale datasets, these captioners' performances are boosted. however, it is still almost impossible to collect an infinite and perfect dataset where no bias exists since even the real world is essentially unbalanced. thus, large-scale training cannot completely deconfound the ic.

attention mechanisms (fig. 4(c)). sat [30] was the first work which applied the attention mechanism in ic, and then this mechanism was used in almost every follow-up ic systems [24], [25], [36], [37], [38], [39], [40], [41]. by this mechanism, captioners first scan over all the visual concepts of an image and then select suitable ones conditioned on the language context, e.g., spatial attention [24], [30] and semantic attention [36] selected the most informative visual features and semantic labels, to generate the captions. the attention mechanism is an approximation of both the backdoor and front-door adjustments. compared with the backdoor adjustment (eq. (7)), where  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{x}))$  averages over all the visual concepts of  $\mathcal{d}$ , while the attention mechanism only averages over the appearing visual concepts of the given image, which only construct a small subset of  $\mathcal{d}$ . thus  $\mathcal{d}$  is not totally deconfounded. from the perspective of the front-door adjustment, the attention mechanism treats the visual feature extracted from a specific position as the mediator  $\mathcal{z}$ . then the captioners generate the words by weighted averaging the visual features at the selected positions at different time steps. however, it uses the observational conditional probability  $p(\mathcal{l}|\mathcal{x})$  (eq. (8)) instead of  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{x}))$  (eq. (15)) to compute the word distribution, which does not

sentence patterns (fig. 4(d)). researchers also designed ics which imitate humans to dynamically structure sentence patterns for captioning. in particular, they used diverse modules for different patterns, e.g., nbt [42] designed two modules respectively for nouns and all the other words, and cnm [43] applied four fine-grained modules for nouns, adjectives, relation related words, and function words. during captioning, sentence patterns are dynamically composed for selecting suitable modules to generate the corresponding words. at first glance, this framework looks like a front-door ic where the mediator  $\mathcal{z}$  is sentence pattern. however, if we look closer, it can be discovered that since both the sentence patterns and the captions are learnt from the language resource of the dataset, both  $\mathcal{z}$  and  $\mathcal{l}$  are affected by the concepts  $\mathcal{d}$  in the language resource, e.g., the more nouns appear, the more object module is trained. however, due to this causal link  $\mathcal{d} \rightarrow \mathcal{z}$ , the front-door adjustment cannot be used and thus these captioners are still confounded.

structured attention (fig. 4(e)). when humans describe an image, they can build a semantic structure (e.g., scene graph) about this image and then turn this structure into the caption. inspired by this, researchers [44], [45], [46], [47] proposed to learn a scene graph from the image and to apply graph neural network [48], [49], [50] to transform this scene graph into a series of embeddings that each embedding corresponds to a sub-structure, e.g., a triplet structure like "subject-predicate-object". then the captioners dynamically attend to these sub-structure embeddings to generate the words. such a strategy is also widely applied in other vision-language tasks like vqa [51], [52], grounding [53], [54], and visual dialogue [55], e.g., [55] exploits the scene graph containing both visual and semantic knowledge of the image to complete the dialogue. these models also belong to the front-door causal graph where  $\mathcal{z}$  is the structure or the graph. however, such structures are usually affected by  $\mathcal{d}$ , e.g., the learning and predicting of the relation is also biased by some preferred concepts [56].

moreover, the applied scene graph parsers [57] are usually trained by vg [32], while vg is also used to pre-train these captioners. thus we also have the causal link  $\mathcal{d} \to \mathcal{z}$  and the front-door adjustment cannot be used. consequently, it is hard to deconfound such captioners and they are still encouraged to learn the spurious correlation.

# 3.2 causal inference in computer vision

recently, researchers have begun to exploit the techniques from causality [14], [58] to design novel methods for solving various learning tasks or develop new perspectives for rethinking certain learning problems. for example, disentangled causal mechanisms are exploited to design more robust disentanglement measurements [59], generate counterfactual samples [60], [61], and alleviate negative effects brought by different biases [62], [63], [64], [65], [66], [67]. specifically, both [64] and [65] assume that the confounder is observable and then they apply the backdoor adjustment to deconfound. in contrast, the confounder is not observable in our case. thus, our situation is more challenging and we exploit not only the backdoor but also the front-door adjustments for deconfounding. for [63], [66], they apply other causality techniques: natural indirect effect and total direct effect for eliminating the mediator effect instead of deconfounding.

# 3.3 group caption

group-based image captioning [68], [69] also exploits the other image-caption samples for helping generate accurate and discriminative captions, while they have three major differences compared with our research. first, their target is different from ours. they focus on group-based captioning, which requires the captioner to exploit the relevance and diversity knowledge from the other images, while our dic focuses on one-line (single image) captioning. second, due to the different aims, the problem settings are also different. specifically, their methods need to divide the whole captioning dataset into different image groups, e.g., images in the same group should have similar scene graphs, while our dic does not require such explicit image groups, but uses more implicit groups, e.g., the same group should contain similar visual concepts (in the backdoor adjustment) or similar visual features

(in the front-door adjustment). third, the technical details are different. both [68] and [69] exploit the relevance and diversity knowledge of the images from the intra-group and the inter-group, respectively. however, our dic does not explicitly distinguish the relevance and diversity knowledge, but only measures the average causal effect over each group.

# 4 deconfounded image captioning

in this section, we discuss how to derive our dicv1.0 from the causal retrospect in section 3.1. in section 4.1, we first discuss how to choose the mediator  $\mathcal{z}$  from three candidates, i.e., spatial position, sentence pattern, and graph structure. then we derive the interventional probability  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{x}))$  and discuss the two major challenges of calculating this probability in a deep network. both challenges are respectively addressed in section 4.2 and 4.3. lastly, we detail how to implement our dicv1.0 into the prevailing encoder-decoder framework in section 4.4.

# 4.1 choosing  $\mathcal{z}$

as discussed in section 2.2, since  $\mathcal{d}$  is very complex and almost unavailable, the backdoor adjustment cannot be deployed, we have to deconfound the ic by the front-door adjustment. to achieve this, the first challenge is the selection of the mediator  $\mathcal{z}$ . based on the retrospect in section 3.1, we know there are three candidates of  $\mathcal{z}$ : spatial position (in attention mechanism), sentence pattern, and some kind of the structure (in structured attention). for spatial position (fig. 4(c)), it only provides the visual concepts of the given image, which is a small subset of  $\mathcal{d}$ , and thus it is not a good candidate. for sentence pattern (fig. 4(d)), the calculation of its probability is almost impossible since it is decided by the whole caption, which is not available during the caption generation, so sentence pattern is also not a good candidate. for structured attention (fig. 4(e)), it is designed to be decided by the image only, while researchers [44], [45] learn the structure generator from the same dataset as the captioning, which adds the link  $\mathcal{d} \to \mathcal{z}$  into the causal graph and as the result, this ic is confounded. however, if we only associate the structure with  $\mathcal{x}$ , we have a causal graph which cuts off the link  $\mathcal{d} \to \mathcal{z}$  as fig. 4(f) and then we can apply the front-door adjustment.

to achieve this, we sample a commonsense structure set from conceptnet [70] and treat it as the mediator  $\mathcal{z}$ , e.g., "car-at location-road" and "apple-is-red". given such a commonsense structure "subject-predicate-object", we transform it into a semantic embedding  $z$ :

$$
\boldsymbol {z} = \operatorname {r e l u} \left(\mathrm {f c} \left(\left[ \boldsymbol {e} _ {s}, \boldsymbol {e} _ {p}, \boldsymbol {e} _ {o} \right]\right)\right), \tag {16}
$$

where  $e_s, e_p, e_o$  denote the trainable word embeddings of the subject, predicate, and object, respectively, and fc denotes the fully connected layer. compared with the original visual features, these semantic embeddings will naturally be more informative since they also belong to the semantic domain as the captions, which means that this commonsense structure set is a suitable candidate of the mediator. intuitively, when a captioner uses this commonsense structure as the mediator, this captioner will first use the visual features to retrieve the related semantic embeddings  $(\mathcal{x} \to \mathcal{z})$  and then exploit the retrieved embeddings to generate the caption  $(\mathcal{z} \to \mathcal{l})$ .

however, when we sample the related commonsense structures  $\mathcal{z}$ , we use the words from the caption vocabulary  $s$  as the keys (see section 5.1), e.g., "person" is used to collect the triplets

"person-related to-human" or "person-capable of-run". therefore, the keywords affect the collections of the triplets and thus we have  $\mathcal{s} \to \mathcal{z}$ . on the other hand, since the vocabulary  $\mathcal{s}$  provides the words to compose the caption  $\mathcal{l}$ , the causal link  $\mathcal{s} \to \mathcal{l}$  is naturally built. consequently,  $\mathcal{s}$  becomes the common cause of both  $\mathcal{z}$  and  $\mathcal{l}$ , which means that  $\mathcal{s}$  is an additional confounder between them. then, if a keyword, e.g., "person", is frequently used to sample the triplets, the words which are closely related to this keyword, e.g., "man", or "woman", will also appear more frequently in the captions. thus, the additional biases are induced.

now we have the complete causal graph in fig. 5(a) where two confounders exist:  $\mathcal{d}$  and  $\mathcal{s}$ . to calculate the causal effect  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{x}))$ , both two confounders should be deconfounded:  $\mathcal{d}$  can be deconfounded by the front-door adjustment (see section 2.2) and  $\mathcal{s}$  can be deconfounded by the backdoor adjustment (section 2.1) since  $\mathcal{s}$  is an observable vocabulary.

this is our dicv1.0 which deconfounds image captioning. briefly, we provide the formula of  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{x}))$  here and the detail derivations are given in section b.1 of the supplementary material:

$$
\begin{array}{l} p (\mathcal {l} | d o (\mathcal {x})) \\ = \sum_ {s} p (s) \sum_ {\boldsymbol {x}} p (\boldsymbol {x}) \sum_ {z} p (\boldsymbol {z} | \mathcal {x}) [ p (\mathcal {l} | s, \boldsymbol {x}, z) ] \tag {17} \\ = \mathbb {e} _ {\boldsymbol {s}} \mathbb {e} _ {\boldsymbol {x}} \mathbb {e} _ {[ z | \mathcal {x} ]} [ p (\mathcal {l} | \boldsymbol {s}, \boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {z}) ]. \\ \end{array}
$$

it can be found that it is the expected value of  $p(\mathcal{l}|s, x, z)$  according to three variables  $s, x,$  and  $z$ , which denote the word embeddings of the keywords, the visual features, and the semantic embeddings, respectively. notice that  $\mathcal{x}$  denotes the current image which we want to generate the caption from and  $x$  denotes the potential visual feature of the whole representation space.

to implement our dicv1.0 into the encoder-decoder framework, we parameterize  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{x}))$  as a network whose last layer is a softmax layer that implements  $p(\mathcal{l}|s, x, z)$  as:

$$
p (\mathcal {l} | \boldsymbol {s}, \boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {z}) = \operatorname {s o f t m a x} [ g (\boldsymbol {s}, \boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {z}) ], \tag {18}
$$

where  $g(\cdot)$  is an embedding layer before the softmax layer, e.g., it can be an fc layer. however, this brings one challenge that in order to compute  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{x}))$  in eq. (17), we need a huge number of outputs sampled from this network to get the expectations of  $p(\mathcal{l}|s,x,z)$ . to solve this challenge, we propose a two-step approximation which allows us to forward the network only once to get an estimation of  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{x}))$ . the first step is called normalized weighted geometric mean (nwgm) approximation [30], [71], [72] which absorbs the expectations into the network (see section 4.2). the second step is to sample finite  $s$ ,  $x$ , and  $z$  from the respective representation spaces of  $s$ ,  $\mathcal{x}$ , and  $\mathcal{z}$  for estimating the expectations in eq. (17) (see section 4.3).

# 4.2 normalized weighted geometric mean

by nwgm approximation [30], the expectation of a softmax unit is approximated as the softmax of the expectation:

$$
\begin{array}{l} p (\mathcal {l} | d o (\mathcal {x})) \\ = \mathbb {e} _ {\boldsymbol {s}} \mathbb {e} _ {\boldsymbol {x}} \mathbb {e} _ {[ \boldsymbol {z} | \mathcal {x} ]} \left\{\operatorname {s o f t m a x} [ g (\boldsymbol {s}, \boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {z}) ] \right\} \tag {19} \\ \approx \operatorname {s o f t m a x} \left\{\mathbb {e} _ {\boldsymbol {s}} \mathbb {e} _ {\boldsymbol {x}} \mathbb {e} _ {[ z | \mathcal {x} ]} [ g (\boldsymbol {s}, \boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {z}) ] \right\}. \\ \end{array}
$$

furthermore, if  $g(\cdot)$  is an fc layer, we have:

$$
p (\mathcal {l} | d o (\mathcal {x})) \approx \operatorname {s o f t m a x} \left\{g \left(\mathbb {e} _ {\boldsymbol {s}} [ \boldsymbol {s} ], \mathbb {e} _ {\boldsymbol {x}} [ \boldsymbol {x} ], \mathbb {e} _ {[ \boldsymbol {z} | \mathcal {x} ]} [ \boldsymbol {z} ]\right) \right\}. \tag {20}
$$

here we can put the expectations into the fc layer  $g(\cdot)$  because the linear projection of the expectation of one variable equals to the expectation of the linear projection of that variable. more details about the derivations of eq. (19) and (20) by nwgm

# 4.3 expt modules for expectations

before this section, we formulate all the probability equations at the sentence level, i.e.,  $\mathcal{l}$  denotes a whole caption. when we compute the probabilities at word level, for convenience, we still use  $\mathcal{l}$  to denote the word and write the word distribution as  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{x}),\boldsymbol {h})$ , where  $\pmb{h}$  is a context vector which accumulates the knowledge of the previously generated words. by modifying eq. (17) (see section b.1 and b.2 of the supplementary material), the word distribution becomes:

$$
\begin{array}{l} p (\mathcal {l} | d o (\mathcal {x}), \boldsymbol {h}) \\ = \sum_ {s} p (s | h) \sum_ {x} p (x | h) \sum_ {z} p (z | \mathcal {x}, h) [ p (\mathcal {l} | s, x, z, h) ] \\ = \mathbb {e} _ {[ s | h ]} \mathbb {e} _ {[ x | h ]} \mathbb {e} _ {[ z | \mathcal {x}, h ]} [ p (\mathcal {l} | s, x, z, h) ]. \tag {21} \\ \end{array}
$$

similar to eq. (19) and (20), we can put the expectations into the softmax by nwgm approximation:

$$
\begin{array}{l} p (\mathcal {l} | d o (\mathcal {x}), \boldsymbol {h}) \tag {22} \\ \approx \operatorname {s o f t m a x} \left\{g \left(\mathbb {e} _ {[ s | h ]} [ s ], \mathbb {e} _ {[ x | h ]} [ x ], \mathbb {e} _ {[ z | \chi , h ]} [ z ]\right) \right\}. \\ \end{array}
$$

then we should calculate:  $\mathbb{e}_{[s|h]}[s]$ ,  $\mathbb{e}_{[x|h]}[x]$ , and  $\mathbb{e}_{[z|\mathcal{x},h]}[z]$ . here we use  $\mathbb{e}_{[x|h]}[x]$  as the example to show how to design an expt module for estimating its value. mathematically, we have:

$$
\mathbb {e} _ {[ \boldsymbol {x} | \boldsymbol {h} ]} [ \boldsymbol {x} ] = \sum_ {\boldsymbol {x}} p (\boldsymbol {x} | \boldsymbol {h}) \boldsymbol {x}. \tag {23}
$$

however, this sum is intractable since both the representation space of image feature  $\mathcal{x}$  and the conditional distribution  $p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{h})$  are so complex that we can not get a closed form solution. to estimate it, we apply two approximations to design our expt module, which are learning the bases of the representation space  $\mathcal{x}$  and calculating the conditional distribution  $p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{h})$  by the key-query operation. we apply a dictionary learning algorithm [73] to learn a basis dictionary  $\boldsymbol{x} = \{\boldsymbol{x}_1,\boldsymbol{x}_2,\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_k\}$  from the representations of all the training images, where each element  $\boldsymbol{x}_k$  is a basis vector. after learning this basis dictionary, we use it to approximate the whole representation space:

$$
\mathbb {e} _ {[ \boldsymbol {x} | \boldsymbol {h} ]} [ \boldsymbol {x} ] = \sum_ {k} p (\boldsymbol {x} _ {k} | \boldsymbol {h}) \boldsymbol {x} _ {k}. \tag {24}
$$

for the conditional distribution  $p(\pmb{x}|\pmb{h})$ , we deploy the efficient query-key operation to approximate it, where  $\pmb{h}$  acts as the query and the elements in  $\pmb{x}$  as the key. this query-key operation is a routine operation used in the attention mechanism [30] and the memory network [74]. to sum up, we have  $\mathrm{expt}(x,h)$  to

estimate  $\mathbb{e}_{[\pmb {x}|\pmb {h}]}\left[\pmb {x}\right]$

$$
\text {i n p u t}: \quad \boldsymbol {x} = \left\{\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}, \boldsymbol {x} _ {2}, \dots , \boldsymbol {x} _ {k} \right\}, \boldsymbol {h}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l l} \text {p r o b a b i l i t y :} & q _ {k} = \boldsymbol {x} _ {k} ^ {t} \boldsymbol {h}, \\ & \boldsymbol {p} = \left\{p _ {1}, \dots , p _ {k} \right\} = \operatorname {s o f t m a x} (\left\{q _ {1}, \dots , q _ {k} \right\}) \end{array} \tag {25}
$$

$$
\text {o u t p u t :} \quad \mathbb {e} _ {[ \boldsymbol {x} | \boldsymbol {h} ]} [ \boldsymbol {x} ] \approx \sum_ {k} p _ {k} \boldsymbol {x} _ {k}.
$$

since all the operations of expt module are differentiable,  $x$  can also be updated during the end-to-end training. similarly, as in fig. 5(c), we use  $\mathrm{expt}(z,\mathrm{cat}(\hat{x},h))$  to estimate  $\mathbb{e}_{[z|\mathcal{x},h]}[z]$  where  $z$  is the semantic embedding dictionary that each element  $z$  of it is computed by eq. (16);  $\mathrm{cat}(\cdot)$  denotes the concatenation operation; and  $\hat{x}$  is the attended feature of the image  $\mathcal{x}$  at the current time step (see section 4.4). when we collect the commonsense structure set, we use the nouns, verbs, and adjectives of the caption vocabulary as the keywords to search the related structures from conceptnet. meanwhile, these keywords' trainable embeddings construct the dictionary  $s$ , which is used in  $\mathrm{expt}(s,h)$  to estimate  $\mathbb{e}_{[s|h]}[s]$ . the numbers of elements of  $x$ ,  $z$ , and  $s$  are set to 10,000, 9,590, and 1,342.

# 4.5 training objectives

when training both models, we minimize the cross-entropy loss by using  $p(\mathcal{l}^* |do(\mathcal{x}))$  as the target in the first 35 epochs:

$$
l _ {x e} = - \log p \left(\mathcal {l} ^ {*} | d o (\mathcal {x})\right), \tag {29}
$$

where  $\mathcal{l}^*$  denotes the ground-truth caption. after that, we minimize the rl-based loss [76] to train both models for another 65 epochs:

$$
l _ {r l} = - \mathbb {e} _ {\mathcal {l} ^ {s} \sim p (\mathcal {l} | d o (\mathcal {x})} [ r \left(\mathcal {l} ^ {s}; \mathcal {l} ^ {*}\right) ], \tag {30}
$$

where  $r$  is a sentence-level metric between the sampled sentence  $\mathcal{l}^s$  and the ground-truth  $\mathcal{l}^*$ , e.g., the cider-d metric [77]. we use adam optimizer [78] to train both models. the learning rate for both models is initialized to  $5e^{-4}$  and is decayed by 0.8 for every 5 epochs. importantly, the learning rate of all the dictionaries in expt modules, e.g.,  $x$ ,  $z$ , and  $s$ , are set 10 times smaller than the other layers, e.g.,  $5e^{-5}$ . the batch sizes for ud-dicv1.0 and aoa-dicv1.0 are set to 100 and 10, respectively. when inference, we use beam search with a beam size of 5.

# 5 experiments

# 5.1 datasets

ms coco [29]. we validated our models on ms coco ic dataset. in particular, our models were tested on two different splits: karpathy split [79] and the official online test split,

which divide the whole dataset into 113, 287/5, 000/5, 000 and 82, 783/40, 504/40, 775 images for training/validation/test, respectively. we followed previous researches to pre-process our captions [24], [45]. at last we trimmed each caption to a maximum of 16 words and had a vocabulary of 10, 369 words by removing the words which appear less than 5 times.

conceptnet [70]. conceptnet has abundant commonsense structures which have the form of "subject-predicate-object" and each of them is assigned with an importance weight. we used the nouns, verbs, and adjectives which appear more than 20 times in ms coco captioning dataset as the keywords to collect the related structures and all these 1,342 keywords were used to construct the dictionary  $s$ . for each commonsense structure in conceptnet, if its importance weight is larger than 2.5 and if both the subject and object of this structure are our keywords, we collected it. finally, we had a semantic structure set with 9,590 structures and they were used to build the dictionary  $z$ .

# 5.2 ablation studies

# 5.2.1 deconfounding techniques

we used up-down as the backbone, whose architecture is the same as fig. 5 (b) but without the bottom three parallel lstm layers and all the expt modules, and gradually incorporated new components into this backbone to design various ablation studies for validating the importance of different deconfounding techniques: the backdoor adjustment (section 2.1), the front-door adjustment (section 2.2), and our dicv1.0 (section 4.4).

# comparing methods.

ud: we re-implemented up-down [24] as our baseline, where only  $\mathrm{att}(\mathcal{x})$  exists in the decoder.

ud-bd: compared with ud, we followed the backdoor adjustment (eq. (7)) and estimated  $\mathbb{e}_{[d|h]}[d]$  by  $\mathrm{expt}(d,h)$  (eq. (25)). we grouped the word embeddings of 80 visual concepts in ms coco as the dictionary  $d$  since they are the most frequently appearing objects in the images and captions, which are likely to induce the spurious correlation. this baseline was designed to confirm the utility of the backdoor adjustment.

ud-bd/s & ud-bd/cc: to test the effects of using more elements to split the confounder  $\mathcal{d}$ , we deploy  $\mathrm{ud + expt}(s,h)$  and  $\mathrm{ud + expt}(d_c,h)$ , respectively.  $s$  is the vocabulary with 1,342 keywords and  $d_c$  contains 20,932 triplets sampled from conceptnet [70], which is equal to the size of dictionaries used in ud-dicv1.0 (10,000/9,590/1,342 elements in  $x / z / s$ ).

ud-fd/cor: compared with ud, we added expt( $z, \operatorname{cat}(\hat{x}, h)$ ) into the decoder. we can treat this as training a front-door ic by the observational correlation  $p(\mathcal{l}|\mathcal{x})$  (eq. (8)) and thus the spurious correlation still exists.

ud-fd: compared with ud-fd/cor, we added  $\mathrm{expt}(\pmb {x},\pmb {h})$  into the decoder. this can be treated as training a front-door ic by the nwgm approximation of  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{x}))$  (eq. (15)) while neglecting the confounder  $\mathcal{s}$ . this baseline was used to confirm the utility of the front-door adjustment. we also expanded  $\mathcal{z}$  with additional 5,000 commonsense triplets to validate that whether more parameters bring consistent improvements as ud-dicv1.0.

ud-dicv1.0: compared with ud-fd, we added  $\mathrm{expt}(s,h)$  into the decoder to get the integral framework of our dicv1.0 as in fig. 5. this equals to train a front-door ic by using the nwgm approximation of eq. (17), which deconfounds both two confounders:  $\mathcal{d}$  and  $s$ .

# 6 conclusions

we used the causal perspective to offer an in-depth analysis about why modern captioners easily learn the spurious correlation. we discovered that some frequently appeared concepts act as a typical confounder that if we neglect such confounder and train the captioners by the observational correlation  $p(\mathcal{l}|\mathcal{x})$ , the learned captioners collapse into the dataset bias. then we introduced two techniques: the backdoor and front-door adjustments which can alleviate such problem by calculating the interventional probability  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{x}))$  as the training target. we also retrospected the major progress in ic and found that many advanced captioners are the approximations of the backdoor and front-door adjustments. after that, we derived an effective method called dicv1.0 based on the causal retrospect and validated its effectiveness by incorporating it into two prevailing models: up-down and aoanet.

# a causal preliminaries

# a.1 causal graph

before detailing the derivations of the deconfounding techniques, it is beneficial to discuss more details about the causal graph. in the framework of causal inference [?], [?], a causal graph is represented by a directed graph where the direction of the arrow means whether this link is causal or anticausal, e.g.,  $\mathcal{x} \to \mathcal{l}$  is a causal link and  $\mathcal{x} \gets \mathcal{d}$  is an anticausal link. note that the information can be passed in both directions: causal or anticausal. for example, as shown in fig. a(a), there are two paths between  $\mathcal{x}$  and  $\mathcal{l}$ : a causal path  $\mathcal{x} \to \mathcal{l}$  and a backdoor path  $\mathcal{x} \gets \mathcal{d} \to \mathcal{l}$  which induces the spurious correlation between  $\mathcal{x}$  and  $\mathcal{l}$  to disturb the learning of the causal effect of  $\mathcal{x}$  on  $\mathcal{l}$ .

formally, a backdoor path between  $\mathcal{x}$  and  $\mathcal{l}$  is defined as: any path from  $\mathcal{x}$  to  $\mathcal{l}$  that starts with an arrow pointing into  $\mathcal{x}$ . here we show another two examples for helping understand this concept. in fig. a(b),  $\mathcal{x} \to \mathcal{z}$  is a causal path between  $\mathcal{x}$  and  $\mathcal{z}$ , while the path  $\mathcal{x} \gets \mathcal{d} \to \mathcal{l} \gets \mathcal{z}$  is a backdoor path between  $\mathcal{x}$  and  $\mathcal{z}$  since  $\mathcal{d}$  pointing into  $\mathcal{x}$ . another example is the backdoor

# a.2 blocking path

here we introduce three rules about how to block a path to stop the information flow between two variables. in a causal graph, there are three different elemental "junctions" which construct the whole graph. correspondingly, there are three rules for blocking information flows in these three junctions. three junctions are given as follows:

1.  $\mathcal{a} \to \mathcal{b} \to \mathcal{c}$ . this is called the chain junction, where  $\mathcal{b}$  is a mediator transmitting information from  $\mathcal{a}$  to  $\mathcal{c}$ . in this junction, once we know the value of the mediator  $\mathcal{b}$ , learning about  $\mathcal{a}$  will not give us any information to raise or lower our belief about  $\mathcal{c}$ . therefore, if we directly control  $\mathcal{b}$  to certain values, the information flow from  $\mathcal{a}$  to  $\mathcal{c}$  is blocked. for example, we know that hard-working causes a high-quality paper and finally affects the acceptance of this paper: hard-working  $\rightarrow$  high-quality  $\rightarrow$  accept, where high-quality is a mediator. once we control high-quality to be true, we know the paper is likely to be accepted and we do not need to know any information about hard-working to lower or raise this belief.  
2.  $\mathcal{a} \gets \mathcal{b} \to \mathcal{c}$ . this is called the confounding junction where  $\mathcal{b}$  is a confounder of  $\mathcal{a}$  and  $\mathcal{c}$ . in this junction, once we know what the value of confounder  $\mathcal{b}$  is, there is no spurious correlation between  $\mathcal{a}$  and  $\mathcal{c}$ . therefore, as in the chain junction, if we directly control  $\mathcal{b}$  to certain values, the information flow from  $\mathcal{a}$  to  $\mathcal{c}$  is blocked. we have already met this junction in introduction of the submitted manuscript where we use digital classification as the example.  
3.  $\mathcal{a} \to \mathcal{b} \gets \mathcal{c}$ . this is called the collider which works in an exactly opposite way from the above chain and confounding junctions. in a collider, if we do not know what the value of  $\mathcal{b}$  is,  $\mathcal{a}$  and  $\mathcal{c}$  are independent. however, once we know the value of  $\mathcal{b}$ ,  $\mathcal{a}$  and  $\mathcal{c}$  are correlated! we still use paper acceptance as the example, suppose both high-quality and luck affect accept, though high-quality and luck are unrelated. under this situation, we have luck  $\rightarrow$  accept  $\leftarrow$  high-quality. if we do not know whether a paper is accepted, luck and high-quality

to sum up, if we want to block the information flow between  $\mathcal{a}$  and  $\mathcal{c}$ , we can directly control  $\mathcal{b}$  to certain values in both the chain and confounding junctions, and we must not control  $\mathcal{b}$  in a collider.

# b formula derivations

# b.1 derivations of eq. (17) and eq. (21)

the causal graphs of our dicv1.0 model are shown in fig. b. we will derive eq. (17) and eq. (21) of the manuscript based on these two causal graphs. we first show how to calculate the sentence level  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{x}))$  (eq. (17)) from the causal graph in fig. b(a). to achieve this, we follow the procedure used in calculating the front-door adjustment (section 2.2): we first calculate  $p(\mathcal{z}|do(\mathcal{x}))$  and  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{z}))$ , then we chain them together to get the final  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{x}))$ . to get  $p(\mathcal{z}|do(\mathcal{x}))$ , we find

$$
p (\mathcal {z} = \boldsymbol {z} | d o (\mathcal {x})) = p (\mathcal {z} = \boldsymbol {z} | \mathcal {x}). \tag {a}
$$

since both two backdoor paths  $\mathcal{x} \gets \mathcal{d} \to \mathcal{l} \gets \mathcal{z}$  and  $\mathcal{x} \gets \mathcal{d} \to \mathcal{l} \gets \mathcal{s} \to \mathcal{z}$  between  $\mathcal{x}$  and  $\mathcal{z}$  are naturally blocked due to the colliders  $\mathcal{d} \to \mathcal{l} \gets \mathcal{z}$  and  $\mathcal{d} \to \mathcal{l} \gets \mathcal{s}$ , respectively.

to get  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{z}))$ , we need to block both two backdoor paths between  $\mathcal{z}$  and  $\mathcal{l}$ , which are  $\mathcal{z} \gets s \to \mathcal{l}$  and  $\mathcal{z} \gets \mathcal{x} \gets \mathcal{d} \to \mathcal{l}$ . to block the former path, we have to control  $s$ , and to block the latter path, we have to control  $\mathcal{x}$  since  $\mathcal{d}$  is not accessible. therefore, we should control two variables  $s$  and  $\mathcal{x}$  to block both the two backdoor paths. therefore, we have

$$
\begin{array}{l} p (\mathcal {l} | d o (\mathcal {z} = z)) \\ = \sum_ {s} p (s) \sum_ {\boldsymbol {x}} p (\boldsymbol {x}) [ p (\mathcal {l} | s, \boldsymbol {x}, z) ]. \tag {b} \\ \end{array}
$$

to sum up, after chaining two partial effects together, we have the sentence level  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{x}))$  as:

$$
\begin{array}{l} p (\mathcal {l} | d o (\mathcal {x})) \\ = \sum_ {s} p (s) \sum_ {\boldsymbol {x}} p (\boldsymbol {x}) \sum_ {z} p (\boldsymbol {z} | \mathcal {x}) [ p (\mathcal {l} | s, \boldsymbol {x}, z) ] \tag {c} \\ = \mathbb {e} _ {\boldsymbol {s}} \mathbb {e} _ {\boldsymbol {x}} \mathbb {e} _ {[ \boldsymbol {z} | \mathcal {x} ]} [ p (\mathcal {l} | \boldsymbol {s}, \boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {z}) ], \\ \end{array}
$$

which is eq. (17) in the submitted manuscript.

when we calculate  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{x}))$  at the word level, as shown in fig. b(b), it is also conditioned on the variable  $\mathcal{h}$  which is the accumulated context knowledge of the partially generated caption. at each time step, the value of  $\mathcal{h}$  can be pre-computed and we use  $h$  to denote this value, which means  $\mathcal{h}$  is already controlled to be  $h$ . note that  $\mathcal{h}$  only appears in the confounding junctions:  $\mathcal{x} \gets \mathcal{h} \to \mathcal{z}, \mathcal{x} \gets \mathcal{h} \to \mathcal{l}, \mathcal{z} \gets \mathcal{h} \to \mathcal{l}$ . therefore, these paths which go through  $\mathcal{h}$  are blocked, e.g.,  $\mathcal{x} \gets \mathcal{h} \to \mathcal{z}$  and  $\mathcal{x} \gets \mathcal{d} \to \mathcal{l} \gets \mathcal{h} \to \mathcal{z}$  are already blocked. as a result, we can modify eq. (a) as:

$$
p (\mathcal {z} = \boldsymbol {z} | d o (\mathcal {x}), \boldsymbol {h}) = p (\mathcal {z} = \boldsymbol {z} | \mathcal {x}, \boldsymbol {h}), \tag {d}
$$

eq. (b) as:

$$
\begin{array}{l} p (\mathcal {l} | d o (\mathcal {z} = z), \boldsymbol {h}) \\ = \sum_ {\boldsymbol {s}} p (\boldsymbol {s} | \boldsymbol {h}) \sum_ {\boldsymbol {x}} p (\boldsymbol {x} | \boldsymbol {h}) [ p (\mathcal {l} | \boldsymbol {s}, \boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {z}, \boldsymbol {h}) ], \tag {e} \\ \end{array}
$$

and eq. (c) as:

$$
\begin{array}{l} p (\mathcal {l} | d o (\mathcal {x}), \boldsymbol {h}) \\ = \sum_ {s} p (s | h) \sum_ {\boldsymbol {x}} p (\boldsymbol {x} | h) \sum_ {z} p (z | \mathcal {x}, h) [ p (\mathcal {l} | s, \boldsymbol {x}, z, h) ] \\ = \mathbb {e} _ {[ \boldsymbol {s} | \boldsymbol {h} ]} \mathbb {e} _ {[ \boldsymbol {x} | \boldsymbol {h} ]} \mathbb {e} _ {[ \boldsymbol {z} | \mathcal {x}, \boldsymbol {h} ]} [ p (\mathcal {l} | \boldsymbol {s}, \boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {z}, \boldsymbol {h}) ], \tag {f} \\ \end{array}
$$

which is eq. (21) in the submitted manuscript. note that  $p(s|h)$  should be  $p(s)$  since there is no direct link from  $\mathcal{h}$  to  $s$ . while in the experiment, we still set  $s$  to be conditioned on  $h$  to increase the representation power of the whole model. and if not, by normalized weighted geometric mean approximation (see section b.2), the expectation of  $s$  will degrade to a fixed vector.

# b.2 derivations of eq. (19) and eq. (20)

here we show how to use normalized weighted geometric mean (nwgm) approximation [?], [?], [?] to absorb the expectations into the network for deriving eq. (19) in the submitted manuscript. before introducing nwgm, we first revisit the calculation of a function  $f(\mathcal{x})$ 's expectation according to the distribution  $p(\mathcal{x})$ :

$$
\mathbb {e} _ {\boldsymbol {x}} [ f (\boldsymbol {x}) ] = \sum_ {\boldsymbol {x}} f (\boldsymbol {x}) p (\boldsymbol {x}), \tag {g}
$$

which is the weighted arithmetic mean of  $f(\pmb{x})$  with  $p(\pmb{x})$  as the weights. correspondingly, the weighted geometric mean (wgm) of  $f(\pmb{x})$  with  $p(\pmb{x})$  as the weights is:

$$
\operatorname {w g m} (f (\boldsymbol {x})) = \prod_ {\boldsymbol {x}} f (\boldsymbol {x}) ^ {p (\boldsymbol {x})}, \tag {h}
$$

where the weights  $p(\pmb{x})$  are put into the exponential terms. if  $f(\pmb{x})$  is an exponential function that  $f(\pmb{x}) = \exp[g(\pmb{x})]$ , we have:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \operatorname {w g m} (f (\boldsymbol {x})) = \prod_ {\boldsymbol {x}} f (\boldsymbol {x}) ^ {p (\boldsymbol {x})} \\ = \prod_ {\boldsymbol {x}} \exp [ g (\boldsymbol {x}) ] ^ {p (\boldsymbol {x})} = \prod_ {\boldsymbol {x}} \exp [ g (\boldsymbol {x}) p (\boldsymbol {x}) ] \tag {i} \\ = \exp \left[ \sum_ {\boldsymbol {x}} g (\boldsymbol {x}) p (\boldsymbol {x}) \right] = \exp \left\{\mathbb {e} _ {\boldsymbol {x}} [ g (\boldsymbol {x}) ] \right\}, \\ \end{array}
$$

where the expectation  $\mathbb{e}_x$  is absorbed into the exponential term. based on this observation, researchers approximate the expectation of a function by the wgm of this function in the deep network whose last layer is a softmax layer [?], [?], [?]

$$
\mathbb {e} _ {\boldsymbol {x}} [ f (\boldsymbol {x}) ] \approx \operatorname {w g m} (f (\boldsymbol {x})) = \exp \left\{\mathbb {e} _ {\boldsymbol {x}} [ g (\boldsymbol {x}) ] \right\}, \tag {j}
$$

where  $f(\pmb{x}) = \exp [g(\pmb{x})]$ .

in our case, we parameterize  $p(\mathcal{l}|s,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})$  in eq. (c) as a network with a softmax layer as the last layer:

$$
p (\mathcal {l} | \boldsymbol {s}, \boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {z}) = \operatorname {s o f t m a x} [ g (\boldsymbol {s}, \boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {z}) ] \propto \exp [ g (\boldsymbol {s}, \boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {z}) ]. \tag {k}
$$

we follow eq. (c) and (j) to get:

$$
\begin{array}{l} p (\mathcal {l} | d o (\mathcal {x})) = \mathbb {e} _ {\boldsymbol {s}} \mathbb {e} _ {\boldsymbol {x}} \mathbb {e} _ {[ z | \mathcal {x} ]} [ p (\mathcal {l} | \boldsymbol {s}, \boldsymbol {x}, z) ] \\ \approx \operatorname {w g m} (p (\mathcal {l} | \boldsymbol {s}, \boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {z})) \approx \exp \left\{\mathbb {e} _ {\boldsymbol {s}} \mathbb {e} _ {\boldsymbol {x}} \mathbb {e} _ {[ \boldsymbol {z} | \mathcal {x} ]} [ g (\boldsymbol {s}, \boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {z}) ] \right\}. \tag {l} \\ \end{array}
$$

note that, as in eq. (k),  $p(\mathcal{l}|s, \boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{z})$  is only proportional to  $\exp[g(\boldsymbol{s}, \boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{z})]$  instead of strictly equalling to, we only have  $\mathrm{wgm}(p(\mathcal{l}|s, \boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{z})) \approx \exp\{\mathbb{e}_s \mathbb{e}_\boldsymbol{x} \mathbb{e}_{[\boldsymbol{z}|\mathcal{x}]}[g(\boldsymbol{s}, \boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{z})]\}$  in eq. (l) instead of equalling to. furthermore, to guarantee the sum of  $p(\mathcal{l}|do(\mathcal{x}))$  to be 1, we use a softmax layer to normalize these exponential units:

$$
p (\mathcal {l} | d o (\mathcal {x})) \approx \operatorname {s o f t m a x} \left\{\mathbb {e} _ {\boldsymbol {s}} \mathbb {e} _ {\boldsymbol {x}} \mathbb {e} _ {[ \boldsymbol {z} | \mathcal {x} ]} [ g (\boldsymbol {s}, \boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {z}) ] \right\}, \tag {m}
$$

which is eq. (19) in the submitted manuscript. since the softmax layer normalizes these exponential terms, this is called the normalized weighted geometric mean (nwgm) approximation. in addition, if  $g(\cdot)$  is a fully connected layer, we have:

$$
p (\mathcal {l} | d o (\mathcal {x})) \approx \operatorname {s o f t m a x} \left\{g \left(\mathbb {e} _ {\boldsymbol {s}} [ \boldsymbol {s} ], \mathbb {e} _ {\boldsymbol {x}} [ \boldsymbol {x} ], \mathbb {e} _ {[ \boldsymbol {z} | \mathcal {x} ]} [ \boldsymbol {z} ]\right) \right\}. \tag {n}
$$

in the same vein, we can use nwgm to approximate the word level distribution eq. (f) as:

$$
\begin{array}{l} p (\mathcal {l} | d o (\mathcal {x}), \boldsymbol {h}) \\ \approx \operatorname {s o f t m a x} \left\{g \left(\mathbb {e} _ {[ s | h ]} [ s ], \mathbb {e} _ {[ x | h ]} [ x ], \mathbb {e} _ {[ z | \mathcal {x}, h ]} [ z ]\right) \right\}. \tag {o} \\ \end{array}
$$

then we can use expt modules introduced in section 4.2 (eq. (25)) of the submitted manuscript to estimate these expectations. as discussed in the end of section b.1, we set  $s$  to be conditioned on  $\pmb{h}$  to increase the representation power. if not, we find that  $\mathbb{e}_{[s][s]}$  will be the same value all the time during the caption generation.

conceptnet. specifically, we used the nouns, verbs, and adjectives which respectively appear more than 20 times in the training set to search the related commonsense knowledge. we followed the previous models [?], [?], [?] to use the cross-entropy loss eq.(19) for fair comparisons. for ud-dicv1.0/aoa-dicv1.0, we trained the models 35 epochs and initialized the learning rates to  $5e^{-4}/1e^{-4}$  and decayed them 0.8 for every 5 epochs, respectively. the results are reported in table f.

for hrn, it builds hierarchical representation and exploits the syntax pattern as the additional supervision, while such supervision still comes from the training dataset, thus the model is not deconfounded. org builds a spatial-temporal graph as the mediator and org+trl further uses the pre-trained bert word embedding to distill the knowledge for alleviating the long tail biases. however, since the graph is got from the training dataset and the pre-trained model may also form a confounder, the whole system is still confounded. from table f, we can find that our ud-dicv1.0 and aoa-dicv1.0 achieve higher cider-d scores than these models. such comparisons also confirm the advantage of using deconfounding techniques in video captioning.

discussion about the other tasks besides captioning, many vision-language models are also damaged from the dataset bias, e.g., vqa or visual dialog systems are discovered to reason by over-relying on the spurious question-answer correlations [?], [?] or the history information [?]. meantime, lots of vision-language systems apply various mediators, e.g., graphs [?], [?] or neural modules [?], [?], to help alleviate the dataset bias problem. however, the used mediators are usually got from the same training set (the confounder) and these systems are usually trained by the cross-entropy loss, which means they are not deconfounded. therefore, they may still be damaged from the dataset bias. to alleviate the dataset bias, researchers can also follow our method to use the mediators from the third-party dataset, e.g., some knowledge graph datasets, to achieve the front-door adjustment.
