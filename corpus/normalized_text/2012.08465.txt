# neural collapse with cross-entropy loss

jianfeng lu and stefan steinerberger

abstract. we consider the variational problem of cross-entropy loss with  $n$  feature vectors on a unit hypersphere in  $\mathbb{r}^d$ . we prove that when  $d \geq n - 1$ , the global minimum is given by the simplex equiangular tight frame, which justifies the neural collapse behavior. we also prove that as  $n \to \infty$  with fixed  $d$ , the minimizing points will distribute uniformly on the hypersphere and show a connection with the frame potential of benedetto & fickus.

# 1. introduction and results

1.1. introduction. we consider the following variational problem

$$
\min  _ {u} \mathcal {l} _ {\alpha} (u) := \min  _ {u} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(1 + \sum_ {\substack {j = 1 \\ j \neq i}} ^ {n} e ^ {\alpha (\langle u _ {j}, u _ {i} \rangle - 1)}\right). \tag{1}
$$

where  $\alpha > 0$  is a parameter and for  $i = 1, \dots, n$ ,  $u_i \in \mathbb{r}^d$  such that  $\| u_i \| = 1$ . here and in the sequel, we use  $\|\cdot\|$  to denote the euclidean norm of a vector, and  $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$  denotes the euclidean inner product. the question we would like to address in this note is the solution structure of such variational problems. the problem has several motivations from some recent works in the literature of machine learning.

our main motivation comes from the very nice recent paper [10]. in that paper, the authors proposed and studied the neural collapse behavior of training of deep neural networks for classification problems. following the work [10] by choosing a cross-entropy loss, while taking unconstrained features (i.e., not parametrized by some nonlinear functions like neural networks) to be vectors on the unit sphere in  $\mathbb{r}^d$ , this amounts to the study of the variational problem

$$
\min  _ {u, v} \mathcal {l} (u, v) := \min  _ {u, v} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(\frac {\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} e ^ {\langle v _ {j} , u _ {i} \rangle}}{e ^ {\langle v _ {i} , u _ {i} \rangle}}\right). \tag {2}
$$

where  $u_{i}, v_{i} \in \mathbb{r}^{d}$  such that  $\| u_{i} \| = 1$  for each  $i$ . note that the model in [10] also contains a bias vector, so that  $\langle v_{j}, u_{i} \rangle$  in (2) is replaced by  $\langle v_{j}, u_{i} \rangle + b_{j}$ , for  $b \in \mathbb{r}^n$ . we drop the bias to remove some degeneracy of the problem for simplicity. another, more crucial, difference is that in actual deep learning, as considered in [10], the feature vectors  $u_{i}$  are given by output of deep neural networks acting on the input data, this would make the variational problem much harder to analyze and thus we will only study the simplified scenario.

1.2. equiangular tight frame as minimizer. the connection between the two variational problems is evident, as (1) can be viewed as a symmetric version of (2): in particular, if we choose  $v_{i} = \alpha u_{i}$  for some parameter  $\alpha > 0$ , then  $\mathcal{l}(\alpha u, u) = \mathcal{l}_{\alpha}(u)$ . in fact, we will prove that the minimum of (2) is indeed achieved by such symmetric solutions. there is a small caveat though as one can take the norm of  $v$  to infinity (or  $\alpha \to \infty$  for the symmetric problem) to reduce the loss. thus, in order to characterize better the solution structure, we will consider the problem for a fixed scaling of  $v$ , and in fact  $\| v_{i} \| = 1$  (the case of  $\| v_{i} \| = \alpha$  will be discussed below). we show that the solution of the variational problem is given by a simplex equiangular tight frame (etf). this proves the neural collapse behavior for (2), which provides some justification to the observation of such behavior in deep learning.

theorem 1. consider the variational problem

$$
\min  _ {u, v} \mathcal {l} (u, v)
$$

$$
\text {s u c h} u _ {i}, v _ {i} \in \mathbb {r} ^ {d}, \| u _ {i} \| = \| v _ {i} \| = 1, \quad i = 1, \dots , n.
$$

if  $d \geq n - 1$ , the global minimum of the problem corresponds to the case where  $\{u_i\}_{i=1}^n$  form a simplex equiangular tight frame and  $u_i = v_i$  for all  $i = 1, \dots, n$ .

we remark that similar results have been proved for different loss functions: for a large deviation type loss function in [10] and for a  $l^2$ -loss function in [9], both for models with unconstrained feature vectors (i.e., without neural network parametrization of  $u_i$ 's). after making the first version of our paper available, we were informed that an equivalent result has been obtained in [5], based on a quite different proof.

in theorem 1, the restriction of the scale of  $\| v_{i} \| = 1$  does not in fact sacrifice generality. as we will comment towards the end of the proof, if instead  $\| v_{i} \| \leq \alpha$  is assumed, the solution would be given by  $v_{i} = \alpha u_{i}$ . this is related to the following result for the symmetric problem (1).

theorem 2. consider the variational problem

$$
\min _ {u} \mathcal {l} _ {\alpha} (u)
$$

$$
\text {s u c h} \quad t h a t \quad u _ {i} \in \mathbb {r} ^ {d}, \| u _ {i} \| = 1, \quad i = 1, \dots , n.
$$

if  $d \geq n - 1$ , then for any  $\alpha > 0$ , the global minimum of the problem corresponds to the case where  $\{u_i\}_{i=1}^n$  form a simplex equiangular tight frame.

we prove theorem 2 first in section 2; the idea of the proof extends to that of theorem 1, which will be presented in section 3.

1.3. more vectors than dimensions. in the above results, the assumption that  $p \geq n - 1$  (or equivalently  $n \leq p + 1$ ) is crucial as only then it is possible to place  $n$  vectors on the unit sphere in  $\mathbb{r}^p$  such that these vectors form a simplex equiangular tight frame. it is natural to ask what happens when  $n \geq p + 2$ . in this case, (1) is related to loss functions used in unsupervised learning and self-supervised learning, such as those used in siamese networks [4] and word2vec [7,8]. in particular, the spherical contrastive loss considered in [11], for which the goal is to embed many points on a hypersphere such that the points are "uniformly distributed", coincides with (1) when unconstrained feature vectors are used.

for general  $n \geq p + 2$ , the study of the structure of the minimizer seems difficult, but in the asymptotic regime  $n \to \infty$ , we have the following theorem states that indeed the points will uniformly distributed on the sphere.

theorem 3. consider the variational problem

$$
\min _ {u} \mathcal {l} _ {\alpha} (u)
$$

such that  $u_{i}\in \mathbb{r}^{d},\| u_{i}\| = 1,\quad i = 1,\dots ,n.$

let  $\mu_{n}$  be the probability measure on  $\mathbb{s}^d$  generated by a minimizer

$$
\mu_ {n} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \delta_ {u _ {i}},
$$

then for any  $\alpha > 0$ , as  $n \to \infty$ ,  $\mu_n$  converges weakly to the uniform measure on  $\mathbb{s}^d$ .

the proof of theorem will be presented in section 4. it uses the following proposition which characterizes the minimizer for a relaxed version of the variational problem defined for probability measures on  $\mathbb{s}^d$ . with some abuse of notation, for  $\mu \in \mathcal{p}(\mathbb{s}^d)$ , we denote

$$
\mathcal {l} _ {\alpha} (\mu) := \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} \log \left(\int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} e ^ {\alpha (\langle x, y \rangle - 1)} \mu (\mathrm {d} y)\right) \mu (\mathrm {d} x). \tag {3}
$$

it is easy to check that  $\mathcal{l}_{\alpha}$  acting on  $\mu_{n} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i = 1}^{n}\delta_{u_{i}}$  is equivalent (up to some additive constants) to the objective function defined in (1) evaluated at the point configuration  $\{u_i\}$ . the following proposition states that the unique minimizer of  $\mathcal{l}_{\alpha}$  on  $\mathcal{p}(\mathbb{s}^d)$  is given by uniform probability measure; this fact was established in [11, theorem 1] using a rather different approach. our proof, deferred to section 4, which is based on variational arguments, seems simpler in comparison.

proposition 4. the unique minimizer of the variational problem

$$
\inf _ {\mu \in \mathcal {p} (\mathbb {s} ^ {d})} \mathcal {l} _ {\alpha} (\mu)
$$

is the uniform probability measure on  $\mathbb{s}^d$ .

one natural question is how quickly sets of  $n$  points can have an energy that is comparable to the energy of the uniform measure. we will show that  $n$  points can have an energy that is super-exponentially close to the energy of the flat distribution. note first that

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathcal {l} _ {\alpha} (\mu) = \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} \log \left(\int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} e ^ {\alpha (\langle x, y \rangle - 1)} \mu (\mathrm {d} y)\right) \mu (\mathrm {d} x) \\ = \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} \log \left(e ^ {- \alpha} \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} e ^ {\alpha \langle x, y \rangle} \mu (\mathrm {d} y)\right) \mu (\mathrm {d} x) \\ = - \alpha + \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} \log \left(\int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} e ^ {\alpha \langle x, y \rangle} \mu (\mathrm {d} y)\right) \mu (\mathrm {d} x) \\ \end{array}
$$

and that we can therefore study this equivalent but slightly more symmetric functional. in particular, when studying the case where  $\mu$  is given by the sum of dirac

measure in a finite number of points, we can obtain an even more symmetric upper bound by applying jensen's inequality

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} e ^ {\alpha \langle x _ {i}, x _ {j} \rangle}\right) \leq n \log \left(\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i, j = 1} ^ {n} e ^ {\alpha \langle x _ {i}, x _ {j} \rangle}\right).
$$

as it turns out, the particular structure of this upper bound allows us to obtain very precise bounds for the minimal energy of optimal configurations.

theorem 5. let  $\alpha >0$  be fixed. for some  $c_{\alpha ,d} > 0$  and all  $n$  sufficiently large, there exist sets of points  $\{x_{1},\ldots ,x_{n}\} \subset \mathbb{s}^{d}$  for which

$$
0 \leq \frac {1}{n ^ {2}} \sum_ {i, j = 1} ^ {n} e ^ {\alpha \langle x _ {i}, x _ {j} \rangle} - \frac {1}{| \mathbb {s} ^ {d} | ^ {2}} \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d} \times \mathbb {s} ^ {d}} e ^ {\alpha \langle x, y \rangle} d x d y \leq e ^ {- c _ {\alpha , d} n \log n}.
$$

this type of extremely rapid convergence might be an indicator that the actual convergence for  $\mathcal{l}_{\alpha}$  does indeed happen from below: our use of jensen's inequality applied to a concave function (the logarithm) in the proof is a further indicator.

1.4. the frame potential. we conclude with a simple observation: for  $\alpha \to 0^{+}$ , the functional  $\mathcal{l}_{\alpha}$  has a taylor expansion with quite excellent properties.

proposition 6. for a fixed set of point  $\{u_1,\ldots ,u_n\} \subset \mathbb{s}^d$  , we have, as  $\alpha \to 0$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} e ^ {\alpha \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle}\right) = n \log n + \frac {\alpha}{n} \left\| \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} u _ {i} \right\| ^ {2} + \frac {\alpha^ {2}}{2 n} \sum_ {i, j = 1} ^ {n} \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle^ {2} \\ - \frac {\alpha^ {2}}{2 n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left\langle u _ {i}, \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} u _ {j} \right\rangle^ {2} + \mathcal {o} (\alpha^ {3}). \\ \end{array}
$$

this expansion has an interesting property: if  $\alpha$  is quite small, then the linear term dominates and minimizers of the energy functional will be forced to have  $\| \sum_{i=1}^{n} u_i \|$  quite small or possibly even 0. this has implications for the third term which will then also be small. as such we would expect that there is an emerging effective energy given by

$$
e (u _ {1}, \ldots , u _ {n}) = \frac {\alpha}{n} \left\| \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} u _ {i} \right\| ^ {2} + \frac {\alpha^ {2}}{2 n} \sum_ {i, j = 1} ^ {n} \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle^ {2}.
$$

this object function, however, is strongly connected to the frame potential

$$
f (u _ {1}, \ldots , u _ {n}) = \sum_ {i, j = 1} ^ {n} \left\langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \right\rangle^ {2}.
$$

the frame potential was introduced in the seminal work of benedetto & fickus [1] and has since played an important role in frame theory. what is utterly remarkable is that the frame potential has a large number of highly structured minimizers (see, for example, fig. 1). as shown by benedetto & fickus, for any  $\{u_1,\ldots ,u_n\} \subset \mathbb{s}^{d - 1}$

$$
f (u _ {1}, \ldots , u _ {n}) = \sum_ {i, j = 1} ^ {n} \left\langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \right\rangle^ {2} \geq \frac {n ^ {2}}{d}
$$

with equality if and only if the set of points form a tight frame, i.e. if

$$
\forall u \in \mathbb {r} ^ {d}: \quad \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left\langle u, u _ {i} \right\rangle^ {2} = \frac {n}{d} \| u \| ^ {2}.
$$

![](images/1f5b648f3dfc35b97efb476ebae73ad086df60b449533d62b5cc618bb21e3af1.jpg)  
figure 1. the 72 vertices of the dodecahedron-icosahedron compound form a unit norm tight frame of  $\mathbb{r}^3$ . the point configuration is also a global minimizer of the frame potential.

in fact, our effective energy  $e(u_{1},\ldots ,u_{n})$  may be understood as the frame potential with an additional strong incentive for the point configuration to have mean value 0. it would be interesting to have a better understanding whether  $\mathcal{l}_{\alpha}$  inherits some of the good properties of the frame potential for  $\alpha$  small, for example, whether it is possible to say anything about minimal energy configurations of  $\mathcal{l}_{\alpha}$  when  $n\geq d$  but not going to  $\infty$ .

# 2. proof for theorem 2

proof. recall the variational problem under consideration

$$
\min_{u}\mathcal{l}_{\alpha}(u):= \min_{u}\sum_{i = 1}^{n}\log \bigl(1 + \sum_{\substack{j = 1\\ j\neq i}}^{n}e^{\alpha (\langle u_{j},u_{i}\rangle -1)}\bigr).
$$

using jensen's inequality, we have, for any fixed  $1 \leq i \leq n$ ,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {1}{n - 1} \sum_ {\substack {j = 1 \\ j \neq i}} ^ {n} e ^ {\alpha \langle u _ {j}, u _ {i} \rangle} \geq \exp \left(\frac {1}{n - 1} \sum_ {\substack {j = 1 \\ j \neq i}} ^ {n} \alpha \langle u _ {j}, u _ {i} \rangle\right) \tag{4} \\ = \exp \left(\frac {\alpha}{n - 1} \left(\langle u, u _ {i} \rangle - 1\right)\right), \\ \end{array}
$$

where we introduce the sum

$$
u = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} u _ {i}.
$$

thus, since the logarithm is monotone,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathcal {l} _ {\alpha} (u) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(1 + e ^ {- \alpha} \sum_ {\substack {j = 1 \\ j \neq i}} ^ {n} e ^ {\alpha \langle u _ {j}, u _ {i} \rangle}\right) \tag{5} \\ \geq \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \big (1 + (n - 1) e ^ {- \frac {n \alpha}{n - 1}} e ^ {\frac {\alpha}{n - 1} \langle u, u _ {i} \rangle} \big). \\ \end{array}
$$

note that for any  $a, b > 0$ , the function  $t \mapsto \log(1 + ae^{bt})$  is convex, applying jensen's inequality again, we have

$$
\frac {1}{n} \mathcal {l} _ {\alpha} (u) \geq \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left[ 1 + (n - 1) e ^ {- \frac {n \alpha}{n - 1}} \exp \left(\frac {\alpha}{n - 1} \langle u, u _ {i} \rangle\right) \right]
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \geq \log \left[ 1 + (n - 1) e ^ {- \frac {n \alpha}{n - 1}} \exp \left(\frac {\alpha}{n - 1} \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \langle u, u _ {i} \rangle\right) \right] \tag {6} \\ = \log \left[ 1 + (n - 1) e ^ {- \frac {n \alpha}{n - 1}} \exp \left(\frac {\alpha}{n - 1} \frac {1}{n} \| u \| ^ {2}\right) \right] \\ \geq \log \left(1 + (n - 1) e ^ {- \frac {n \alpha}{n - 1}}\right), \\ \end{array}
$$

therefore, we arrive at

$$
\mathcal {l} _ {\alpha} (u) \geq n \log \left(1 + (n - 1) e ^ {- \frac {n \alpha}{n - 1}}\right). \tag {7}
$$

to see when the minimum is achieved, note that in (4), due to strict convexity of the exponential function, equality only holds when, for  $j \neq i$

$$
\langle u _ {j}, u _ {i} \rangle = c _ {i} \quad \text {i s i n d e p e n d e n t o f} j.
$$

equality in (6) only holds if

$$
\langle u, u _ {i} \rangle = c \qquad \text {i n d e p e n d e n t o f} i.
$$

finally, inequality in the last part of (6) only holds when  $u = 0$ . if  $u = 0$ , then

$$
c = \langle u,u_{i}\rangle = \left\langle \sum_{j = 1}^{n}u_{j},u_{i}\right\rangle = 1 + \left\langle \sum_{\substack{j = 1\\ j\neq i}}^{n}u_{j},u_{i}\right\rangle = 1 + (n - 1)c_{i}.
$$

this shows that  $c_{i}$  does not actually depend on  $i$  and thus  $\langle u_i, u_j \rangle \equiv c$  for some constant  $c$  whenever  $i$  is different from  $j$ . thus, we conclude

$$
0 = \left\| \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} u _ {i} \right\| ^ {2} = n + \sum_ {i \neq j} \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle = n + n (n - 1) c. \tag {8}
$$

this implies that

$$
\langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle = - \frac {1}{n - 1}.
$$

therefore, the global minimum is achieved if and only if when  $\{u_i\}_{i=1}^n$  form a simplex equiangular tight frame (recall  $d \geq n-1$  so it is achievable).

# 3. proof of theorem 1

proof. the proof follows along similar lines as the proof of theorem 2. recall

$$
\mathcal{l}(u,v) = \sum_{i = 1}^{n}\log \bigg(\frac{\sum_{j = 1}^{n}e^{\langle v_{j},u_{i}\rangle}}{e^{\langle v_{i},u_{i}\rangle}}\bigg) = \sum_{i = 1}^{n}\log \bigg(1 + \sum_{\substack{j = 1\\ j\neq i}}^{n}e^{\langle v_{j} - v_{i},u_{i}\rangle}\bigg)
$$

applying jensen's inequality, we have, for fixed  $1 \leq i \leq n$ ,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \sum_{\substack{j = 1\\ j\neq i}}^{n}e^{\langle v_{j} - v_{i},u_{i}\rangle} = e^{-\langle v_{i},u_{i}\rangle}\sum_{\substack{j = 1\\ j\neq i}}^{n}e^{\langle v_{j},u_{i}\rangle} \\ \geq (n - 1) e ^ {- \langle v _ {i}, u _ {i} \rangle} \exp \left(\frac {1}{n - 1} \sum_ {\substack {j = 1 \\ j \neq i}} ^ {n} \langle v _ {j}, u _ {i} \rangle\right) \tag{9} \\ = (n - 1) e ^ {- \langle v _ {i}, u _ {i} \rangle} \exp \left(\frac {\langle v , u _ {i} \rangle - \langle v _ {i} , u _ {i} \rangle}{n - 1}\right) \\ = (n - 1) \exp \left(\frac {\langle v , u _ {i} \rangle - n \langle v _ {i} , u _ {i} \rangle}{n - 1}\right), \\ \end{array}
$$

where we denote the sum of  $v_{i}$  as

$$
v = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} v _ {i}.
$$

thus, using the monotonicity of logarithm,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathcal {l} (u, v) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(1 + \sum_ {\substack {j = 1 \\ j \neq i}} ^ {n} e ^ {\langle v _ {j} - v _ {i}, u _ {i} \rangle}\right) \tag{10} \\ \geq \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left[ 1 + (n - 1) \exp \left(\frac {\langle v , u _ {i} \rangle}{n - 1} - \frac {n}{n - 1} \langle v _ {i}, u _ {i} \rangle\right) \right] \\ \end{array}
$$

applying jensen's inequality to the convex function  $t \mapsto \log(1 + ae^{bt})$  for  $a, b > 0$ , we have

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathcal {l} (u, v) \geq n \log \left[ 1 + (n - 1) \exp \left(\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(\frac {\langle v , u _ {i} \rangle}{n - 1} - \frac {n}{n - 1} \langle v _ {i}, u _ {i} \rangle\right)\right) \right] \tag {11} \\ = n \log \left[ 1 + (n - 1) \exp \left(\frac {1}{n} \left(\frac {\langle v , u \rangle}{n - 1} - \frac {n}{n - 1} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \langle v _ {i}, u _ {i} \rangle\right)\right) \right], \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $u = \sum_{i} u_{i}$ . for the equalities to hold in the above inequalities (9) and (11), we require for some constants  $c_{i}$  and  $c$  such that

$$
\langle v _ {j}, u _ {i} \rangle = c _ {i}, \quad \forall j \neq i \tag {12}
$$

and

$$
\frac {\langle v , u _ {i} \rangle}{n - 1} - \frac {n}{n - 1} \left\langle v _ {i}, u _ {i} \right\rangle = c, \quad \forall i. \tag {13}
$$

therefore, in order to find a lower bound on  $\mathcal{l}$ , we have to solve

$$
\frac {\langle v , u \rangle}{n - 1} - \frac {n}{n - 1} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \langle v _ {i}, u _ {i} \rangle \rightarrow \min .
$$

if there is a minimizing configuration of this simpler problem that also satisfies (12) and (13), then all inequalities are actually equalities. the above variational problem is equivalent to maximizing

$$
n \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left\langle v _ {i}, u _ {i} \right\rangle - \left\langle \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} v _ {i}, \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} u _ {i} \right\rangle = \vec {v} ^ {\top} \left(\left(n \mathbb {i} _ {n} - \mathbf {1} _ {n} \mathbf {1} _ {n} ^ {\top}\right) \otimes \mathbb {i} _ {d}\right) \vec {u}, \tag {14}
$$

where  $\otimes$  denotes the kronecker product,  $\mathbb{i}_d(\mathbb{i}_n)$  denotes a  $d\times d$ $(n\times n)$  identity matrix,  $\mathbf{1}_n$  denotes an all-1  $n$ -vector,  $\vec{u}$  denotes a long  $\mathbb{r}^{nd}$  column vector formed by concatenating  $u_{i}\in \mathbb{r}^{d}$  for  $i = 1,\dots ,n$ , and similarly for  $\vec{v}$ . we note that, being the concatenation of unit vectors,  $\| \vec{u}\| = \sqrt{n} = \| \vec{v}\|$ .

the eigenvalues of a kronecker product  $a \otimes b$  are given by  $\lambda_i \mu_j$ , where  $\lambda_i$  are the eigenvalues of  $a$  and  $\mu_j$  are the eigenvalues of  $b$ . the matrix  $n \mathbb{i}_n - \mathbf{1}_n \mathbf{1}_n^\top$  is acting like  $n \mathbb{i}_n$  on vectors having mean value 0 while sending the constant vector to 0. its spectrum is thus given by  $n$  (with multiplicity  $n - 1$ ) and 0. it follows that  $\left( (n \mathbb{i}_n - \mathbf{1}_n \mathbf{1}_n^\top) \otimes \mathbb{i}_d \right)$  is symmetric, its largest eigenvalue is  $n$  and its smallest eigenvalue is 0. recalling that  $\| \vec{u} \| = \sqrt{n} = \| \vec{v} \|$ , we have that (without constraints (12) and (13))

$$
n \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left\langle v _ {i}, u _ {i} \right\rangle - \left\langle \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} v _ {i}, \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} u _ {i} \right\rangle \leq n ^ {2}. \tag {15}
$$

however, setting  $\vec{u}$  to be the simplex and  $\vec{v} = \vec{u}$ , we see that  $u = 0 = v$  and we have equality in (15) while simultaneously satisfying the constraints (12) and (13) with

$$
c _ {i} = - \frac {1}{n - 1} \qquad \text {a n d} \qquad c = - \frac {n}{n - 1}.
$$

we will now argue that this is the only extremal example. using the spectral theorem, we see that equality in (15) can only occur if  $\vec{u}$  is an eigenvector of the matrix corresponding to the eigenvalue  $n$ . in that case, we have

$$
\left\langle \vec {v}, \left(\left(n \mathbb {i} _ {n} - \mathbf {1} _ {n} \mathbf {1} _ {n} ^ {\top}\right) \otimes \mathbb {i} _ {d}\right) \vec {u} \right\rangle = n \left\langle \vec {v}, \vec {u} \right\rangle \leq n \| \vec {v} \| \| \vec {u} \| \leq n ^ {2}.
$$

we have equality in cauchy-schwarz if and only if  $\vec{v} = \lambda \vec{u}$  for some  $\lambda > 0$ . for  $\alpha = 1$ , this implies that  $\vec{v} = \vec{u}$  and we are back in the symmetric case and can argue as in the proof of theorem 2. conditions (12) and (13) simplify to

$$
\langle u _ {j}, u _ {i} \rangle = c _ {i}, \forall j \neq i \qquad \mathrm {a n d} \qquad \frac {\langle u , u _ {i} \rangle}{n - 1} - \frac {n}{n - 1} \langle u _ {i}, u _ {i} \rangle = c, \forall i.
$$

moreover, by summing over the second condition, we see that we want to minimize

$$
c \cdot n = \frac {\langle u , u \rangle}{n - 1} - \frac {n}{n - 1} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \langle u _ {i}, u _ {i} \rangle \rightarrow \min .
$$

we are thus interested in minimizing  $c$  which is given by

$$
c = \frac {\langle u , u _ {i} \rangle}{n - 1} - \frac {n}{n - 1} \langle u _ {i}, u _ {i} \rangle = c _ {i} - \langle u _ {i}, u _ {i} \rangle \geq c _ {i} - 1.
$$

however, for any set of  $n$  unit vectors, the largest inner product between any pair of distinct vectors satisfies

$$
0 \leq \left\| \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} u _ {i} \right\| ^ {2} = n + n (n - 1) \max _ {i \neq j} \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle .
$$

thus

$$
c \geq \max _ {1 \leq i \leq n} c _ {i} - 1 \geq - \frac {1}{n - 1} - 1 = - \frac {n}{n - 1}.
$$

we see that equality is achieved for the simplex. moreover, in the case of equality, we have to require that  $\langle u_i, u_j \rangle = -1 / (n - 1)$  for any pair of distinct vectors and

this characterizes the simplex. moreover, if  $\| v_{i} \| \leq \alpha$ , then it is easy to see that the maximum is achieved when  $\vec{v} = \alpha \vec{u}$ , we can again conclude using theorem 2.

# 4. proof of theorem 3

we first prove proposition 4; theorem 3 then follows from a limiting argument.

proof of proposition 4. without loss of generality, we assume  $\alpha = 1$  in the proof, and note that the energy functional can be rewritten as

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathcal {l} (\mu) = \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} \log \left(\int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} e ^ {\langle x, y \rangle - 1} \mu (\mathrm {d} y)\right) \mu (\mathrm {d} x) \\ = \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} \log \left(\int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} e ^ {\langle x, y \rangle - (| x | ^ {2} + | y | ^ {2}) / 2} \mu (\mathrm {d} y)\right) \mu (\mathrm {d} x) \\ = \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} \log \left(\int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} e ^ {- | x - y | ^ {2} / 2} \mu (\mathrm {d} y)\right) \mu (\mathrm {d} x). \\ \end{array}
$$

using the gaussian convolution identity

$$
e ^ {- | x - y | ^ {2} / 2} = \frac {1}{(8 \pi) ^ {d / 2}} \int_ {\mathbb {r} ^ {d}} e ^ {- | x - z | ^ {2} / 4} e ^ {- | z - y | ^ {2} / 4} \mathrm {d} z
$$

and the jensen's inequality, we have

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathcal {l} (\mu) = \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} \log \left(\int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} \frac {1}{(8 \pi) ^ {d / 2}} \int_ {\mathbb {r} ^ {d}} e ^ {- | x - z | ^ {2} / 4} e ^ {- | z - y | ^ {2} / 4} \mathrm {d} z \mu (\mathrm {d} y)\right) \mu (\mathrm {d} x) \\ = \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} \log \left(\int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} \frac {1}{(4 \pi) ^ {d / 2}} \int_ {\mathbb {r} ^ {d}} e ^ {- | x - z | ^ {2} / 4} e ^ {- | z - y | ^ {2} / 4} \mathrm {d} z \mu (\mathrm {d} y)\right) \mu (\mathrm {d} x) - \frac {d}{2} \log 2 \\ \geq \frac {1}{(4 \pi) ^ {d / 2}} \int_ {\mathbb {r} ^ {d}} \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} e ^ {- | x - z | ^ {2} / 4} \log \left(\int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} e ^ {- | z - y | ^ {2} / 4} \mu (\mathrm {d} y)\right) \mu (\mathrm {d} x) \mathrm {d} z - \frac {d}{2} \log 2 \\ = \frac {1}{(4 \pi) ^ {d / 2}} \int_ {\mathbb {r} ^ {d}} \left(\int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} e ^ {- | x - z | ^ {2} / 4} \mu (\mathrm {d} x)\right) \log \left(\int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} e ^ {- | z - y | ^ {2} / 4} \mu (\mathrm {d} y)\right) \mathrm {d} z - \frac {d}{2} \log 2. \\ \end{array}
$$

the above calculation shows that minimizing  $\mathcal{l}(\mu)$  is equivalent to minimizing

$$
\mathcal {g} (\mu) := \int_ {\mathbb {r} ^ {d}} f _ {\mu} (z) \log f _ {\mu} (z) \mathrm {d} z,
$$

where we define the short-hand

$$
f _ {\mu} (z) := \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} e ^ {- | x - z | ^ {2} / 4} \mu (\mathrm {d} x).
$$

let us write the integral of  $\mathcal{g}(\mu)$  in spherical coordinates and get

$$
\mathcal {g} (\mu) = \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} \left(\frac {1}{| \mathbb {s} ^ {d} |} \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} f _ {\mu} (r, \theta) \log f _ {\mu} (r, \theta) \mathrm {d} \theta\right) r ^ {d - 1} \mathrm {d} r.
$$

lemma. for any  $r$ , the integral

$$
\int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} f _ {\mu} (r, \theta) \mathrm {d} \theta
$$

is independent of  $\mu \in \mathcal{p}(\mathbb{s}^d)$ .

proof. by definition, we have (letting  $z = (r,\theta)$  in the spherical coordinates)

$$
\begin{array}{l} \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} f _ {\mu} (r, \theta) \mathrm {d} \theta = \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} e ^ {- | x - z | ^ {2} / 4} \mu (\mathrm {d} x) \mathrm {d} \theta \\ = \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} e ^ {- | x - z | ^ {2} / 4} \mathrm {d} \theta \mu (\mathrm {d} x) \\ = \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} m (r) \mu (\mathrm {d} x) = m (r), \\ \end{array}
$$

where

$$
m (r) := \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} e ^ {- | x - z | ^ {2} / 4} \mathrm {d} \theta , \tag {16}
$$

which is independent of  $x$  due to the spherical symmetry, and hence is only a function of  $r$ .

now for each fixed  $r$ , we can consider the variational problem

$$
\begin{array}{l} \arg \inf  _ {\mu} \mathcal {g} _ {r} (\mu) := \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} f _ {\mu} (r, \theta) \log f _ {\mu} (r, \theta) d \theta \\ \mathrm {s . t .} \int f _ {\mu} (r, \theta) = m (r), \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $m(r)$  is defined in (16). we note that  $\mathcal{g}_r(\mu)$  in terms of  $f_{\mu}(r,\cdot)$  is just the entropy functional, which is strongly convex and is minimized if and only if  $f_{\mu}(r,\cdot)$  is uniform on  $\mathbb{s}^d$ , which is equivalent to the uniformity of  $\mu$ . since  $\mathcal{g}(\mu) = \int_0^\infty \mathcal{g}_r(\mu)r^{d - 1}\mathrm{d}r$  is a positive linear combination of the energy functional  $\mathcal{g}_r(\mu)$ , we conclude that the global minimum of  $\mathcal{g}(\mu)$  and hence  $\mathcal{l}(\mu)$  corresponds to uniform probability distribution on  $\mathbb{s}^d$ .

we are now ready to prove theorem 3.

proof of theorem 3. let  $\mu_{n}$  be a sequence of probability measures corresponding to minimizers for  $n = 1,2,\dots$ . since  $\mathbb{s}^d$  is compact, it suffices to prove that any weakly convergent subsequence  $\mu_{n_k}$  would converge to the uniform measure. denote  $\mu$  the limit, and define

$$
h _ {n _ {k}} (x) := \log \left(\int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} e ^ {- | x - y | ^ {2} / 2} \mu_ {n _ {k}} (\mathrm {d} y)\right),
$$

and

$$
h (x) := \log \left(\int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} e ^ {- | x - y | ^ {2} / 2} \mu (\mathrm {d} y)\right).
$$

as  $e^{-|x - y|^2 / 2}$  is a smooth function in  $x$  and  $y$  and is bounded from below by  $e^{-2}$  for  $x, y \in \mathbb{s}^d$ ,  $\mu_{n_k} \rightharpoonup \mu$  implies that  $h_{n_k}$  converges to  $h(x)$  uniformly on  $\mathbb{s}^d$ , and hence

$$
\mathcal {l} (\mu_ {n _ {k}}) = \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} h _ {n _ {k}} (x) \mu_ {n _ {k}} (\mathrm {d} x) \to \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d}} h (x) \mu (\mathrm {d} x) = \mathcal {l} (\mu).
$$

thus, the functional  $\mathcal{l}$  is weakly continuous on  $\mathcal{p}(\mathbb{s}^d)$ . since  $\mu_{n_k}$  corresponds to a minimizer of the variational problem for  $n_k$  points, using the upper bound theorem 5, the limit  $\mu$  minimizes  $\mathcal{l}$  on  $\mathcal{p}(\mathbb{s}^d)$ , which implies by proposition 4 that  $\mu$  is the uniform probability measure on  $\mathbb{s}^d$ .

# 5. proof of theorem 5

5.1. outline. we start with jensen's inequality: since the logarithm is concave, we obtain

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} e ^ {\alpha \langle x _ {i}, x _ {j} \rangle}\right) \leq n \log \left(\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i, j = 1} ^ {n} e ^ {\alpha \langle x _ {i}, x _ {j} \rangle}\right).
$$

for the rest of the proof, it suffices to understand this double sum. we will prove that there exists a sequence of positive  $a_{k} > 0$  such that

$$
\sum_ {i, j = 1} ^ {n} e ^ {\alpha \langle x _ {i}, x _ {j} \rangle} = \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {\infty} a _ {k} \left| \sum_ {\ell = 1} ^ {n} \phi_ {k} (x _ {\ell}) \right| ^ {2},
$$

where  $\phi_{k}$  denotes the  $k$ -th spherical harmonic. recalling that the 0-th spherical harmonic is a constant normalized in  $l^2$ , we see that

$$
\phi_ {0} (x) = \frac {1}{\sqrt {| \mathbb {s} ^ {d} |}}.
$$

from this, we obtain

$$
\sum_ {i, j = 1} ^ {n} e ^ {\alpha \langle x _ {i}, x _ {j} \rangle} = \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {\infty} a _ {k} \left| \sum_ {\ell = 1} ^ {n} \phi_ {k} (x _ {\ell}) \right| ^ {2} \geq a _ {0} \frac {n ^ {2}}{| \mathbb {s} ^ {d} |}.
$$

we can moreover determine the constant  $a_0$ : by plugging in randomly chosen points (independently and identically distributed with respect to the uniform measure), we see that

$$
\lim  _ {n \rightarrow \infty} \frac {1}{n ^ {2}} \sum_ {i, j = 1} ^ {n} e ^ {\alpha \langle x _ {i}, x _ {j} \rangle} = \frac {1}{| \mathbb {s} ^ {d} | ^ {2}} \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d} \times \mathbb {s} ^ {d}} e ^ {\alpha \langle x, y \rangle} d x d y
$$

while simultaneously

$$
\lim  _ {n \to \infty} \frac {1}{n ^ {2}} \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {\infty} a _ {k} \left| \sum_ {\ell = 1} ^ {n} \phi_ {k} (x _ {\ell}) \right| ^ {2} = \frac {a _ {0}}{| \mathbb {s} ^ {d} |}
$$

and therefore

$$
a _ {0} = \frac {1}{| \mathbb {s} ^ {d} |} \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d} \times \mathbb {s} ^ {d}} e ^ {\alpha \langle x, y \rangle} d x d y.
$$

if we can prove that  $a_{k} > 0$ , then this would imply that

$$
\sum_ {i, j = 1} ^ {n} e ^ {\alpha \langle x _ {i}, x _ {j} \rangle} \geq \frac {n ^ {2}}{| \mathbb {s} ^ {d} | ^ {2}} \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d} \times \mathbb {s} ^ {d}} e ^ {\alpha \langle x, y \rangle} d x d y.
$$

we will show that this is indeed the case and that one can find sets of  $n$  points for which the expression is not much larger than that.

5.2. the expansion. we will now prove the desired expansion. we start by expanding the square

$$
\sum_ {k = 0} ^ {\infty} a _ {k} \left| \sum_ {\ell = 1} ^ {n} \phi_ {k} (x _ {\ell}) \right| ^ {2} = \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {\infty} a _ {k} \sum_ {i, j = 1} ^ {n} \phi_ {k} (x _ {i}) \phi_ {k} (x _ {j}).
$$

at this point we start using a property of the sphere: by grouping spherical harmonics with respect to the laplacian eigenvalue and prescribing that  $a_{k}$  be constant for all spherical harmonics with the same eigenvalue, we can rewrite the sum as

$$
\sum_ {k = 0} ^ {\infty} a _ {k} \sum_ {i, j = 1} ^ {n} \phi_ {\ell} (x _ {i}) \phi_ {\ell} (x _ {j}) = \sum_ {\ell = 0} ^ {\infty} a _ {\ell} \sum_ {{- \delta \phi_ {k} = \ell (\ell + 1) \phi_ {k}}} ^ {k} \sum_ {i, j = 1} ^ {n} \phi_ {k} (x _ {i}) \phi_ {k} (x _ {j}).
$$

however, on  $\mathbb{s}^d$  the spherical harmonics are ordered in bands we have the addition formula valid for all  $x,y\in \mathbb{s}^d$ ,

$$
\sum_{\substack{k\\ -\delta \phi_{k} = \ell (\ell +1)\phi_{k}}}\phi_{k}(x)\phi_{k}(y) = \frac{\ell + \lambda}{\lambda} c_{k}^{\lambda}(\langle x,y\rangle),
$$

where  $c_k^\lambda$  are the gegenbauer polynomials and

$$
\lambda = \frac {d - 1}{2}.
$$

this leads to

$$
\sum_ {k = 0} ^ {\infty} a _ {k} \left| \sum_ {\ell = 1} ^ {n} \phi_ {k} (x _ {\ell}) \right| ^ {2} = \sum_ {\ell = 0} ^ {\infty} a _ {\ell} \frac {\ell + \lambda}{\lambda} \sum_ {i, j = 1} ^ {n} c _ {\ell} ^ {(d - 1) / 2} \left(\langle x _ {i}, x _ {j} \rangle\right),
$$

where, by an abuse of notation, we exploit that (by assumption) the  $a_{k}$  coincide whenever the two spherical harmonics share the same laplacian eigenvalue. using [2, prop. 2.2], we see that if all the coefficients in the expansion

$$
e ^ {\alpha x} = \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {\infty} b _ {k} c _ {k} ^ {(d - 1) / 2} (x) \quad \text {f o r} x \in [ - 1, 1 ]
$$

are positive, then the function is positive on  $\mathbb{s}^d$  and then [2, lemma 2.3] implies that the expansion converges uniformly. it suffices to show that  $b_{k} > 0$ . the gegenbauer polynomials  $c_k^\alpha$  are orthogonal on  $[-1,1]$  with respect to the weight

$$
w (z) = (1 - z ^ {2}) ^ {\alpha - \frac {1}{2}}.
$$

making an ansatz

$$
e ^ {\alpha x} = \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {\infty} b _ {k} c _ {k} ^ {(d - 1) / 2} (x),
$$

we see that the coefficient  $b_{k}$  is given by

$$
b _ {k} = \frac {\int_ {- 1} ^ {1} e ^ {\alpha x} (1 - x ^ {2}) ^ {(d - 2) / 2} c _ {k} ^ {(d - 1) / 2} (x) d x}{\int_ {- 1} ^ {1} (1 - x ^ {2}) ^ {(d - 2) / 2} \left(c _ {k} ^ {(d - 1) / 2} (x)\right) ^ {2} d x}.
$$

the denominator has a closed form expression which we will abbreviate by  $\alpha_{1}(k,d)$

$$
\alpha_ {1} (k, d) = \int_ {- 1} ^ {1} (1 - x ^ {2}) ^ {(d - 2) / 2} \left(c _ {k} ^ {(d - 1) / 2} (x)\right) ^ {2} d x = \frac {\pi 2 ^ {2 - d} \cdot \gamma (k + d - 1)}{k ! \cdot (k + (d - 1) / 2) \cdot \gamma \left(\frac {d - 1}{2}\right) ^ {2}}.
$$

it remains to understand the numerator. gegenbauer polynomials have a rodrigues formula which is as follows:

$$
c _ {k} ^ {(d - 1) / 2} = \frac {(- 1) ^ {k}}{2 ^ {k} \cdot k !} \frac {\gamma (d / 2) \cdot \gamma (k + d - 1)}{\gamma (d - 1) \cdot \gamma (d / 2 + k)} (1 - x ^ {2}) ^ {- (d - 2) / 2} \frac {d ^ {k}}{d x ^ {k}} \left[ (1 - x ^ {2}) ^ {k + (d - 2) / 2} \right].
$$

we will abbreviate the constant by  $\alpha_{2}(k,d)$ , i.e.

$$
c _ {k} ^ {(d - 1) / 2} (x) = (- 1) ^ {k} \alpha_ {2} (k, d) (1 - x ^ {2}) ^ {- (d - 2) / 2} \frac {d ^ {k}}{d x ^ {k}} \left[ (1 - x ^ {2}) ^ {k + (d - 2) / 2} \right],
$$

where

$$
\alpha_ {2} (k, d) = \frac {1}{2 ^ {k} \cdot k !} \frac {\gamma (d / 2) \cdot \gamma (k + d - 1)}{\gamma (d - 1) \cdot \gamma (d / 2 + k)}.
$$

therefore

$$
\alpha_ {1} (k, d) \cdot b _ {k} = \alpha_ {2} (k, d) \int_ {- 1} ^ {1} e ^ {\alpha x} (- 1) ^ {k} \frac {d ^ {k}}{d x ^ {k}} \left[ (1 - x ^ {2}) ^ {k + (d - 2) / 2} \right] d x.
$$

it is easy to see that  $b_0 > 0$ . we now distinguish the cases  $d = 2$  and  $d \geq 3$ . let us first assume that  $d = 2$ . we see that

$$
\frac {d ^ {\ell}}{d x ^ {\ell}} \left[ (1 - x ^ {2}) ^ {k} \right] \big | _ {x = - 1, 1} = 0 \qquad \mathrm {f o r} \quad \ell = 0, 1, \ldots , k - 1.
$$

we can thus use integration by parts and get

$$
\alpha_ {1} (k, d) \cdot b _ {k} = \alpha_ {2} (k, d) \cdot \alpha^ {k} \cdot \int_ {- 1} ^ {1} e ^ {\alpha x} \left[ (1 - x ^ {2}) ^ {k + (d - 2) / 2} \right] d x > 0.
$$

the same argument applies to  $d \geq 3$ . in that case we even have

$$
\frac {d ^ {k}}{d x ^ {k}} \left[ (1 - x ^ {2}) ^ {k + (d - 2) / 2} \right] | _ {x = - 1, 1} = 0
$$

and can again integrate by parts  $k$  times to obtain

$$
\alpha_ {1} (k, d) \cdot b _ {k} = \alpha_ {2} (k, d) \cdot \alpha^ {k} \int_ {- 1} ^ {1} e ^ {\alpha x} (1 - x ^ {2}) ^ {k + (d - 2) / 2} d x > 0.
$$

altogether, we see that  $b_{k} > 0$  for  $\alpha > 0$  and thus there exists a sequence of positive  $a_{k}$  such that

$$
\frac {1}{n ^ {2}} \sum_ {i, j = 1} ^ {n} e ^ {\alpha \langle x _ {i}, x _ {j} \rangle} = \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {\infty} a _ {k} \left| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {\ell = 1} ^ {n} \phi_ {k} (x _ {\ell}) \right| ^ {2}.
$$

5.3. obtaining quantitative estimates. we will now go through the argument in the preceding section with the goal of getting quantitative estimates on  $b_{k}$ , where

$$
\alpha_ {1} (k, d) \cdot b _ {k} = \alpha_ {2} (k, d) \cdot \alpha^ {k} \cdot \int_ {- 1} ^ {1} e ^ {\alpha x} (1 - x ^ {2}) ^ {k + (d - 2) / 2} d x > 0
$$

and  $\alpha_{1}(k,d)$  and  $\alpha_{2}(k,d)$  are given above in closed form. we are interested in bounds from above, it therefore suffices to estimate the integral. we see that the integral decays at a polynomial rate - this is perhaps not all that relevant and we bound very roughly

$$
\int_ {- 1} ^ {1} e ^ {\alpha x} (1 - x ^ {2}) ^ {k + (d - 2) / 2} \leq 2 e ^ {\alpha}.
$$

therefore

$$
0 \leq b _ {k} \leq 2 e ^ {\alpha} \cdot \alpha^ {k} \cdot \frac {\alpha_ {2} (k , d)}{\alpha_ {1} (k , d)}
$$

we have, ignoring factors that depend solely on the dimension  $d$ ,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {\alpha_ {2} (k , d)}{\alpha_ {1} (k , d)} = \frac {k ! (k + (d - 1) / 2) \cdot \gamma \left(\frac {d - 1}{2}\right) ^ {2}}{\pi 2 ^ {2 - d} \cdot \gamma (k + d - 1)} \frac {1}{2 ^ {k} \cdot k !} \frac {\gamma (d / 2) \cdot \gamma (k + d - 1)}{\gamma (d - 1) \cdot \gamma (d / 2 + k)} \\ \lesssim_ {d} \frac {1}{2 ^ {k}} \frac {(k + (d - 1) / 2)}{\gamma (d / 2 + k)} \\ \end{array}
$$

which decays faster than any exponential in  $k$ . we see that this is inherited by the coefficient  $b_{k}$  which satisfies

$$
0 \leq b _ {k} \lesssim_ {d} e ^ {\alpha} \cdot \frac {\alpha^ {k}}{2 ^ {k}} \frac {(k + (d - 1) / 2)}{\gamma (d / 2 + k)}.
$$

we note that

$$
\lim _ {k \to \infty} \log \left(\frac {1}{b _ {k}}\right) \frac {1}{k \log k} = 1.
$$

5.4. proof of theorem 5. the behavior of these coefficients, decaying faster than exponential in  $m$ , has a number of interesting consequences. first and foremost, it means that

$$
\sum_ {k = 0} ^ {\infty} a _ {k} \left| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {\ell = 1} ^ {n} \phi_ {k} (x _ {\ell}) \right| ^ {2} \qquad \text {b e i n g s m a l l}
$$

is really a statement about the distribution of the measure with respect to the first few spherical harmonics. having a large error with regards to some intermediate spherical harmonic is barely detectable - in particular, deducing structural statements about the points via energy arguments is presumably more difficult than it is for other kernels with slower decay in the coefficients. another consequence is that we expect a fairly 'flat' energy landscape.

proof of theorem 5. we use the representation

$$
\frac {1}{n ^ {2}} \sum_ {i, j = 1} ^ {n} e ^ {\alpha \left\langle x _ {i}, x _ {j} \right\rangle} = \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {\infty} a _ {k} \left| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {\ell = 1} ^ {n} \phi_ {k} (x _ {\ell}) \right| ^ {2}
$$

and the fact that the  $k = 0$  term corresponds to the integral. thus

$$
\frac {1}{n ^ {2}} \sum_ {i, j = 1} ^ {n} e ^ {\alpha \langle x _ {i}, x _ {j} \rangle} - \frac {1}{| \mathbb {s} ^ {d} | ^ {2}} \int_ {\mathbb {s} ^ {d} \times \mathbb {s} ^ {d}} e ^ {\alpha \langle x, y \rangle} d x d y = \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {\infty} a _ {k} \left| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {\ell = 1} ^ {n} \phi_ {k} (x _ {\ell}) \right| ^ {2}.
$$

now we pick the set  $\{x_{1},\ldots ,x_{n}\} \subset \mathbb{s}^{d}$  to be an optimal spherical design: by a result of bondarenko, radchenko & viazovska [3], there exist  $\{x_1,\dots,x_n\} \subset \mathbb{s}^d$  such that the average of any polynomial of degree  $\deg (p)\leq c\cdot n^{1 / d}$  evaluated in these points coincides with the global average of the polynomial on the sphere. in particular, the first  $\sim_d n$  spherical harmonics are being evaluated exactly. then, however,

$$
\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {\infty} a _ {k} \left| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {\ell = 1} ^ {n} \phi_ {k} (x _ {\ell}) \right| ^ {2} = \sum_ {k = c \cdot n} ^ {\infty} a _ {k} \left| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {\ell = 1} ^ {n} \phi_ {k} (x _ {\ell}) \right| ^ {2}.
$$

we now use several rather crude bounds. we note that the  $k$ -th spherical harmonics have eigenvalue  $\sim_d k^{2/d}$  (by weyl's asymptotic) and use an old result of hörmander [6] to conclude that

$$
\left\| \phi_ {k} \right\| _ {l ^ {\infty}} \lesssim \lambda_ {k} ^ {\frac {d - 1}{4}} \sim k ^ {\frac {d - 1}{2 d}}.
$$

this implies, for some  $c_{1} > 0$

$$
\sum_ {k = c \cdot n} ^ {\infty} a _ {k} \left| \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {\ell = 1} ^ {n} \phi_ {k} (x _ {\ell}) \right| ^ {2} \lesssim \sum_ {k = c _ {1} \cdot n} ^ {\infty} a _ {k} k ^ {\frac {d - 1}{d}}.
$$

by the previous result, we see that the superexponential decay of  $a_{k}$  turns the sum essentially into its largest term and from this the desired bound follows.

# 6. proof of proposition 6

proof. we are interested in asymptotics for

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} e ^ {\alpha \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle}\right) \qquad \text {a s} \alpha \rightarrow 0.
$$

we have the taylor expansion

$$
\log \left(1 + x\right) = x - \frac {x ^ {2}}{2} + \mathcal {o} (x ^ {3})
$$

and thus, as  $\alpha \to 0$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} e ^ {\alpha \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle}\right) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} 1 + \alpha \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle + \frac {\alpha^ {2}}{2} \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle^ {2} + \dots\right) \\ = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(n \left(1 + \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} \alpha \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle + \frac {\alpha^ {2}}{2} \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle^ {2} + \dots\right)\right) \\ = n \log n + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(1 + \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} \alpha \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle + \frac {\alpha^ {2}}{2} \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle^ {2} + \dots\right). \\ \end{array}
$$

using the taylor expansion of the logarithm and collecting all the terms that are constant, linear or quadratic in  $\alpha$ , we arrive at

$$
\begin{array}{l} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} e ^ {\alpha \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle}\right) = n \log n + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} \alpha \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle + \frac {\alpha^ {2}}{2} \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle^ {2}\right) \\ - \frac {1}{2} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} \alpha \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle + \frac {\alpha^ {2}}{2} \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle^ {2}\right) ^ {2} + \mathcal {o} (\alpha^ {3}). \\ \end{array}
$$

the first term simplifies to

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} \alpha \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle + \frac {\alpha^ {2}}{2} \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle^ {2}\right) = \frac {\alpha}{n} \left\| \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} u _ {i} \right\| ^ {2} + \frac {\alpha^ {2}}{2 n} \sum_ {i, j = 1} ^ {n} \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle^ {2}.
$$

the summand in the second term simplifies to, up to first and second order in  $\alpha$

$$
- \frac {1}{2} \left(\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} \alpha \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle + \frac {\alpha^ {2}}{2} \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle^ {2}\right) ^ {2} = - \frac {\alpha^ {2}}{2 n ^ {2}} \left(\sum_ {j} \langle u _ {i}, u _ {j} \rangle\right) ^ {2} + \mathcal {o} (\alpha^ {3}).
$$
