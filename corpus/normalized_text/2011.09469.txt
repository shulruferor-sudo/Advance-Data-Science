# abstract

in transportation applications such as real-time route guidance, ramp metering, congestion pricing and special events traffic management, accurate short-term traffic flow prediction is needed. for this purpose, this paper proposes several novel online grey system models (gm):  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1|cos(\omega t))$ ,  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1|sin(\omega t),cos(\omega t))$ , and  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1|e^{-at},sin(\omega t),cos(\omega t))$ . to evaluate the performance of the proposed models, they are compared against a set of benchmark models:  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model, grey verhulst models with and without fourier error corrections, linear time series model, and nonlinear time series model. the evaluation is performed using loop detector and probe vehicle data from california, virginia, and oregon. among the benchmark models, the error corrected grey verhulst model with fourier outperformed the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model, linear time series, and non-linear time series models. in turn, the three proposed models,  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1|cos(\omega t))$ ,  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1|sin(\omega t),cos(\omega t))$ , and  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1|e^{-at},sin(\omega t),cos(\omega t))$ , outperformed the grey verhulst model in prediction by at least  $65\%$ ,  $16\%$  and  $11\%$ , in terms of root mean squared error, and by  $82\%$ ,  $58\%$  and  $42\%$ , in terms of mean absolute percentage error, respectively. it is observed that the proposed grey system models are more adaptive to location (e.g., perform well for all roadway types) and traffic parameters (e.g., speed, travel time, occupancy, and volume), and they do not require as many data points for training (4 observations are found to be sufficient).

keywords: grey system models, travel time, traffic speed, traffic volume, occupancy, transferable models.

# 1. introduction

traffic congestion has become a way of life for the majority of u.s. commuters in urban areas. with nearly 90 percent of commuters wanting to get to their destinations at the same time, it is simply a matter of supply not being able to accommodate the demand during peak-hour loads. many solutions have been investigated to mitigate traffic congestion, including real-time route guidance, ramp metering, and congestion pricing. the cornerstone of these solutions is a proactive traffic management system that relies on accurate short-term prediction of traffic parameters, such as speed, travel time, volume, and occupancy (density). by their nature, these traffic parameters are time series, and therefore, many researchers have attempted to study their behavior using time series models. however, a shortcoming of time series models is their inability to deal with non-recurring abrupt changes such as those caused by accidents (hamilton (2010);

clements and harvey (2010); hamilton (2008)). this is because traffic flow is nonlinear, stochastic and highly non-stationary.

to overcome the aforementioned limitation with time series models, this paper explores the use of grey system theory. as the name implies, grey (i.e., hazy or fuzzy) systems are those systems without complete information. examples of grey systems include the human body, economy and weather system. grey system theory provides methods for resolving latent processes. as such, it uses discrete or continuous grey numbers encoded by symbol  $\otimes$  to represent the unknown parameters of the system. grey models are popular due to their ability to estimate the behavior of systems using only a small amount of data. this characteristic makes grey models suitable for short-term traffic parameter estimation.

the grey model  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  (gm(1,1) stand for "grey model first order one variable") is the most widely used and documented in the literature (kayacan et al. (2010)). in bezuglov and comert (2016), first author explored the applicability of  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$ , grey verhulst, and their error corrected versions and showed that grey models were capable of producing accurate forecasts for un-seasonal or cyclic average traffic speed and travel time data. authors also noticed slow and excessive forecasts at change points. to improve the performance of the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model for short-term estimation of various traffic parameters, this study proposes to augment the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model with trigonometric functions. incorporating the oscillating behavior of the sine and cosine functions, or their combinations, these grey models will be more adept at predicting abrupt changes in traffic flow. in this paper, the following models are proposed:  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1|cos(\omega t))$ ,  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1|sin(\omega t),cos(\omega t))$ , and  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1|e^{-at},sin(\omega t),cos(\omega t))$ . to the best of our knowledge, these models have not been studied for short term traffic parameter estimation. to evaluate the performance of these models, they are compared with a set of benchmark models:  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model, grey verhulst models with and without fourier error corrections, seasonal time series model, and nonlinear time series model. the evaluations are done using data from california, virginia, and oregon. the models proposed in this paper outperformed the grey verhulst model in prediction by  $65\%$ ,  $16\%$  and  $11\%$ , in terms of root mean squared error, and by  $82\%$ ,  $58\%$  and  $42\%$ , in terms of mean absolute percentage error respectively. it is observed that the proposed grey system models are more adaptive to location (e.g., perform well for all roadway types) and traffic parameters (e.g., speed, travel time, occupancy, and volume), and they do not require many data points for training (4 observations are found to be sufficient).

# 2. literature review

grey system theory has been widely applied in different areas of applications for more than 30 years. thus, there is much information about its theory and applications in the literature. readers are referred to the work of liu et al. (2010) for the theory and the work by deng (1989).

in transportation related studies, the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model has been used to predict annual average daily traffic (aadt), hourly and monthly volume, traffic accidents, and pavement deterioration. to predict traffic volume, specifically aadt, zhang (2010) combined the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model with a markov transition matrix. the proposed model performed better in predicting aadts. badhrudeen et al. (2016) compared the performance of the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model against neural network models (nns). using about 100 minutes of observational data to train the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model, the results indicated that the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model outperformed the nns by 1 to  $3\%$  in mean absolute percent error (mape). similarly, an et al. (2012) compared the performance of the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model with back propagating nn and radial basis nn to predict aadt values. their results indicated that the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model outperformed both types of neural network models. in contrast, pan and shi (2016) showed that the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model performed poorly against the nn model in

predicting traffic speed (about  $6\%$  on average in mape). yu et al. (2015) examined the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model, the arima (autoregressive integrated moving average) model, generalized nn regression model and their combinations. the authors tested different combinations of models with fixed and elman nn-based weights, a framework similar to forecast encompassing (clements and harvey (2010)). elman nn-based weighted combined model (i.e.,  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$ , arima and nn regression combined) showed the highest accuracy in predicting monthly traffic volume. liu et al. (2014) combined cubic exponential smoothing with  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model. in their study, they showed that the proposed model provided lower error in predicting the monthly traffic volume.

to predict hourly volume, several researchers (e.g., shuhua et al. (2012) and shuhua and xinping (2010)) have enhanced the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model by incorporating periodic sine and tangent trigonometric terms. according to shuhua et al. (2012), the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model with the sine term included outperformed the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model by  $7\%$  in terms of mape in predicting hourly traffic volume. in the study by shuhua and xinping (2010), the authors optimized the trigonometric function periods for the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model using particle swarm optimization. the authors did not evaluate the performance of the proposed model.

grey models have also been applied to predict traffic accidents and pavement deterioration. studies in these areas are rather limited. na et al. (2010) examined the contributing factors to accidents. the factors found to have an effect on accident probability, from lowest to highest are: traffic signal timing, driving behavior, weather condition, roadway surface, and visibility. jie et al. (2015) applied a variation of the grey verhulst model to forecast annual high speed traffic accidents. they reported  $9.5\%$  accuracy in mape. jiang and li (2005) applied the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model to estimate pavement roughness index. the reported errors in mape ranged between 5 and  $45\%$ .

the above review highlights the effectiveness of the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model and its various enhancements. a particularly promising approach is the incorporation of trigonometric terms into the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model. this study further contributes to the literature by incorporating a cosine function, a combined sine and cosine function, and an exponential term into the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model for short-term prediction of different traffic parameters.

# 3. methods

# 3.1.  $gm(1,1)$  model

to model time series, grey system theory provides a family of grey models, where the first order grey model is referred to as  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$ . the underlying theory of the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model is as follows. notation was adopted from kayacan et al. (2010); bezuglov and comert (2016) and presented here for completeness and initiation to the proposed new grey models.

let  $x^{(0)} = (x^{(0)}(1), x^{(0)}(2), \ldots, x^{(0)}(n))$  denote a sequence of non-negative observations of a stochastic process and  $x^{(1)} = (x^{(1)}(1), x^{(1)}(2), \ldots, x^{(1)}(n))$  be an accumulation sequence of  $x^{(0)}$  where

$$
x ^ {(1)} (k) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {k} x ^ {(0)} (i) \tag {1}
$$

the original form of  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  is defined by the following equation.

$$
x ^ {(0)} (k) + a x ^ {(1)} (k) = b \tag {2}
$$

let  $z^{(1)} = (z^{(1)}(2), z^{(1)}(3), \ldots, z^{(1)}(n))$  be a mean sequence of  $x^{(1)}$  where

$$
z ^ {(1)} (k) = \frac {z ^ {(1)} (k - 1) + z ^ {(1)} (k)}{2}, \forall k = 2, 3, \dots , n \tag {3}
$$

the basic form of  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  is given by the following equation.

$$
x ^ {(0)} (k) + a z ^ {(1)} (k) = b \tag {4}
$$

if  $\hat{a} = (a,b)^t$  and

$$
y = \left[ \begin{array}{c} x ^ {(0)} (2) \\ x ^ {(0)} (3) \\ \vdots \\ x ^ {(0)} (n) \end{array} \right], b = \left[ \begin{array}{c c} - z ^ {(1)} (2) & 1 \\ - z ^ {(1)} (3) & 1 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ - z ^ {(1)} (n) & 1 \end{array} \right]. \tag {5}
$$

then, as in liu and lin (2006), the least squares estimate of the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model is  $\hat{a} = (b^t b)^{-1}b^t y$  and the whitenization equation of the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  model is given by,

$$
\frac {d x ^ {(1)}}{d t} + a x ^ {(1)} (k) = b \tag {6}
$$

suppose that  $\hat{x}^{(0)}(k)$  and  $\hat{x}^{(1)}(k)$  represent the time response sequence (the forecast) and the accumulated time response sequence of  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  at time  $k$  respectively. then, the latter can be obtained by solving eq. (6):

$$
\hat {x} ^ {(1)} (k + 1) = \left(x ^ {(0)} (1) - \frac {b}{a}\right) e ^ {- a k} + \frac {b}{a}, k = 1, 2, \dots , n \tag {7}
$$

from eq. (1), the restored values of  $\hat{x}^{(0)}(k + 1)$  are calculated as  $\hat{x}^{(1)}(k + 1) - \hat{x}^{(1)}(k)$ . using eq. 7, the following prediction equation is obtained.

$$
\hat {x} ^ {(0)} (k + 1) = \left(1 - e ^ {a}\right) \left(x ^ {(0)} (1) - \frac {b}{a}\right) e ^ {- a k}, k = 1, 2, \dots , n \tag {8}
$$

eq. (8) is the main forecasting equation that generates values  $\forall k = 2,3,\dots,n$ . in the rolling window approach:  $x^{(0)}(k + 1), x^{(0)}(k + 2), \ldots, x^{(0)}(k + w)$ , where  $w \geq 4$  is the window size (e.g.,  $w = 4$  found to produce very good results bezuglov and comert (2016)). forecasts for next data points can be calculated as  $\hat{x}^{(0)}(k + w + 1), \hat{x}^{(0)}(k + w + 2)$ .

# 3.2. the grey verhulst model (gvm)

the grey verhulst model (gvm) is generally used for more nonlinearly behaving data (liu et al. (2010)). underlying structure of the gvm is given by the following equation kayacan et al. (2010); bezuglov and comert (2016).

$$
x ^ {(0)} (k) + a z ^ {(1)} (k) = b \left(z ^ {(1)} (k)\right) ^ {2} \tag {9}
$$

the whitenization equation of gvm is:

$$
\frac {d x ^ {(1)}}{d t} + a x ^ {(1)} = b \left(x ^ {(1)}\right) ^ {2} \tag {10}
$$

similar to the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$ , the least squares estimate is applied to find  $\hat{a} = (b^t b)^{-1}b^t y$ , where

$$
y = \left[ \begin{array}{c} x ^ {(0)} (2) \\ x ^ {(0)} (3) \\ \vdots \\ x ^ {(0)} (n) \end{array} \right], b = \left[ \begin{array}{c c} - z ^ {(1)} (2) & z ^ {(1)} (2) ^ {2} \\ - z ^ {(1)} (3) & z ^ {(1)} (3) ^ {2} \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ - z ^ {(1)} (n) & z ^ {(1)} (n) ^ {2} \end{array} \right]. \tag {11}
$$

the forecasts  $\hat{x}^{(0)}(k + 1)$  are calculated using eq. (12).

$$
\hat {x} ^ {(0)} (k + 1) = \left[ \frac {a x ^ {(0)} (1) \left(a - b x ^ {(0)} (1)\right)}{b x ^ {(0)} (1) + \left(a - b x ^ {(0)} (1)\right) e ^ {a (k - 1)}} \right] \left[ \frac {\left(1 - e ^ {a}\right) e ^ {a (k - 2)}}{b x ^ {(0)} (1) + \left(a - b x ^ {(0)} (1)\right) e ^ {a (k - 2)}} \right] \tag {12}
$$

# 3.3.  $gm(1,1|\sin (\omega t))$  model

the idea of this proposed model is to enhance prediction of the original  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  under oscillating behavior. this is accomplished by introducing trigonometric terms on the right hand side (rhs). in order to investigate possible improvements in the model performances, we used a single sine function, a single cosine function, a linear combination of sine and cosine functions, and a linear combination of sine and cosine function multiplied by an exponential term.  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1|\sin (\omega t))$  model is adopted from shuhua et al. (2012) and derived via eq. 13 using the same steps as  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1)$  and gvm models.

$$
\frac {d x ^ {(1)}}{d t} + a x ^ {(1)} (k) = b _ {1} \sin (\omega t) + b _ {2} \tag {13}
$$

model parameters  $(\hat{a},\hat{b}_1,\hat{b}_2)^t = (a,b_1,b_2)^t$  are estimated by  $(\hat{a},\hat{b}_1,\hat{b}_2) = (b^t b)^{-1}b^t y$  where  $y = [x^{(0)}(2),\dots,x^{(0)}(n)]^t$  is given above and  $b$  is:

$$
b = \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} - z ^ {(1)} (2) & \sin (\omega 2) & 1 \\ - z ^ {(1)} (3) & \sin (\omega 3) & 1 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \\ - z ^ {(1)} (n) & \sin (\omega n) & 1 \end{array} \right]. \tag {14}
$$

suppose that  $\hat{x}^{(0)}(k)$  and  $\hat{x}^{(1)}(k)$  represent the time response sequence (the forecast) and the accumulated time response sequence of  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1|\sin (\omega t))$  at time  $k$  respectively. then, the latter can be obtained by solving the following equation.

$$
\begin{array}{l} x ^ {(1)} (t + 1) = \left(x ^ {(1)} (1) + \frac {b _ {1} \omega}{a ^ {2} + \omega^ {2}} - \frac {b}{a}\right) e ^ {- a t} + \tag {15} \\ \frac {b _ {1} \omega}{a ^ {2} + \omega^ {2}} (a s i n (\omega t) - \omega c o s (\omega t)) + \frac {b}{a} \\ \end{array}

$$

\psi (b) = \frac {\phi (b) \phi (b) (1 - b) ^ {d} (1 - b) ^ {d}}{\theta (b)} \tag {35}
$$

using eq. (35), one can obtain the forecasting formula for arima(1,1,2) as follows.

$$
(1 - \phi b) (1 - b) (z _ {t} - \mu) = (1 - \theta_ {1} b - \theta_ {2} b ^ {2}) a _ {t}
$$

$$
\hat {z} _ {t + 1} = z _ {t} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {\infty} b \psi_ {i} \tag {36}
$$

where  $\psi_0 = 1$ ,  $\psi_{1} = 1 + \phi -\theta_{1}$ ,  $\psi_{2} = \psi_{1}\theta_{1} - \phi -\theta_{2}$ , and  $\psi_{i} = \psi_{i - 1}\theta_{1} + \theta_{2}$  for  $i > 2$ . similarly, sarima(1,0,3)(1,0,0) forecasts are calculated from eq. (37). arima and sarima model parameters (e.g.,  $\phi$  and  $\theta$ s) are trained per dataset and traffic parameter and kept constant otherwise.

$$
(1 - \phi b) (1 - \phi b ^ {1 8}) (z _ {t} - \mu) = (1 - \theta_ {1} b - \theta_ {2} b ^ {2} - \theta_ {3} b ^ {3}) a _ {t}
$$

$$
\hat {z} _ {t + 1} = \left(1 - \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {\infty} \psi_ {i} \mu\right) + z _ {t} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {\infty} b \psi_ {i} \tag {37}
$$

where  $\psi_0 = 1$ ,  $\psi_{1} = \phi -\theta_{1}$ ,  $\psi_{2} = \psi_{1}\theta_{1} - \theta_{2}$ ,  $\psi_{3} = \psi_{2}\theta_{1} + \psi_{2}\theta_{2} - \theta_{3}$ , and  $\psi_{i} = \psi_{i - 1}\theta_{1} + \psi_{i - 2}\theta_{2} + \psi_{i - 3}\theta_{3}$  for  $i > 3$ ,  $\psi_{i} = \psi_{i - 1}\theta_{1} + \psi_{i - 2}\theta_{2} + \psi_{i - 3}\theta_{3} + \phi$ , for  $i = 18$ ,  $\psi_{i} = \psi_{i - 1}\theta_{1} + \psi_{i - 2}\theta_{2} + \psi_{i - 3}\theta_{3} - \phi\phi$ , for  $i = 19$ .

$$
z _ {t + 1} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l} \phi_ {1} + \phi_ {1 0} z _ {t} + \phi_ {1 1} z _ {t - \delta} + \dots + \phi_ {1 l} z _ {t - (l - 1) \delta} + \epsilon_ {t + 1}, x _ {t} \leq t h \\ \phi_ {2} + \phi_ {2 0} z _ {t} + \phi_ {2 1} z _ {t - \delta} + \dots + \phi_ {2 h} z _ {t - (h - 1) \delta} + \epsilon_ {t + 1}, x _ {t} > t h \end{array} \right. \tag {38}
$$

where  $z_{t}$  denotes observed traffic parameters at time  $t$ ,  $l = 1$  to 5 and  $h = 1$  to 5 low and high regimes,  $x_{t}$  is threshold function (the transition variable),  $\delta$  delay of the transition variable,  $th$  is the threshold value.

$$
z _ {t + 1} = \mu + \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {m - 1} s _ {j} \left(z _ {t - (j) \delta}\right) \tag {39}
$$

in eq. 39,  $s$  represents nonparametric univariate smoothing function that depends on  $z_{t}$ ,  $\delta$  is the delay parameter,  $m$  denotes embedding dimension,  $d$  is hidden layer of the neural network, and  $\beta_{i}, \gamma_{0j}, \gamma_{ij}$  represent the weights. splines from gaussian family are fitted in the form of  $z_{t+1} \sim \sum_{i=0}^{m-1} s(z_{t},..,z_{t-j})$ .

# 6. conclusions

this paper introduced new grey system models to forecast traffic parameters and compared them to other benchmark models. overall, the proposed grey models yielded more accurate forecasts. the  $\mathrm{gm}(1,1|\cos (\omega t))$  model outperformed other grey models when the system has abrupt changes. the key advantages of using the proposed grey system models are: (1) simple expert system with well-defined mathematics and straightforward adaptation to include multidimensional data such as weather and other location specific observations, and (2) simple implementation that would allow the model to be implemented and deployed on portable electronic devices such as smart phones and tablets (vlahogianni et al. (2014)).

future work in this area could include exploration of whitenization functions, long-term predictions, and the use of multivariable grey models. these models could be further extended for use on interrupted facilities (i.e., highways instead of freeways).
