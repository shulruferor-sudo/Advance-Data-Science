# abstract

in the last few years, various communication compression techniques have emerged as an indispensable tool helping to alleviate the communication bottleneck in distributed learning. however, despite the fact biased compressors often show superior performance in practice when compared to the much more studied and understood unbiased compressors, very little is known about them. in this work we study three classes of biased compression operators, two of which are new, and their performance when applied to (stochastic) gradient descent and distributed (stochastic) gradient descent. we show for the first time that biased compressors can lead to linear convergence rates both in the single node and distributed settings. we prove that distributed compressed sgd method, employed with error feedback mechanism, enjoys the ergodic rate  $\mathcal{o}\left(\delta l\exp \left[-\frac{\mu k}{\delta l}\right] + \frac{(c + \delta d)}{k\mu}\right)$ , where  $\delta \geq 1$  is a compression parameter which grows when more compression is applied,  $l$  and  $\mu$  are the smoothness and strong convexity constants,  $c$  captures stochastic gradient noise ( $c = 0$  if full gradients are

computed on each node) and  $d$  captures the variance of the gradients at the optimum  $(d = 0$  for over-parameterized models). further, via a theoretical study of several synthetic and empirical distributions of communicated gradients, we shed light on why and by how much biased compressors outperform their unbiased variants. finally, we propose several new biased compressors with promising theoretical guarantees and practical performance.

keywords: compression operators, biased compressors, distributed learning, linear convergence, error feedback

# 1 introduction

in order to achieve state-of-the-art performance, modern machine learning models need to be trained using large corpora of training data, and often feature an even larger number of trainable parameters (vaswani et al., 2019; brown et al., 2020). the data is typically collected in a distributed manner and stored across a network of edge devices, as is the case in federated learning (konečný et al., 2016; mcmahan et al., 2017; li et al., 2019; kairouz and et al, 2019), or collected centrally in a data warehouse composed of a large collection of commodity clusters. in either scenario, communication among the workers is typically the bottleneck.

motivated by the need for more efficient training methods in traditional distributed and emerging federated environments, we consider optimization problems of the form

$$
\min  _ {x \in \mathbb {r} ^ {d}} \left\{f (x) := \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f _ {i} (x) \right\}, \tag {1}
$$

where  $x \in \mathbb{r}^d$  collects the parameters of a statistical model to be trained,  $n$  is the number of workers/devices, and  $f_{i}(x)$  is the loss incurred by model  $x$  on data stored on worker  $i$ . the loss function  $f_{i}: \mathbb{r}^{d} \to \mathbb{r}$  often has the form

$$
f _ {i} (x) := \operatorname {e} _ {\xi \sim \mathcal {p} _ {i}} \left[ f _ {\xi} (x) \right],
$$

with  $\mathcal{p}_i$  being the distribution of training data owned by worker  $i$ . in federated learning applications, these local distributions can be very different and we do not impose any similarity assumption for them.

# 1.1 distributed optimization

a fundamental baseline for solving problem (1) is (distributed) gradient descent (gd), performing updates of the form

$$
x ^ {k + 1} = x ^ {k} - \frac {\eta^ {k}}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \nabla f _ {i} (x ^ {k}),
$$

where  $\eta^k > 0$  is a stepsize. due to the communication issues inherent to distributed systems, several enhancements to this baseline have been proposed that can better deal with the communication cost challenges of distributed environments, including acceleration (nesterov, 2013; beck and teboulle, 2009; allen-zhu, 2017), reducing the number of iterations via momentum, local methods (mcmahan et al., 2017; khaled et al., 2020a; karimireddy et al.,

2019a), reducing the number of communication rounds via performing multiple local updates before each communication round, and communication compression (seide et al., 2014; alistarh et al., 2017; zhang et al., 2017; lim et al., 2018; alistarh et al., 2018; lin et al., 2018; safaryan and richtárik, 2021), reducing the size of communicated messages via compression operators.

# 1.2 contributions

in this paper we contribute to a better understanding of the latter approach to alleviating the communication bottleneck: communication compression. in particular, we study the theoretical properties of gradient-type methods which employ biased gradient compression operators, such as top- $k$  sparsification (alistarh et al., 2018), or deterministic rounding (sapio et al., 2019). surprisingly, current * theoretical understanding of such methods is very limited. for instance, there is no general theory of such methods even in the  $n = 1$  case, only a handful of biased compression techniques have been proposed in the literature, we do not have any theoretical understanding of why biased compression operators could outperform their unbiased counterparts and when. more importantly, there is no good convergence theory for any gradient-type method with a biased compression in the crucial  $n > 1$  setting.

in this work we address all of the above problems. in particular, our main contributions are:

(a) we define and study three parametric classes of biased compression operators (see section 2), which we denote  $\mathbb{b}^1 (\alpha ,\beta)$ ,  $\mathbb{b}^2 (\gamma ,\beta)$  and  $\mathbb{b}^3 (\delta)$ , the first two of which are new. we prove that, despite they are alternative parameterization of the same collection of operators, the last two are more favorable than the first one, thus highlighting the importance of parametrization and providing further reductions. we show how is the commonly used class of unbiased compression operators, which we denote  $\mathbb{u}(\zeta)$ , relates to these biased classes. we also study scaling and compositions of such compressors.  
(b) we then proceed to give a long list of new and known biased (and some unbiased) compression operators which belong to the above classes in section 2.2. a summary of all compressors considered can be found in table 3.  
(c) in section 3 we analyze compressed gd in the  $n = 1$  case for compressors belonging to all three classes under smoothness and strong convexity assumption. our theorems generalize existing results which hold for unbiased operators in a tight manner, and also recover the rate of gd in this regime. our linear convergence results are summarized in table 1.  
(d) we ask the question: do biased compressors outperform their unbiased counterparts in theory, and by how much? we answer this question by studying the performance of compressors under various synthetic and empirical statistical assumptions on the distribution of the entries of gradient vectors which need to be compressed. particularly, we quantify the gains of the top- $k$  sparsifier when compared against the unbiased rand- $k$  sparsifier in section 4.

# 1.3 related work

there has been extensive work related to communication compression, mostly focusing on unbiased compressions (alistarh et al., 2017) as these are much easier to analyze. in particular, it was shown (gorbunov et al., 2020a) that both the classical method with unbiased compression (alistarh et al., 2017) and more advanced modifications (mishchenko et al., 2019; horváth et al., 2019b) can be considered as special versions of sgd. subsequently, the results of (gorbunov et al., 2020a) for strongly convex problems were transferred to general convex (khaled et al., 2020b) and non-convex (li and richtárik, 2020) target functions. in the meantime, works concerning biased compressions show stronger empirical results but with limited or no analysis (vogels et al., 2019; lin et al., 2017a; sun et al., 2019). there have been several attempts trying to address this issue, e.g., wu et al. (2018) provided analysis for quadratics in distributed setting, zhao et al. (2019) gave analysis for momentum

sgd with a specific biased compression, but under unreasonable assumptions, i.e., bounded gradient norm and memory. the first result that obtained linear rate of convergence for biased compression was done by karimireddy et al. (2019b), but only for one node and under bounded gradient norm assumption, which was later overcome by stich and karimireddy (2019).

after the initial online appearance of our work, there has been several enhancements in the literature. in particular, ajalloeian and stich (2021) developed theory for nonconvex objectives in the single node setup, gorbunov et al. (2020b) designed a novel error compensated sgd algorithm converging linearly in a more relaxed setting with the help of additional unbiased compressor, horváth and richtárik (2021) proposed a simple trick to convert any biased compressor to corresponding induced (unbiased) compressor leading to improved theoretical guarantees. recently, a new variant of error feedback mechanism was introduced in (richtárik et al., 2021; fatkhullin et al., 2021) showing an improved rates for distributed non-convex problems.

# 1.4 basic notation and definitions

we use  $\langle x,y\rangle \coloneqq \sum_{i = 1}^{d}x_iy_i$  to denote standard inner product of  $x,y\in \mathbb{r}^d$ , where  $x_{i}$  corresponds to the  $i$ -th component of  $x$  in the standard basis in  $\mathbb{r}^d$ . this induces the  $\ell_2$ -norm in  $\mathbb{r}^d$  in the following way  $\| x\| _2\coloneqq \sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle}$ . we denote  $\ell_p$ -norms as  $\| x\| _p\coloneqq (\sum_{i = 1}^{d}|x_i|^p)^{1 / p}$  for  $p\in (1,\infty)$ . by e  $[\cdot ]$  we denote mathematical expectation. for a given differentiable function  $f:\mathbb{r}^d\to \mathbb{r}$ , we say that it is  $l$ -smooth if

$$
f (y) \leq f (x) + \langle \nabla f (x), y - x \rangle + \frac {l}{2} \| y - x \| _ {2} ^ {2}, \quad \forall x, y \in \mathbb {r} ^ {d}.
$$

we say that it is  $\mu$ -strongly convex if

$$
f (y) \geq f (x) + \langle \nabla f (x), y - x \rangle + \frac {\mu}{2} \| y - x \| _ {2} ^ {2}, \quad \forall x, y \in \mathbb {r} ^ {d}.
$$

# 2 biased compressors

by compression operator we mean a (possibly random) mapping  $\mathcal{c} \colon \mathbb{r}^d \to \mathbb{r}^d$  with some constraints. typically, literature considers unbiased compression operators  $\mathcal{c}$  with a bounded second moment, i.e.

definition 1 let  $\zeta \geq 1$ . we say that  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{u}(\zeta)$  if  $\mathcal{c}$  is unbiased (i.e.,  $\operatorname{e}[\mathcal{c}(x)] = x$  for all  $x$ ) and if the second moment is bounded as

$$
\operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] \leq \zeta \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2}, \quad \forall x \in \mathbb {r} ^ {d}. \tag {2}
$$

# 2.1 three classes of biased compressors

we instead focus on understanding biased compression operators, or "compressors" in short. we now introduce three classes of biased compressors, the first two are new, which can be seen as natural extensions of unbiased compressors.

definition 2 we say that  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^1(\alpha, \beta)$  for some  $\alpha, \beta > 0$  if

$$
\alpha \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} \leq \operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] \leq \beta \langle \operatorname {e} \left[ \mathcal {c} (x) \right], x \rangle , \quad \forall x \in \mathbb {r} ^ {d}. \tag {3}
$$

as we shall show next, the second inequality in (3) implies  $\operatorname{e}\left[\| \mathcal{c}(x)\| _2^2\right] \leq \beta^2\| x\| _2^2$ .

lemma 3 for any  $x \in \mathbb{r}^d$ , if  $\operatorname{e}\left[\| \mathcal{c}(x) \|_2^2\right] \leq \beta \langle \operatorname{e}\left[\mathcal{c}(x)\right], x \rangle$ , then

$$
\operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] \leq \beta^ {2} \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2}. \tag {4}
$$

proof fix any  $x \in \mathbb{r}^d$ . applying jensen's inequality, the second inequality in (3) and cauchy-schwarz, we get

$$
\| \operatorname {e} [ \mathcal {c} (x) ] \| _ {2} ^ {2} \leq \operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] \stackrel {(3)} {\leq} \beta \langle \operatorname {e} [ \mathcal {c} (x) ], x \rangle \leq \beta \| \operatorname {e} [ \mathcal {c} (x) ] \| _ {2} \| x \| _ {2}. \tag {5}
$$

if  $\operatorname{e}[\mathcal{c}(x)] \neq 0$ , this implies  $\| \operatorname{e}[\mathcal{c}(x)] \|_2 \leq \beta \| x \|_2$ . plugging this back into (5), we get (4). if  $\operatorname{e}[\mathcal{c}(x)] = 0$ , then from (3) we see that  $\operatorname{e} \left[ \| \mathcal{c}(x) \|_2^2 \right] = 0$ , and (4) holds trivially.

in the second class, we require the inner product between uncompressed  $x$  and compressed  $\mathcal{c}(x)$  vectors to dominate the squared norms of both vectors in expectation.

definition 4 we say that  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^2(\gamma, \beta)$  for some  $\gamma, \beta > 0$  if

$$
\max  \left\{\gamma \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2}, \frac {1}{\beta} \mathrm {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] \right\} \leq \langle \mathrm {e} [ \mathcal {c} (x) ], x \rangle \quad \forall x \in \mathbb {r} ^ {d}. \tag {6}
$$

finally, in the third class, we require the compression error  $\| \mathcal{c}(x) - x\| _2^2$  to be strictly smaller than the squared norm  $\| x\| _2^2$  of the input vector  $x$  in expectation.

definition 5 we say  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^3(\delta)$  for some  $\delta > 0$  if

$$
\operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) - x \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] \leq \left(1 - \frac {1}{\delta}\right) \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2}, \quad \forall x \in \mathbb {r} ^ {d}. \tag {7}
$$

this last definition was also considered by stich et al. (2018); cordonnier (2018). all three definitions require the compressed vector  $\mathcal{c}(x)$  to be in the neighborhood of the uncompressed vector  $x$  so that initial information is preserved with some accuracy. we now establish several basic properties and connections between the classes. we first show that the three classes of biased compressors defined above are equivalent in the following sense: a compressor from any of those three classes can be shown to belong to all three classes with different parameters and after possible scaling.

theorem 6 (equivalence between biased compressors) let  $\lambda >0$  be a free scaling parameter.

1. if  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^1(\alpha, \beta)$ , then

(i)  $\beta^2\geq \alpha$  and  $\lambda \mathcal{c}\in \mathbb{b}^{1}(\lambda^{2}\alpha ,\lambda \beta)$  ，and  
(ii)  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^2(\alpha/\beta, \beta)$  and  $\frac{1}{\beta}\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^3(\beta^2/\alpha)$ .

2. if  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^2(\gamma, \beta)$ , then

(i)  $\beta \geq \gamma$  and  $\lambda \mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^2 (\lambda \gamma ,\lambda \beta)$ , and  
(ii)  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^1(\gamma^2, \beta)$  and  $\frac{1}{\beta}\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^3(\beta/\gamma)$ .

3. if  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^3(\delta)$ , then

(i)  $\delta \geq 1$ , and  
(ii)  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^2\left(\frac{1}{2\delta}, 2\right) \subseteq \mathbb{b}^1\left(\frac{1}{4\delta^2}, 2\right)$ .

proof let us prove this implications for each class separately.

1. case  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^1(\alpha, \beta)$ :

(i) let us choose any  $x \neq 0$  and observe that (3) implies that  $\operatorname{e}[\mathcal{c}(x)] \neq 0$ . further, from (3) we get the bounds

$$
\frac {\operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right]}{\langle \operatorname {e} [ \mathcal {c} (x) ] , x \rangle} \leq \beta , \quad \alpha \leq \frac {\operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right]}{\| x \| _ {2} ^ {2}}.
$$

finally,

$$
\beta^ {2} \geq \left(\frac {\operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right]}{\langle \operatorname {e} [ \mathcal {c} (x) ] , x \rangle}\right) ^ {2} \geq \frac {\operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] \operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right]}{\| \operatorname {e} [ \mathcal {c} (x) ] \| _ {2} ^ {2} \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2}} \geq \alpha \frac {\operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right]}{\| \operatorname {e} [ \mathcal {c} (x) ] \| _ {2} ^ {2}} \geq \alpha ,
$$

where the second inequality is due to cauchy-schwarz, and the last inequality follows by applying jensen inequality.

the scaling property  $\lambda \mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^1(\alpha \lambda^2, \beta \lambda)$  follows directly from (3).

(ii) the inclusion  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^2(\alpha/\beta, \beta)$  follows directly from inequalities (3) and (6). in view of (i),  $\lambda \mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^1(\lambda^2\alpha, \lambda\beta)$ . if we choose  $\lambda \leq \frac{2}{\beta}$ , then

$$
\begin{array}{l} \operatorname {e} \left[ \| \lambda \mathcal {c} (x) - x \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] = \operatorname {e} \left[ \| \lambda \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] - 2 \langle \operatorname {e} [ \lambda \mathcal {c} (x) ], x \rangle + \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} \\ \stackrel {(3)} {\leq} \quad (\beta \lambda - 2) \left\langle \operatorname {e} \left[ \lambda \mathcal {c} (x) \right], x \right\rangle + \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} \\ \stackrel {(3)} {\leq} \quad (\beta \lambda - 2) \frac {\alpha \lambda^ {2}}{\beta \lambda} \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} + \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} \\ \stackrel {(3)} {\leq} \left(\alpha \lambda^ {2} - 2 \frac {\alpha}{\beta} \lambda + 1\right) \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2}. \\ \end{array}
$$

minimizing the above expression in  $\lambda$ , we get  $\lambda = \frac{1}{\beta}$ , and the result follows.

2. case  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^2(\gamma, \beta)$ .

(i) using (6) we get

$$
\gamma \leq \frac {\langle \operatorname {e} [ \mathcal {c} (x) ] , x \rangle}{\| x \| _ {2} ^ {2}} \leq \frac {\operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right]}{\sqrt {\operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2}}} \leq \beta \frac {\langle \operatorname {e} [ \mathcal {c} (x) ] , x \rangle}{\sqrt {\operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2}}} \leq \beta ,
$$

where the first and third inequalities follow from (6) and the third and the last from cauchy-schwarz inequality with jensen inequality.

the scaling property  $\lambda \mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^2 (\lambda \gamma ,\lambda \beta)$  follows directly from (6).

(ii) if  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^2(\gamma, \beta)$ , then  $\operatorname{e}\left[\| \mathcal{c}(x) \|_2^2\right] \leq \beta \langle \operatorname{e}\left[\mathcal{c}(x)\right], x \rangle$  and

$$
\gamma^ {2} \left\| x \right\| _ {2} ^ {4} \stackrel {(6)} {\leq} \langle \operatorname {e} \left[ \mathcal {c} (x) \right], x \rangle^ {2} \leq \left\| \operatorname {e} \left[ \mathcal {c} (x) \right] \right\| _ {2} ^ {2} \left\| x \right\| _ {2} ^ {2} \leq \operatorname {e} \left[ \left\| \mathcal {c} (x) \right\| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] \left\| x \right\| _ {2} ^ {2},
$$

where the second inequality is cauchy-schwarz, and the third is jensen. therefore,  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^1(\gamma^2, \beta)$ .

further, for any  $\lambda > 0$ , we get

$$
\begin{array}{l} \operatorname {e} \left[ \| \lambda \mathcal {c} (x) - x \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] = \operatorname {e} \left[ \| \lambda \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] - 2 \langle \operatorname {e} [ \lambda \mathcal {c} (x) ], x \rangle + \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} \\ = \lambda^ {2} \mathrm {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] - 2 \lambda \langle \mathrm {e} [ \mathcal {c} (x) ], x \rangle + \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} \\ \stackrel {(6)} {\leq} \quad (\lambda \beta - 2) \lambda \langle \operatorname {e} [ \mathcal {c} (x) ], x \rangle + \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2}. \\ \end{array}
$$

if we choose  $\lambda = \frac{1}{\beta}$ , then we can continue as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \operatorname {e} \left[ \| \lambda \mathcal {c} (x) - x \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] \leq - \frac {1}{\beta} \langle \operatorname {e} \left[ \mathcal {c} (x) \right], x \rangle + \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} \\ \stackrel {(6)} {\leq} \left(1 - \frac {\gamma}{\beta}\right) \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2}, \\ \end{array}
$$

whence  $\frac{1}{\beta}\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^3(\beta/\gamma)$ .

3. case  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^3(\delta)$ .

(i) pick  $x \neq 0$ . since  $0 \leq \operatorname{e}\left[\| \mathcal{c}(x) - x\|_2^2\right] \leq \left(1 - \frac{1}{\delta}\right)\| x\|_2^2$  and we assume  $\delta > 0$ , we must necessarily have  $\delta \geq 1$ .  
(ii) if  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^3(\delta)$  then

$$
\operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] - 2 \left\langle \operatorname {e} \left[ \mathcal {c} (x) \right], x \right\rangle + \frac {1}{\delta} \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} \leq 0,
$$

which implies that

$$
\frac {1}{2 \delta} \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} \leq \left\langle \operatorname {e} \left[ \mathcal {c} (x) \right], x \right\rangle \quad \text {a n d} \quad \operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] \leq 2 \left\langle \operatorname {e} \left[ \mathcal {c} (x) \right], x \right\rangle .
$$

therefore,  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^2\left(\frac{1}{2\delta}, 2\right) \subseteq \mathbb{b}^1\left(\frac{1}{4\delta^2}, 2\right)$ .

next, we show that, with a proper scaling, any unbiased compressor also belongs to all the three classes of biased compressors.

theorem 7 (from unbiased to biased with scaling) if  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{u}(\zeta)$ , then for the scaled operator  $\lambda \mathcal{c}$  we have

(i)  $\lambda \mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^1(\lambda^2, \lambda \zeta)$  for  $\lambda > 0$ ,  
(ii)  $\lambda \mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^2(\lambda, \lambda \zeta)$  for  $\lambda > 0$ ,  
(iii)  $\lambda \mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^3\left(\frac{1}{\lambda(2 - \zeta\lambda)}\right)$  for  $\zeta \lambda < 2$ .

proof let  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{u}(\zeta)$ .

(i) given any  $\lambda > 0$ , consider the scaled operator  $\lambda c$ . we have

$$
\lambda^ {2} \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} = \left\| \operatorname {e} \left[ \lambda \mathcal {c} (x) \right] \right\| _ {2} ^ {2} \leq \operatorname {e} \left[ \| \lambda \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] \leq \lambda^ {2} \zeta \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} = \lambda \zeta \langle \operatorname {e} [ \lambda \mathcal {c} (x) ], x \rangle ,
$$

whence  $\mathcal{c}\in \mathbb{b}^1 (\lambda^2,\lambda \zeta)$

(ii) given any  $\lambda > 0$ , consider the scaled operator  $\lambda c$ . we have

$$
\lambda \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} = \langle \operatorname {e} [ \lambda \mathcal {c} (x) ], x \rangle ,
$$

$$
\operatorname {e} \left[ \| \lambda \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] \leq \lambda^ {2} \zeta \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} = \lambda \zeta \langle \operatorname {e} [ \lambda \mathcal {c} (x) ], x \rangle ,
$$

whence  $\lambda \mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^2(\lambda, \lambda\zeta)$ .

(iii) given  $\lambda > 0$  such that  $\lambda \zeta < 2$ , consider the scaled operator  $\lambda \mathcal{c}$ . we have

$$
\begin{array}{l} \operatorname {e} \left[ \| \lambda \mathcal {c} (x) - x \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] = \operatorname {e} \left[ \| \lambda \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] - 2 \langle \operatorname {e} [ \lambda \mathcal {c} (x) ], x \rangle + \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} \\ \leq \left(\zeta \lambda^ {2} - 2 \lambda + 1\right) \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} \\ \end{array}
$$

whence  $\lambda \mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^3\left(\frac{1}{\lambda(2 - \zeta\lambda)}\right)$ .

we now give some examples of compression operators belonging to the classes  $\mathbb{b}^1$ ,  $\mathbb{b}^2$ ,  $\mathbb{b}^3$  and  $\mathbb{u}$ . several of them are new. for a summary, refer to table 3.

(a) for  $k \in [d] \coloneqq \{1, \ldots, d\}$ , the unbiased random (aka rand- $k$ ) sparsification operator is defined via

$$
\mathcal {c} (x) := \frac {d}{k} \sum_ {i \in s} x _ {i} e _ {i}, \tag {8}
$$

where  $s \subseteq [d]$  is the  $k$ -nice sampling; i.e., a subset of  $[d]$  of cardinality  $k$  chosen uniformly at random, and  $e_1, \ldots, e_d$  are the standard unit basis vectors in  $\mathbb{r}^d$ .

lemma 8 the rand-k sparsifier (8) belongs to  $\mathbb{u}\left(\frac{d}{k}\right)$ .

(b) let  $s \subseteq [d]$  be a random set, with probability vector  $p \coloneqq (p_1, \ldots, p_d)$ , where  $p_i \coloneqq \operatorname{prob}(i \in s) > 0$  for all  $i$  (such a set is called a proper sampling (richtárik and takáč, 2016)). define biased random sparsification operator via

$$
\mathcal {c} (x) := \sum_ {i \in s} x _ {i} e _ {i}. \tag {9}
$$

lemma 9 letting  $q \coloneqq \min_i p_i$ , the biased random sparsification operator (9) belongs to  $\mathbb{b}^1(q,1)$ ,  $\mathbb{b}^2(q,1)$ ,  $\mathbb{b}^3(1/q)$ .

(c) adaptive random sparsification is defined via

$$
\mathcal {c} (x) := x _ {i} e _ {i} \quad \text {w i t h p r o b a b i l i t y} \quad \frac {\left| x _ {i} \right|}{\left\| x \right\| _ {1}}. \tag {10}
$$

lemma 10 adaptive random sparsification operator (10) belongs to  $\mathbb{b}^1 (\frac{1}{d},1)$ ,  $\mathbb{b}^2 (\frac{1}{d},1)$ ,  $\mathbb{b}^3 (d)$ .

(d) greedy (aka top-  $k$  ) sparsification operator is defined via

$$
\mathcal {c} (x) := \sum_ {i = d - k + 1} ^ {d} x _ {(i)} e _ {(i)}, \tag {11}
$$

where coordinates are ordered by their magnitudes so that  $|x_{(1)}| \leq |x_{(2)}| \leq \dots \leq |x_{(d)}|$ .

lemma 11 top-k sparsification operator (11) belongs to  $\mathbb{b}^1 (\frac{k}{d},1),\mathbb{b}^2 (\frac{k}{d},1)$ , and  $\mathbb{b}^3 (\frac{d}{k})$

(e) let  $(a_{k})_{k\in \mathbb{z}}$  be an arbitrary increasing sequence of positive numbers such that inf  $a_{k} = 0$  and sup  $a_{k} = \infty$ . then general unbiased rounding  $\mathcal{c}$  is defined as follows: if  $a_{k}\leq |x_{i}|\leq a_{k + 1}$  for some coordinate  $i\in [d]$ , then

$$
\mathcal {c} (x) _ {i} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \operatorname {s i g n} \left(x _ {i}\right) a _ {k} & \text {w i t h p r o b a b i l i t y} \quad \frac {a _ {k + 1} - \left| x _ {i} \right|}{a _ {k + 1} - a _ {k}} \\ \operatorname {s i g n} \left(x _ {i}\right) a _ {k + 1} & \text {w i t h p r o b a b i l i t y} \quad \frac {\left| x _ {i} \right| - a _ {k}}{a _ {k + 1} - a _ {k}} \end{array} \right. \tag {12}
$$

lemma 12 general unbiased rounding operator (12) belongs to  $\mathbb{u}(\zeta)$ , where

$$
\zeta = \frac {1}{4} \sup  _ {k \in \mathbb {z}} \left(\frac {a _ {k}}{a _ {k + 1}} + \frac {a _ {k + 1}}{a _ {k}} + 2\right).
$$

notice that  $\zeta$  is minimizing for exponential roundings  $a_{k} = b^{k}$  with some basis  $b > 1$ , in which case  $\zeta = \frac{1}{4} (b + 1 / b + 2)$ .

(f) let  $(a_{k})_{k\in \mathbb{z}}$  be an arbitrary increasing sequence of positive numbers such that  $\inf a_k = 0$  and  $\sup a_{k} = \infty$ . then general biased rounding is defined via

$$
\mathcal {c} (x) _ {i} := \operatorname {s i g n} \left(x _ {i}\right) \arg \min  _ {t \in \left(a _ {k}\right)} | t - | x _ {i} | |, \quad i \in [ d ]. \tag {13}
$$

lemma 13 general biased rounding operator (13) belongs to  $\mathbb{b}^1 (\alpha ,\beta)$ ,  $\mathbb{b}^2 (\gamma ,\beta)$ , and  $\mathbb{b}^3 (\delta)$ , where

$$
\beta = \sup _ {k \in \mathbb {z}} \frac {2 a _ {k + 1}}{a _ {k} + a _ {k + 1}}, \qquad \gamma = \inf _ {k \in \mathbb {z}} \frac {2 a _ {k}}{a _ {k} + a _ {k + 1}}, \qquad \alpha = \gamma^ {2}, \qquad \delta = \sup _ {k \in \mathbb {z}} \frac {(a _ {k} + a _ {k + 1}) ^ {2}}{4 a _ {k} a _ {k + 1}}.
$$

in the special case of exponential rounding  $a_k = b^k$  with some base  $b > 1$ , we get

$$
\alpha = \left(\frac {2}{b + 1}\right) ^ {2}, \quad \beta = \frac {2 b}{b + 1}, \quad \gamma = \frac {2}{b + 1}, \quad \delta = \frac {(b + 1) ^ {2}}{4 b}.
$$

remark 14 plugging these parameters into the iteration complexities of table 1, we find that the class  $\mathbb{b}^3$  gives the best iteration complexity as  $\frac{\beta^2}{\alpha} = b^2 > \frac{\beta}{\gamma} = b > \delta = \frac{(b + 1)^2}{4b}$ .

(g) natural compression operator  $\mathcal{c}_{nat}$  of horváth et al. (2019a) is the special case of general unbiased rounding operator (12) when  $b = 2$ . so,

$$
\mathcal {c} _ {n a t} \in \mathbb {u} \left(\frac {9}{8}\right).
$$

(h) for  $b > 1$ , define general exponential dithering operator with respect to  $l_{p}$ -norm and with  $s$  exponential levels  $0 < b^{1 - s} < b^{2 - s} < \dots < b^{-1} < 1$  via

$$
\mathcal {c} (x) := \| x \| _ {p} \times \operatorname {s i g n} (x) \times \xi \left(\frac {\left| x _ {i} \right|}{\left\| x \right\| _ {p}}\right), \tag {14}
$$

where the random variable  $\xi(t)$  for  $t \in [b^{-u-1}, b^{-u}]$  is set to either  $b^{-u-1}$  or  $b^{-u}$  with probabilities proportional to  $b^{-u}-t$  and  $t-b^{-u-1}$ , respectively.

lemma 15 general exponential dithering operator (14) belongs to  $\mathbb{u}(\zeta_b)$ , where, letting  $r = \min(p,2)$ ,

$$
\zeta_ {b} = \frac {1}{4} \left(b + \frac {1}{b} + 2\right) + d ^ {\frac {1}{r}} b ^ {1 - s} \min  \left(1, d ^ {\frac {1}{r}} b ^ {1 - s}\right). \tag {15}
$$

(i) natural dithering introduced by horváth et al. (2019a) without norm compression is the spacial case of general exponential dithering (14) when  $b = 2$ .  
(j) ternary quantization of wen et al. (2017) is the extreme case of general exponential dithering (14) with  $s = 1$  levels and  $b = 1$ .  
(k) top- $k$  combined with exponential dithering. let  $\mathcal{c}_{top}$  be the top- $k$  sparsification operator (11) and  $\mathcal{c}_{dith}$  be general exponential dithering operator (14) with some base  $b > 1$  and parameter  $\zeta_{b}$  from (15). define a new compression operator as the composition of these two:

$$
\mathcal {c} (x) := \mathcal {c} _ {\text {d i t h}} \left(\mathcal {c} _ {\text {t o p}} (x)\right). \tag {16}
$$

lemma 16 the composition operator (16) of top- $k$  sparsification and exponential dithering with base  $b$  belongs to  $\mathbb{b}^1\left(\frac{k}{d},\zeta_b\right)$ ,  $\mathbb{b}^2\left(\frac{k}{d},\zeta_b\right)$ ,  $\mathbb{b}^3\left(\frac{d}{k}\zeta_b\right)$ , where  $\zeta_b$  is as in (15).

# 3 gradient descent with biased compression

as we discussed in previous section, compression operators can have different equivalent parametrizations. next, we aim to investigate the influence of those parametrizations on the theoretical convergence rate of an algorithm employing compression operators. to achieve clearer understanding of the interaction of compressor parametrization and convergence rate, we first consider the single node, unconstrained optimization problem

$$
\min  _ {x \in \mathbb {r} ^ {d}} f (x),
$$

where  $f:\mathbb{r}^d\to \mathbb{r}$  is  $l$  -smooth and  $\mu$  -strongly convex. we study the method

$$
\boxed {x ^ {k + 1} = x ^ {k} - \eta \mathcal {c} ^ {k} (\nabla f (x ^ {k}))}, \tag {cgd}
$$

where  $\mathcal{c}^k:\mathbb{r}^d\to \mathbb{r}^d$  are (potentially biased) compression operators belonging to one of the classes  $\mathbb{b}^1$ ,  $\mathbb{b}^2$  and  $\mathbb{b}^3$  studied in the previous sections, and  $\eta >0$  is a stepsize. we refer to this method as cgd: compressed gradient descent.

# 3.1 complexity theory

we now establish three theorems, performing complexity analysis for each of the three classes  $\mathbb{b}^1$ ,  $\mathbb{b}^2$  and  $\mathbb{b}^3$  individually. let  $\mathcal{e}_k \coloneqq \operatorname{e}\left[f(x^k)\right] - f(x^\star)$ , with  $\mathcal{e}_0 = f(x^0) - f(x^\star)$ .

theorem 17 let  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^1(\alpha, \beta)$ . then as long as  $0 \leq \eta \leq \frac{2}{\beta l}$ , we have  $\mathcal{e}_k \leq \left(1 - \frac{\alpha}{\beta} \eta \mu (2 - \eta \beta l)\right) \mathcal{e}_{k-1}$ . if we choose  $\eta = \frac{1}{\beta l}$ , then

$$
\mathcal {e} _ {k} \leq \left(1 - \frac {\alpha}{\beta^ {2}} \frac {\mu}{l}\right) ^ {k} \mathcal {e} _ {0}.
$$

theorem 18 let  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^2(\gamma, \beta)$ . then as long as  $0 \leq \eta \leq \frac{2}{\beta l}$ , we have  $\mathcal{e}_k \leq (1 - \gamma \eta(2 - \eta \beta)l))\mathcal{e}_{k-1}$ . if we choose  $\eta = \frac{1}{\beta l}$ , then

$$
\mathcal {e} _ {k} \leq \left(1 - \frac {\gamma}{\beta} \frac {\mu}{l}\right) ^ {k} \mathcal {e} _ {0}.
$$

theorem 19 let  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^3(\delta)$ . then as long as  $0 \leq \eta \leq \frac{1}{l}$ , we have  $\mathcal{e}_k \leq \left(1 - \frac{1}{\delta}\eta\mu\right)^k\mathcal{e}_{k-1}$ . if we choose  $\eta = \frac{1}{l}$ , then

$$
\mathcal {e} _ {k} \leq \left(1 - \frac {1}{\bar {\delta}} \frac {\mu}{l}\right) ^ {k} \mathcal {e} _ {0}.
$$

the iteration complexity for these results can be found in table 1. note that the identity compressor  $\mathcal{c}(x) \equiv x$  belongs to  $\mathbb{b}^1(1,1), \mathbb{b}^2(1,1)$ , and  $\mathbb{b}^3(1)$ , hence all these result exactly recover the rate of gd. in the first two theorems, scaling the compressor by a positive scalar  $\lambda > 0$  does not influence the rate (see theorem 6).

one can note that it is possible to adapt the results of theorems 17-19 to the case with the time-varying parameters of compression operators (lin et al., 2017b; agarwal et al., 2020). in the case of theorem 19 and  $\mathbb{b}^3$  compressors, it is sufficient to introduce a sequence  $\{\delta^i\}$  responsible for the changes of the parameter  $\delta$  and obtain the following convergence:  $\mathcal{e}_k \leq \left[\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\left(1 - \frac{1}{\delta^i} \frac{\mu}{l}\right)\right] \mathcal{e}_0$ . in the case of theorems 17 and 18, the results are similar, but it is necessary to add in the algorithm (cgd) the possibility of using time-varying steps  $\eta^k = \frac{1}{\beta^k l}$ .

# 3.2  $\mathbb{b}^3$  and  $\mathbb{b}^2$  are better than  $\mathbb{b}^1$

in light of the results above, we make the following observation. if  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^1(\alpha, \beta)$ , then by theorem 6,  $\frac{1}{\beta}\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^3\left(\frac{\beta^2}{\alpha}\right)$ . applying theorem 19, we get the bound  $\mathcal{o}\left(\frac{\beta^2}{\alpha}\frac{l}{\mu}\log \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)$ . this is the same result as that obtained by theorem 17. on the other hand, if  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^3(\delta)$ , then by theorem 6,  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^1\left(\frac{1}{4\delta^2},2\right)$ . applying theorem 17, we get the bound  $\mathcal{o}\left(16\delta^2\frac{l}{\mu}\log \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)$ . this is a worse result than what theorem 19 offers by a factor of  $16\delta$ .

similarly, if  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^1(\alpha, \beta)$ , then by theorem 6,  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^2\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}, \beta\right)$ . applying theorem 18, we get the bound  $\mathcal{o}\left(\frac{\beta^2}{\alpha} \frac{l}{\mu} \log \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)$ . this is the same result as that obtained by theorem 17. on the other hand, if  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^2(\gamma, \beta)$ , then by theorem 6,  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^1(\gamma^2, \beta)$ . applying theorem 17,

we get the bound  $\mathcal{o}\left(\frac{\beta^2}{\gamma^2}\frac{l}{\mu}\log \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)$ . this is a worse result than what theorem 18 offers by a factor of  $\frac{\beta}{\gamma} \geq 1$ .

hence, while  $\mathbb{b}^1, \mathbb{b}^2$  and  $\mathbb{b}^3$  describe the same classes of compressors, for the purposes of cgd it is better to parameterize them as members of  $\mathbb{b}^2$  or  $\mathbb{b}^3$ .

# 4 superiority of biased compressors under statistical assumptions

here we highlight some advantages of biased compressors by comparing them with their unbiased cousins. we evaluate compressors by their average capacity of preserving the gradient information or, in other words, by expected approximation error they produce. in the sequel, we assume that gradients have i.i.d. coordinates drawn from some distribution.

# 4.1 top- $k$  vs rand- $k$

we now compare two sparsification operators: rand-  $k$  (8) which is unbiased and which we denote as  $\mathcal{c}_{rnd}^k$ , and top-  $k$  (11) which is biased and which we denote as  $\mathcal{c}_{top}^k$ . we define variance of the approximation error of  $x$  via

$$
\omega_ {r n d} ^ {k} (x) := \operatorname {e} \left[ \left\| \frac {k}{d} \mathcal {c} _ {r n d} ^ {k} (x) - x \right\| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] = \left(1 - \frac {k}{d}\right) \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2}
$$

and

$$
\omega_ {t o p} ^ {k} (x) := \left\| \mathcal {c} _ {t o p} ^ {k} (x) - x \right\| _ {2} ^ {2} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {d - k} x _ {(i)} ^ {2}
$$

and the energy "saving" via

$$
s _ {r n d} ^ {k} (x) := \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} - \omega_ {r n d} ^ {k} (x) = \operatorname {e} \left[ \left\| \frac {k}{d} \mathcal {c} _ {r n d} ^ {k} (x) \right\| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] = \frac {k}{d} \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2}
$$

and

$$
s _ {t o p} ^ {k} (x) := \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} - \omega_ {t o p} ^ {k} (x) = \left\| \mathcal {c} _ {t o p} ^ {k} (x) \right\| _ {2} ^ {2} = \sum_ {i = d - k + 1} ^ {d} x _ {(i)} ^ {2}.
$$

expectations in these expressions are taken with respect to the randomization of the compression operator rather than input vector  $x$ . clearly, there exists  $x$  for which these two operators incur identical variance, e.g.  $x_{1} = \dots = x_{d}$ . however, in practice we apply compression to gradients  $x$  which evolve in time, and which may have heterogeneous components. in such situations,  $\omega_{top}^{k}(x)$  could be much smaller than  $\omega_{rnd}^{k}(x)$ . this motivates a quantitative study of the average case behavior in which we make an assumption on the distribution of the coordinates of the compressed vector.

uniform and exponential distribution. we first consider the case of uniform and exponentially distributed entries, and quantify the difference.

$$

# 4.2 new compressor: top- $k$  combined with dithering

in section 2.2 we gave a new biased compression operator (see (16)), where we combined top- $k$  sparsification operator (see (11)) with the general exponential dithering (see (14)).

consider the composition operator with natural dithering, i.e., with base  $b = 2$ . we showed that it belongs to  $\mathbb{b}^1\left(\frac{k}{d},\frac{9}{8}\right)$ ,  $\mathbb{b}^2\left(\frac{k}{d},\frac{9}{8}\right)$  and  $\mathbb{b}^3\left(\frac{9d}{8k}\right)$ . figure 3 empirically confirms that it attains the lowest compression parameter  $\delta \geq 1$  among all other known compressors (see (5)). furthermore, the iteration complexity  $\mathcal{o}\left(\delta \frac{l}{\mu}\log \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)$  of cgd for  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^3 (\delta)$  implies that it enjoys fastest convergence.

# 5 distributed setting

we now focus attention on a distributed setup with  $n$  machines, each of which owns non-iid data defining one loss function  $f_{i}$ . our goal is to minimize the average loss:

$$
\min  _ {x \in \mathbb {r} ^ {d}} \left\{f (x) := \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f _ {i} (x) \right\}. \tag {17}
$$

# 5.1 distributed cgd with unbiased compressors

perhaps the most straightforward extension of cgd to the distributed setting is to consider the method

$$
x ^ {k + 1} = x ^ {k} - \eta \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \mathcal {c} _ {i} ^ {k} (\nabla f _ {i} (x ^ {k})). \tag {dcgd}
$$

indeed, for  $n = 1$  this method reduces to cgd. for unbiased compressors belonging to  $\mathbb{u}(\zeta)$ , this method converges under suitable assumptions on the functions. for instance, if  $f_{i}$  are  $l$ -smooth and  $f$  is  $\mu$ -strongly convex, then as long as the stepsize is chosen appropriately,

the method converges to a  $\mathcal{o}\left(\frac{\eta d(\zeta - 1)}{\mu n}\right)$  neighborhood of the (necessarily unique) solution  $x^{\star}$  with the linear rate

$$
\mathcal {o} \left(\left(\frac {l}{\mu} + \frac {l (\zeta - 1)}{\mu n}\right) \log \frac {1}{\epsilon}\right),
$$

where  $d \coloneqq \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \| \nabla f_i(x^{\star}) \|_2^2$  (gorbunov et al., 2020a). in particular, in the overparameterized setting when  $d = 0$ , the method converges to the exact solution, and does so at the same rate as gd as long as  $\zeta = \mathcal{o}(n)$ . these results hold even if a regularizer is considered, and a proximal step is added to dcgd. moreover, as shown by mishchenko et al. (2019) and horváth et al. (2019b), a variance reduction technique can be devised to remove the neighborhood convergence and replace it by convergence to  $x^{\star}$ , at the negligible additional cost of  $\mathcal{o}((\zeta - 1) \log \frac{1}{\epsilon})$ .

# 5.2 failure of dcgd with biased compressors

however, as we now demonstrate by giving some counter-examples, dcgd may fail if the compression operators are allowed to be biased. in the first example below, dcgd used with the top-1 compressor diverges at an exponential rate.

example 1 consider  $n = d = 3$  and define

$$
f _ {1} (x) = \langle a, x \rangle^ {2} + \frac {1}{4} \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2}, \qquad f _ {2} (x) = \langle b, x \rangle^ {2} + \frac {1}{4} \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2}, \qquad f _ {3} (x) = \langle c, x \rangle^ {2} + \frac {1}{4} \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2},
$$

where  $a = (-3,2,2)$ ,  $b = (2, -3,2)$  and  $c = (2,2, -3)$ . let the starting iterate be  $x^0 = (t,t,t)$ , where  $t > 0$ . then

$$
\nabla f _ {1} (x ^ {0}) = \frac {t}{2} (- 1 1, 9, 9), \qquad \nabla f _ {2} (x ^ {0}) = \frac {t}{2} (9, - 1 1, 9), \qquad \nabla f _ {3} (x ^ {0}) = \frac {t}{2} (9, 9, - 1 1).
$$

using the top-1 compressor, we get  $\mathcal{c}(\nabla f_1(x^0)) = \frac{t}{2} (-11,0,0)$ ,  $\mathcal{c}(\nabla f_2(x^0)) = \frac{t}{2} (0, - 11,0)$  and  $\mathcal{c}(\nabla f_3(x^0)) = \frac{t}{2} (0,0, - 11)$ . the next iterate of dcgd is

$$
x ^ {1} = x ^ {0} - \eta \frac {1}{3} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {3} \mathcal {c} (\nabla f _ {i} (x ^ {0})) = \left(1 + \frac {1 1 \eta}{6}\right) x ^ {0}.
$$

repeated application gives

$$
x ^ {k} = \left(1 + \frac {1 1 \eta}{6}\right) ^ {k} x ^ {0}.
$$

since  $\eta > 0$ , the entries of  $x^k$  diverge exponentially fast to  $+\infty$ .

the above counter-example can be extended to the case of  $\operatorname{top} - d_1$  when  $d_1 < \left\lceil \frac{d}{2} \right\rceil$ .

example 2 fix the dimension  $d \geq 3$  and let  $n = \binom{d}{d_1}$  be the number of nodes, where  $d_1 < \left\lceil \frac{d}{2} \right\rceil$  and  $d_2 = d - d_1 > d_1$ . choose positive numbers  $b, c > 0$  such that

$$
- b d _ {1} + c d _ {2} = 1, b > c + 1.
$$

one possible choice could be  $b = d_{2} + \frac{d_{2}}{d_{1}}$ ,  $c = d_{1} + \frac{1}{d_{2}} + 1$ . define vectors  $a_{j} \in \mathbb{r}^{d}$ ,  $j \in [n]$  via

$$
a _ {j} = \sum_ {i \in i _ {j}} (- b) e _ {i} + \sum_ {i \in [ d ] \backslash i _ {j}} c e _ {i},
$$

where sets  $i_{j} \subset [d]$ ,  $j \in [n]$  are all possible  $d_{1}$ -subsets of  $[d]$  enumerated in some way. define

$$
f _ {j} (x) = \langle a _ {j}, x \rangle^ {2} + \frac {1}{2} \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2}, \quad j \in [ n ]
$$

and let the initial point be  $x^0 = te$ ,  $t > 0$ , where  $e = \sum_{i=1}^{d} e_i$  is the vector of all 1s. then

$$
\nabla f _ {j} (x ^ {0}) = 2 \langle a _ {j}, x ^ {0} \rangle \cdot a _ {j} + x ^ {0} = 2 t (- b d _ {1} + c d _ {2}) \cdot a _ {j} + t e = t (2 a _ {j} + e).
$$

since  $|2(-b) + 1| > |2c + 1|$ , then using the top- $d_1$  compressor, we get

$$
\mathcal {c} (\nabla f _ {j} (x ^ {0})) = - t (2 b - 1) \sum_ {i \in i _ {j}} e _ {i}.
$$

therefore, the next iterate of dcgd is

$$
\begin{array}{l} x ^ {1} = x ^ {0} - \eta \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} \mathcal {c} (\nabla f _ {j} (x ^ {0})) = x ^ {0} + \frac {\eta t (2 b - 1)}{n} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} \sum_ {i \in i _ {j}} e _ {i} \\ = x ^ {0} + \frac {\eta (2 b - 1)}{n} \binom {d} {d _ {1} - 1} x ^ {0} = \left(1 + \frac {\eta (2 b - 1) d _ {1}}{d _ {2} + 1}\right) x ^ {0}, \\ \end{array}
$$

which implies

$$
x ^ {k} = \left(1 + \frac {\eta (2 b - 1) d _ {1}}{d _ {2} + 1}\right) ^ {k} x ^ {0}.
$$

since  $\eta > 0$  and  $b > 1$ , the entries of  $x^k$  diverge exponentially fast to  $+\infty$ .

finally, we present more general counter-example with different type of failure for dcgd when non-randomized compressors are used.

example 3 let  $\mathcal{c} \colon \mathbb{r}^d \to \mathbb{r}^d$  be any deterministic mapping for which there exist vectors  $v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_m \in \mathbb{r}^d$  such that

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {m} v _ {i} \neq 0, \quad a n d \quad \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {m} \mathcal {c} (v _ {i}) = 0. \tag {18}
$$

consider the distributed optimization problem (17) with  $n = m$  devices and with the following strongly convex loss functions

$$
f _ {i} (x) = \langle v _ {i}, x \rangle + \frac {1}{2} \| x \| ^ {2}, \quad i \in [ m ].
$$

then  $\nabla f_{i}(x) = v_{i} + x$  and  $\nabla f(x) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i} + x$ . hence, the optimal point  $x^{*} = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i} \neq 0$ . however, it can be easily checked that, with initialization  $x_{0} = 0$ , we have

$$
x ^ {1} = x ^ {0} - \gamma \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \mathcal {c} (\nabla f _ {i} (x ^ {0})) = x ^ {0} - \gamma \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \mathcal {c} (v _ {i} + x ^ {0}) = x ^ {0}.
$$

thus, when initialized at  $x_0 = 0$ , not only dcgd does not converge to the solution  $x^* \neq 0$ , it remains stuck at the same initial point for all iterates, namely  $x^k = x^0 = 0$  for all  $k \geq 1$ .

condition (18) can be easily satisfied for specific biased compressors. for instance, top-1 satisfies (18) with  $v_{1} = \left[\frac{1}{4}\right]$ ,  $v_{2} = \left[\frac{-1}{-2}\right]$ ,  $v_{3} = \left[\frac{1}{-2}\right]$ .

the above examples suggest that one needs to devise a different approach to solving the distributed problem (17) with biased compressors. we resolve this problem by employing a memory feedback mechanism.

# 5.3 error feedback

we show that distributed version of distributed sgd with error-feedback (karimireddy et al., 2019b), displayed in algorithm 1, is able to resolve the issue. moreover, this algorithm allows for the computation of stochastic gradients. each step starts with all machines  $i$  in parallel computing a stochastic gradient  $g_{i}^{k}$  of the form

$$
g _ {i} ^ {k} = \nabla f _ {i} \left(x ^ {k}\right) + \xi_ {i} ^ {k}, \tag {19}
$$

where  $\nabla f_{i}(x^{k})$  is the true gradient, and  $\xi_i^k$  is a stochastic error. then, this is multiplied by a stepsize  $\eta^k$  and added to the memory/feedback term  $e_i^k$ , and subsequently compressed. the compressed messages are communicated and aggregated. the difference of message we wanted to send and its compressed version becomes stored as  $e_i^{k + 1}$  for further correction in the next communication round. the output  $\overline{x}^{k}$  is an ergodic average of the form

$$
\bar {x} ^ {k} := \frac {1}{w ^ {k}} \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {k} w ^ {k} x ^ {k}, \quad w ^ {k} := \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {k} w ^ {k}. \tag {20}
$$

# 5.4 complexity theory

we assume the stochastic error  $\xi_{i}^{k}$  in (19) satisfies the following condition.

assumption 1 stochastic error  $\xi_{i}^{k}$  is unbiased, i.e.  $\operatorname{e}\left[\xi_{i}^{k}\right] = 0$ , and for some constants  $b, c \geq 0$

$$
\left. \operatorname {e} \left[ \left\| \xi_ {i} ^ {k} \right\| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] \leq b \left\| \nabla f _ {i} \left(x ^ {k}\right) \right\| _ {2} ^ {2} + c, \quad \forall i \in [ n ], k \geq 0. \right. \tag {24}
$$

note that this assumption is much weaker than the bounded variance assumption (i.e.,  $\operatorname{e}\left[\| \xi_i^k\| _2^2\right]\leq c)$  and bounded gradient assumption (i.e.,  $\operatorname{e}\left[\| g_i^k\| _2^2\right]\leq c)$ . we can now state the main result of this section. to the best of our knowledge, this was an open problem: we are not aware of any convergence results for distributed optimization that tolerate general classes of biased compression operators and have reasonable assumptions on the stochastic gradient.

# algorithm 1 distributed sgd with biased compression and error feedback

parameters: compressors  $\mathcal{c}_i^k\in \mathbb{b}^3 (\delta)$ ; stepsizes  $\{\eta^k\}_{k\geq 0}$ ; iteration count  $k$

initialization: choose  $x^0\in \mathbb{r}^d$  and  $e_i^0 = 0$  for all  $i$

for  $k = 0,1,2,\ldots ,k$  do

server sends  $x^{k}$  to all  $n$  machines

all machines in parallel perform these updates:

$$
\tilde {g} _ {i} ^ {k} = \mathcal {c} _ {i} ^ {k} \left(e _ {i} ^ {k} + \eta^ {k} g _ {i} ^ {k}\right) \tag {21}
$$

$$
e _ {i} ^ {k + 1} = e _ {i} ^ {k} + \eta^ {k} g _ {i} ^ {k} - \tilde {g} _ {i} ^ {k} \tag {22}
$$

each machine  $i$  sends  $\tilde{g}_i^k$  to the server

server performs aggregation:

$$
x ^ {k + 1} = x ^ {k} - \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \tilde {g} _ {i} ^ {k} \tag {23}
$$

end for

output: weighted average of the iterates:  $\overline{x}^k$  (20)

theorem 21 (convergence guarantees for algorithm 1) let  $\{x^k\}_{k\geq 0}$  denote the iterates of algorithm 1 for solving problem (1), where each  $f_{i}$  is  $l$ -smooth and  $\mu$ -strongly convex. let  $x^{\star}$  be the minimizer of  $f$  and let  $f^{\star} := f(x^{\star})$  and

$$
d := \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \| \nabla f _ {i} (x ^ {\star}) \| _ {2} ^ {2}.
$$

assume the compression operator used by all nodes is in  $\mathbb{b}^3 (\delta)$ . then we have the following convergence rates under three different stepsize and iterate weighting regimes:

(i)  $\mathcal{o}\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)$  stepsizes &  $\mathcal{o}(k)$  weights. let, for all  $k\geq 0$  , the stepsizes and weights be set as  $\eta^k = \frac{4}{\mu(\kappa + k)}$  and  $w^{k} = \kappa +k$  , respectively, where  $\kappa = \frac{56(2\delta + b)l}{\mu}$  . then

$$
\boxed {\operatorname {e} \left[ f (\bar {x} ^ {k}) \right] - f ^ {\star} = \mathcal {o} \left(\frac {a _ {1}}{k ^ {2}} + \frac {a _ {2}}{k}\right),}
$$

where  $a_{1} \coloneqq \frac{l^{2}(2\delta + b)^{2}}{\mu}\left\| x^{0} - x^{\star}\right\|_{2}^{2}$  and  $a_{2} \coloneqq \frac{c(1 + 1 / n) + d(2b / n + 3\delta)}{\mu}$ .

(ii)  $\mathcal{o}(1)$  stepsizes &  $\mathcal{o}(e^{-k})$  weights. let, for all  $k\geq 0$ , the stepsizes and weights be set as  $\eta^k = \eta$  and  $w^{k} = (1 - \mu \eta /2)^{-(k + 1)}$ , respectively, where  $\eta \leq \frac{1}{14(2\delta + b)l}$ . then

$$
\boxed {\operatorname {e} \left[ f (\bar {x} ^ {k}) \right] - f ^ {\star} = \tilde {\mathcal {o}} \left(a _ {3} \exp \left[ - \frac {k}{a _ {4}} \right] + \frac {a _ {2}}{k}\right),}
$$

where  $a_3 \coloneqq l(2\delta + b) \left\| x^0 - x^\star \right\|_2^2$  and  $a_4 \coloneqq \frac{28l(2\delta + b)}{\mu}$ .

(iii)  $\mathcal{o}(1)$  stepsizes & equal weights. let, for all  $k \geq 0$ , the stepsizes and weights be set as  $\eta^k = \eta$  and  $w^k = 1$ , respectively, where  $\eta \leq \frac{1}{14(2\delta + b)l}$ . then

$$
\boxed {\operatorname {e} \left[ f (\bar {x} ^ {k}) \right] - f ^ {\star} = \mathcal {o} \left(\frac {a _ {3}}{k} + \frac {a _ {5}}{\sqrt {k}}\right),}
$$

where  $a_{5} \coloneqq \sqrt{c(1 + 1 / n) + d(2b / n + 3\delta)}\left\|x^{0} - x^{\star}\right\|_{2}$ .

let us make a few observations on these results. first, algorithm 1 employing general biased compressors and error feedback mechanism indeed resolves convergence issues of dcgd method by converging the optimal solution  $x^{*}$ . second, note that the choice of stepsizes  $\eta^k$  and weights  $w^k$  leading to convergence is not unique and several schedules are feasible. third, all the rates are sublinear and based on the second rate (ii) above, linear convergence is guaranteed if  $c = d = 0$ . based on (24), one setup when the condition  $c = 0$  holds is when all devices compute full local gradients (i.e.,  $g_{i}^{k} = \nabla f_{i}(x^{k})$ ). furthermore, the condition  $d = 0$  is equivalent to  $\nabla f_{i}(x^{\star}) = 0$  for all  $i\in [n]$ , which is typically satisfied for over-parameterized models. lastly, under these two assumptions (i.e., devices can compute full local gradients and the model is over-parameterized), we show that distributed sgd method with error feedback converges with the same  $\mathcal{o}\left(\delta \frac{l}{\mu}\log \frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)$  linear rate as single node cgd algorithm. to the best of our knowledge, this was the first regime where distributed first order method with biased compression is guaranteed to converge linearly.

# 6.4 theoretical behavior predicts the actual performance in practice

in the next experiment, we provide numerical results to further show that our predicted theoretical behavior matches the actual performance observed in practice. we run two regression experiments optimized by gradient descent with step-size  $\eta = \frac{1}{l}$ . we use a slightly

$$
\frac {f (x ^ {k}) - f (x ^ {\star})}{f (x ^ {0}) - f (x ^ {\star})} \leq \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {k} \left(1 - \frac {\mu}{l \delta_ {i}}\right),

$$
1 - \frac {1}{\delta_ {i}} = \frac {\left\| \mathcal {c} (\nabla f (x ^ {i})) - \nabla f (x ^ {i}) \right\| _ {2} ^ {2}}{\| \nabla f (x ^ {i}) \| _ {2} ^ {2}}.

$$

# appendix

# appendix a. basic facts and inequalities

# a.1 strong convexity

function  $f$  is strongly convex on  $\mathbb{r}^d$  when it is continuously differentiable and there is a constant  $\mu > 0$  such that the following inequality holds:

$$
\frac {\mu}{2} \| x - y \| _ {2} ^ {2} \leq f (x) - f (y) - \langle \nabla f (y), x - y \rangle , \quad \forall x, y \in \mathbb {r} ^ {d}. \tag {25}
$$

# a.2 smoothness

function  $f$  is called  $l$ -smooth in  $\mathbb{r}^d$  with  $l > 0$  when it is differentiable and its gradient is  $l$ -lipschitz continuous, i.e.

$$
\| \nabla f (x) - \nabla f (y) \| _ {2} \leq l \| x - y \| _ {2}, \quad \forall x, y \in \mathbb {r} ^ {d}.
$$

if convexity is assumed as well, then the following inequalities hold:

$$
\frac {1}{2 l} \| \nabla f (x) - \nabla f (y) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \leq f (x) - f (y) - \langle \nabla f (y), x - y \rangle , \quad \forall x, y \in \mathbb {r} ^ {d} \tag {26}
$$

by plugging  $y = x^{*}$  to (26), we get

$$
\left\| \nabla f (x) \right\| _ {2} ^ {2} \leq 2 l \left(f (x) - f \left(x ^ {*}\right)\right), \quad \forall x \in \mathbb {r} ^ {d}. \tag {27}
$$

# a.3 useful inequalities

for all  $a, b, x_1, \ldots, x_n \in \mathbb{r}^d$  and  $\xi > 0$  the following inequalities hold:

$$
2 \langle a, b \rangle \leq \frac {\left\| a \right\| _ {2} ^ {2}}{\xi} + \xi \| b \| _ {2} ^ {2}, \tag {28}
$$

$$
\left\| a + b \right\| _ {2} ^ {2} \leq \left(1 + \frac {1}{\xi}\right) \left\| a \right\| _ {2} ^ {2} + (1 + \xi) \| b \| _ {2} ^ {2}, \tag {29}
$$

$$
\left\| \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} x _ {i} \right\| _ {2} ^ {2} \leq n \cdot \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \| x _ {i} \| _ {2} ^ {2}. \tag {30}
$$

# a.4 facts from order statistics

for i.i.d. samples  $x_{1}, x_{2}, \ldots, x_{d}$  from an absolutely continuous distribution with probability density function  $\phi$  and cumulative distribution function  $\phi$  let  $x_{(1)} \leq x_{(2)} \leq \dots \leq x_{(d)}$  be the order statistics obtained by arranging samples in increasing order of magnitude. then the following expressions give the density function of  $x_{(i)}$  ( $1 \leq i \leq d$ )

$$
\phi_ {x _ {(i)}} (u) = \frac {d !}{(i - 1) ! (d - i) !} \left\{\phi (u) \right\} ^ {i - 1} \left\{1 - \phi (u) \right\} ^ {n - i} \phi (u), \quad - \infty <   u <   \infty , \tag {31}
$$

and the joint density function of all  $d$  order statistics

$$
\phi_ {x _ {(1)}, \dots , x _ {(d)}} \left(u _ {1}, \dots , u _ {d}\right) = d! \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {d} \phi \left(u _ {i}\right), \quad - \infty <   u _ {1} \leq \dots \leq u _ {d} <   \infty . \tag {32}
$$

# appendix b. proofs for section 2.2

# b.1 proof of lemma 8: unbiased random sparsification

from the definition of  $k$ -nice sampling we have  $p_i \coloneqq \operatorname{prob}(i \in s) = \frac{k}{d}$ . hence

$$
\operatorname {e} \left[ \mathcal {c} (x) \right] = \frac {d}{k} \operatorname {e} \left[ \sum_ {i \in s} x _ {i} e _ {i} \right] = \frac {d}{k} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {d} p _ {i} x _ {i} e _ {i} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {d} x _ {i} e _ {i} = x,
$$

$$
\operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] = \frac {d ^ {2}}{k ^ {2}} \operatorname {e} \left[ \sum_ {i \in s} x _ {i} ^ {2} \right] = \frac {d ^ {2}}{k ^ {2}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {d} p _ {i} x _ {i} ^ {2} = \frac {d}{k} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {d} x _ {i} ^ {2} = \frac {d}{k} \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2},
$$

which implies  $\mathcal{c}\in \mathbb{u}(\frac{d}{k})$

# b.2 proof of lemma 9: biased random sparsification

let  $s \subseteq [d]$  be a proper sampling with probability vector  $p = (p_1, \ldots, p_d)$ , where  $p_i \coloneqq \operatorname{prob}(i \in s) > 0$  for all  $i$ . then

$$
\operatorname {e} \left[ \mathcal {c} (x) \right] = \operatorname {d i a g} (p) x = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {d} p _ {i} x _ {i} e _ {i} \quad \text {a n d} \quad \operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {d} p _ {i} x _ {i} ^ {2}.
$$

letting  $q \coloneqq \min_{i} p_{i}$ , we get

$$
q \left\| x \right\| _ {2} ^ {2} \leq \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {d} p _ {i} x _ {i} ^ {2} = \operatorname {e} \left[ \left\| \mathcal {c} (x) \right\| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] = \left\langle \operatorname {e} \left[ \mathcal {c} (x) \right], x \right\rangle .
$$

so,  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^1(q, 1)$  and  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^2(q, 1)$ . for the third class, note that

$$
\operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) - x \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {d} (1 - p _ {i}) x _ {i} ^ {2} \leq (1 - q) \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2}.
$$

hence,  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^3\left(\frac{1}{q}\right)$ .

# b.3 proof of lemma 10: adaptive random sparsification

from the definition of the compression operator, we have

$$
\operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] = \operatorname {e} \left[ x _ {i} ^ {2} \right] = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {d} \frac {| x _ {i} |}{\| x \| _ {1}} x _ {i} ^ {2} = \frac {\| x \| _ {3} ^ {3}}{\| x \| _ {1}},
$$

$$
\operatorname {e} \left[ \langle \mathcal {c} (x), x \rangle \right] = \operatorname {e} \left[ x _ {i} ^ {2} \right] = \frac {\| x \| _ {3} ^ {3}}{\| x \| _ {1}},
$$

whence  $\beta = 1$ . furthermore, by chebychev's sum inequality, we have

$$
\frac {1}{d ^ {2}} \left\| x \right\| _ {1} \left\| x \right\| _ {2} ^ {2} = \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {d} \frac {1}{d} | x _ {i} |\right) \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {d} \frac {1}{d} x _ {i} ^ {2}\right) \leq \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {d} \frac {1}{d} | x _ {i} | x _ {i} ^ {2} = \frac {1}{d} \left\| x \right\| _ {3} ^ {3},
$$

which implies that  $\alpha = \frac{1}{d}$ ,  $\delta = d$ . so,  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^1(\frac{1}{d}, 1)$ ,  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^2(\frac{1}{d}, 1)$ , and  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^3(d)$ .

# b.4 proof of lemma 11: top- $k$  sparsification

clearly,  $\| \mathcal{c}(x)\| _2^2 = \sum_{i = d - k + 1}^d x_{(i)}^2$  and  $\| \mathcal{c}(x) - x\| _2^2 = \sum_{i = 1}^{d - k}x_{(i)}^2$ . hence

$$
\frac {k}{d} \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} \leq \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} = \langle \mathcal {c} (x), x \rangle \leq \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2}, \quad \| \mathcal {c} (x) - x \| _ {2} ^ {2} \leq \left(1 - \frac {k}{d}\right) \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2}.
$$

so,  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^1\left(\frac{k}{d}, 1\right)$ ,  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^2\left(\frac{k}{d}, 1\right)$ , and  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^3\left(\frac{d}{k}\right)$ .

# b.5 proof of lemma 12: general unbiased rounding

the unbiasedness follows immediately from the definition (12)

$$
\operatorname {e} \left[ \mathcal {c} (x) \right] = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {d} \operatorname {e} \left[ \mathcal {c} (x) _ {i} \right] e _ {i} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {d} \operatorname {s i g n} \left(x _ {i}\right) \left(a _ {k} \frac {a _ {k + 1} - \left| x _ {i} \right|}{a _ {k + 1} - a _ {k}} + a _ {k + 1} \frac {\left| x _ {i} \right| - a _ {k}}{a _ {k + 1} - a _ {k}}\right) e _ {i} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {d} x _ {i} e _ {i} = x \tag {33}
$$

since the rounding compression operator  $\mathcal{c}$  applies to each coordinate independently, without loss of generality we can consider the compression of scalar values  $x = t > 0$  and show that  $\operatorname{e}\left[\mathcal{c}(t)^2\right] \leq \zeta \cdot t^2$ . from the definition we compute the second moment as follows

$$
\operatorname {e} \left[ \mathcal {c} (t) ^ {2} \right] = a _ {k} ^ {2} \frac {a _ {k + 1} - t}{a _ {k + 1} - a _ {k}} + a _ {k + 1} ^ {2} \frac {t - a _ {k}}{a _ {k + 1} - a _ {k}} = \left(a _ {k} + a _ {k + 1}\right) t - a _ {k} a _ {k + 1} = t ^ {2} + (t - a _ {k}) \left(a _ {k + 1} - t\right), \tag {34}
$$

from which

$$
\frac {\operatorname {e} \left[ \mathcal {c} (t) ^ {2} \right]}{t ^ {2}} = 1 + \left(1 - \frac {a _ {k}}{t}\right) \left(\frac {a _ {k + 1}}{t} - 1\right), \quad a _ {k} \leq t \leq a _ {k + 1}. \tag {35}
$$

checking the optimality condition, one can show that the maximum is achieved at

$$
t _ {*} = \frac {2 a _ {k} a _ {k + 1}}{a _ {k} + a _ {k + 1}} = \frac {2}{\frac {1}{a _ {k}} + \frac {1}{a _ {k + 1}}},
$$

which being the harmonic mean of  $a_{k}$  and  $a_{k + 1}$ , is in the range  $[a_k, a_{k + 1}]$ . plugging it to the expression for variance we get

$$
\frac {\mathrm {e} \left[ \mathcal {c} (t _ {*}) ^ {2} \right]}{t _ {*} ^ {2}} = 1 + \frac {1}{4} \left(1 - \frac {a _ {k}}{a _ {k + 1}}\right) \left(\frac {a _ {k + 1}}{a _ {k}} - 1\right) = \frac {1}{4} \left(\frac {a _ {k}}{a _ {k + 1}} + \frac {a _ {k + 1}}{a _ {k}} + 2\right).
$$

thus, the parameter  $\zeta$  for general unbiased rounding would be

$$
\zeta = \sup _ {t > 0} \frac {\operatorname {e} \left[ \mathcal {c} (t) ^ {2} \right]}{t ^ {2}} = \sup _ {k \in \mathbb {z}} \sup _ {a _ {k} \leq t \leq a _ {k + 1}} \frac {\operatorname {e} \left[ \mathcal {c} (t) ^ {2} \right]}{t ^ {2}} = \frac {1}{4} \sup _ {k \in \mathbb {z}} \left(\frac {a _ {k}}{a _ {k + 1}} + \frac {a _ {k + 1}}{a _ {k}} + 2\right) \geq 1.
$$

# b.6 proof of lemma 13: general biased rounding

from the definition (13) of compression operator  $\mathcal{c}$  we derive the following inequalities

$$
\inf _ {k \in \mathbb {z}} \left(\frac {2 a _ {k}}{a _ {k} + a _ {k + 1}}\right) ^ {2} \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} \leq \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2},
$$

$$
\| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \leq \sup _ {k \in \mathbb {z}} \frac {2 a _ {k + 1}}{a _ {k} + a _ {k + 1}} \langle \mathcal {c} (x), x \rangle ,
$$

$$
\inf _ {k \in \mathbb {z}} \frac {2 a _ {k}}{a _ {k} + a _ {k + 1}} \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} \leq \langle \mathcal {c} (x), x \rangle ,
$$

which imply that  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^1(\alpha, \beta)$  and  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^2(\gamma, \beta)$ , with

$$
\beta = \sup _ {k \in \mathbb {z}} \frac {2 a _ {k + 1}}{a _ {k} + a _ {k + 1}}, \qquad \gamma = \inf _ {k \in \mathbb {z}} \frac {2 a _ {k}}{a _ {k} + a _ {k + 1}}, \qquad \alpha = \gamma^ {2}.
$$

for the third class  $\mathbb{b}^3 (\delta)$ , we need to upper bound the ratio  $\| \mathcal{c}(x) - x\| _2^2 /\| x\| _2^2$ . again, as  $\mathcal{c}$  applies to each coordinate independently, without loss of generality we consider the case when  $x = t > 0$  is a scalar. from definition (13), we get

$$
\frac {(\mathcal {c} (t) - t) ^ {2}}{t ^ {2}} = \min  \left[ \left(1 - \frac {a _ {k}}{t}\right) ^ {2}, \left(1 - \frac {a _ {k + 1}}{t}\right) ^ {2} \right], \quad a _ {k} \leq t \leq a _ {k + 1}. \tag {36}
$$

it can be easily checked that  $\left(1 - \frac{a_k}{t}\right)^2$  is an increasing function and  $\left(1 - \frac{a_{k + 1}}{t}\right)^2$  is a decreasing function of  $t\in [a_k,a_{k + 1}]$ . thus, the maximum is achieved when they are equal. in contrast to unbiased general rounding, it happens at the middle of the interval,

$$
t _ {*} = \frac {a _ {k} + a _ {k + 1}}{2} \in [ a _ {k}, a _ {k + 1} ].
$$

plugging  $t_*$  into (36), we get

$$
\frac {(\mathcal {c} (t _ {*}) - t _ {*}) ^ {2}}{t _ {*} ^ {2}} = \left(\frac {a _ {k + 1} - a _ {k}}{a _ {k + 1} + a _ {k}}\right) ^ {2}.
$$

given this, the parameter  $\delta$  can be computed from

$$
1 - \frac {1}{\delta} = \sup _ {k \in \mathbb {z}} \sup _ {a _ {k} \leq t \leq a _ {k + 1}} \frac {(\mathcal {c} (t) - t) ^ {2}}{t ^ {2}} = \sup _ {k \in \mathbb {z}} \left(\frac {a _ {k + 1} - a _ {k}}{a _ {k + 1} + a _ {k}}\right) ^ {2},
$$

which gives

$$
\delta = \sup _ {k \in \mathbb {z}} \frac {\left(a _ {k} + a _ {k + 1}\right) ^ {2}}{4 a _ {k} a _ {k + 1}} \geq 1,
$$

and  $\mathcal{c}\in \mathbb{b}^3 (\delta)$

# b.7 proof of lemma 15: general exponential dithering

the proof goes with the same steps as in theorem 4 of horváth et al. (2019a). to show the unbiasedness of  $\mathcal{c}$ , first we show the unbiasedness of  $\xi(t)$  for  $t \in [0,1]$  in the same way as (33) was done. then we note that

$$
\operatorname {e} \left[ \mathcal {c} (x) \right] = \operatorname {s i g n} (x) \times \| x \| _ {p} \times \operatorname {e} \left[ \xi \left(\frac {| x |}{\| x \| _ {p}}\right) \right] = \operatorname {s i g n} (x) \times \| x \| _ {p} \times \left(\frac {| x |}{\| x \| _ {p}}\right) = x.
$$

to compute the parameter  $\zeta$ , we first estimate the second moment of  $\xi$  as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \leq \mathbb {1} \left(\frac {| x _ {i} |}{\| x \| _ {p}} \geq b ^ {1 - s}\right) \cdot \frac {1}{4} \left(b + \frac {1}{b} + 2\right) \frac {x _ {i} ^ {2}}{\| x \| _ {p} ^ {2}} + \mathbb {1} \left(\frac {| x _ {i} |}{\| x \| _ {p}} <   b ^ {1 - s}\right) \cdot \frac {| x _ {i} |}{\| x \| _ {p}} b ^ {1 - s} \\ \leq \frac {1}{4} \left(b + \frac {1}{b} + 2\right) \frac {x _ {i} ^ {2}}{\| x \| _ {p} ^ {2}} + \mathbb {1} \left(\frac {| x _ {i} |}{\| x \| _ {p}} <   b ^ {1 - s}\right) \cdot \frac {| x _ {i} |}{\| x \| _ {p}} b ^ {1 - s}. \\ \end{array}
$$

then we use this bound to estimate the second moment of compressor  $\mathcal{c}$ :

$$
\begin{array}{l} \operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] = \| x \| _ {p} ^ {2} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {d} \operatorname {e} \left[ \xi \left(\frac {| x _ {i} |}{\| x \| _ {p}}\right) ^ {2} \right] \\ \leq \| x \| _ {p} ^ {2} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {d} \left(\frac {1}{4} \left(b + \frac {1}{b} + 2\right) \frac {x _ {i} ^ {2}}{\| x \| _ {p} ^ {2}} + \mathbb {1} \left(\frac {| x _ {i} |}{\| x \| _ {p}} <   b ^ {1 - s}\right) \cdot \frac {| x _ {i} |}{\| x \| _ {p}} b ^ {1 - s}\right) \\ = \frac {1}{4} \left(b + \frac {1}{b} + 2\right) \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {d} \mathbb {1} \left(\frac {| x _ {i} |}{\| x \| _ {p}} <   b ^ {1 - s}\right) \cdot | x _ {i} | \| x \| _ {p} b ^ {1 - s} \\ \leq \frac {1}{4} \left(b + \frac {1}{b} + 2\right) \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} + \min  \left(\| x \| _ {1} \| x \| _ {p} b ^ {1 - s}, d \| x \| _ {p} ^ {2} b ^ {2 - 2 s}\right) \\ \leq \frac {1}{4} \left(b + \frac {1}{b} + 2\right) \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} + \min  \left(d ^ {1 / 2} \| x \| _ {2} \| x \| _ {p} b ^ {1 - s}, d \| x \| _ {p} ^ {2} b ^ {2 - 2 s}\right) \\ \leq \left[ \frac {1}{4} \left(b + \frac {1}{b} + 2\right) + d ^ {1 / r} b ^ {1 - s} \min  \left(1, d ^ {1 / r} b ^ {1 - s}\right) \right] \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2} \\ = \zeta_ {b} \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2}, \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $r = \min(p, 2)$  and hölder's inequality is used to bound  $\| x \|_p \leq d^{1/p - 1/2} \| x \|_2$  in case of  $0 \leq p \leq 2$  and  $\| x \|_p \leq \| x \|_2$  in the case  $p \geq 2$ .

# b.8 proof of lemma 16: top- $k$  combined with exponential dithering

from the unbiasedness of general dithering operator  $\mathcal{c}_{dith}$  we have

$$
\operatorname {e} \left[ \mathcal {c} (x) \right] = \operatorname {e} \left[ \mathcal {c} _ {d i t h} (\mathcal {c} _ {t o p} (x)) \right] = \mathcal {c} _ {t o p} (x),
$$

from which we conclude  $\langle \operatorname{e}[\mathcal{c}(x)], x \rangle = \langle \mathcal{c}_{top}(x), x \rangle = \| \mathcal{c}_{top}(x) \|_2^2$ . next, using lemma 15 on exponential dithering we get

$$
\operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] \leq \zeta_ {b} \cdot \| \mathcal {c} _ {t o p} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} = \zeta_ {b} \cdot \langle \operatorname {e} \left[ \mathcal {c} (x) \right], x \rangle ,
$$

which implies  $\beta = \zeta_{b}$ . using lemma 11 we show  $\gamma = \frac{k}{d}$  as  $\langle \operatorname{e}[\mathcal{c}(x)], x \rangle = \| \mathcal{c}_{top}(x) \|_2^2 \geq \frac{k}{d} \| x \|_2^2$ . utilizing the derivations (34) and (35) it can be shown that  $\operatorname{e} \left[ \| \mathcal{c}_{dith}(x) \|_2^2 \right] \geq \| x \|_2^2$  and therefore

$$
\operatorname {e} \left[ \| \mathcal {c} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] \geq \| \mathcal {c} _ {t o p} (x) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \geq \frac {k}{d} \| x \| _ {2} ^ {2}.
$$

hence,  $\alpha = \frac{k}{d}$ . to compute the parameter  $\delta$  we use theorem 6, which yields  $\delta = \frac{\beta}{\gamma} = \frac{d}{k}\zeta_{b}$ .

# appendix c. proofs for section 3

we now perform analysis of cgd for compression operators in  $\mathbb{b}^1$ ,  $\mathbb{b}^2$  and  $\mathbb{b}^3$ , establishing theorems 17, 18 and 19, respectively.

# c.1 analysis for  $\mathcal{c} \in \mathbb{b}^1(\alpha, \beta)$
