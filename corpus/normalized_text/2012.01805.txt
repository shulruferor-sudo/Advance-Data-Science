in this review, we examine the problem of designing interpretable and explainable machine learning models. interpretability and explainability lie at the core of many machine learning and statistical applications in medicine, economics, law, and natural sciences. although interpretability and explainability have escaped a clear universal definition, many techniques motivated by these properties have been developed over the recent 30 years with the focus currently shifting towards deep learning methods. in this review, we emphasise the divide between interpretability and explainability and illustrate these two different research directions with concrete examples of the state-of-the-art. the review is intended for a general machine learning audience with interest in exploring the problems of interpretation and explanation beyond logistic regression or random forest variable importance. this work is not an exhaustive literature survey, but rather a primer focusing selectively on certain lines of research which the authors found interesting or informative.

# 1 interpretability, explainability, and intelligibility

interpretable and explainable ml techniques emerge from a need to design intelligible machine learning systems, i.e. ones that can be comprehended by a human mind, and to understand and explain predictions made by opaque models, such as deep neural networks [1] or gradient boosting machines [2, 3]. the early research on interpretable machine learning dates back to the 1990s [4] and often does not refer to terms like "interpretability" or "explainability", not to mention that many classical statistical models can be deemed interpretable.

in general, there is no agreement within the ml community on the definition of interpretability and the task of interpretation [5, 6]. for example, doshi-velez and kim define interpretability of ml systems as "the ability to explain or to present in understandable terms to a human" [5]. this definition clearly lacks mathematical rigour [6]. nevertheless, the notion of interpretability often depends on the domain of application [4] and the target explainee [7], i.e. the recipient of interpretations and explanations, therefore, an all-purpose definition might be infeasible [4] or unnecessary. other terms that are synonymous to interpretability and also appear in the ml literature are "intelligibility" [8, 9] and "understandability" [6]. these concepts are often used interchangeably.

yet another term prevalent in the literature is "explainability", giving rise to the direction of explainable artificial intelligence (xai) [10]. this concept is closely tied with interpretability; and many authors do not differentiate between the two [7]. doshi-velez and kim [5] provide a definition of explanation that originates from psychology: "explanations are... the currency in which we exchange beliefs". in [4], rudin draws a clear line between interpretable and explainable ml: interpretable ml focuses on designing models that are inherently interpretable; whereas explainable ml tries to provide post hoc explanations for existing black box models, i.e. models that are incomprehensible to humans or are proprietary [4]. for the sake of convenience, in this review, we adhere to the distinction proposed by rudin [4]. in [6], lipton stresses the difference in questions the two families of techniques try to address: interpretability raises the question "how does the model work?"; whereas explanation methods try to answer "what else can the model tell me?"

since there is no general definition of either interpretability or explainability, researchers have elicited various desiderata, diverse and often contradicting, to provide motivation for different techniques. below we summarise, based on [5], [6], and [7], a few of the goals that could be achieved with interpretability:

- trust is often mentioned in association with interpretability [5,6]. interpretability can be a prerequisite for a trustable ml system. lipton [6] decomposes trust into knowing "how often a model is right" and "for which examples it is right".  
- causality might be desirable when we want to leverage an interpretable model or an explanation method to generate hypotheses about causal relationships between variables [6]. therefore, it is often desirable for the technique to pick up causal relationships [7], rather than mere associations. such formulation of interpretability usually requires solving the problem of the observational causal discovery [11].  
- reliability / robustness / transferability [5-7] posit that ml systems should be resistant to noisy inputs and (reasonable) domain shifts. an example wherein interpretability facilitates domain adaptation is given by caruana et al. in [9] who use additive models for pneumonia risk prediction, exhibit and alleviate unwanted confounding in the dataset. in general, this direction of research is strongly related to the problems of domain adaptation and transfer learning [12].  
- fairness is important when ml algorithms are incorporated into decision-making, e.g. social, economical or medical. interpretations and explanations can be instrumental in exposing demographic and other biases in algorithms and datasets [6, 7]. needless to mention, this is not a straightforward task, especially, when an explanation method is not faithful w.r.t. the original predictive model [4], i.e. does not really reflect inner workings of the model.  
- privacy [5, 7] can be of concern in systems relying on sensitive personal data. similarly to fairness, interpretations and explanations can help to understand if user privacy is preserved.

in the remainder of this review, we briefly discuss a need for interpretable and explainable machine learning techniques giving examples from several application areas (see section 2). we provide an overview of evaluation methods of interpretability and explainability (see section 3). the main body of this paper (see section 4) outlines a partial taxonomy of techniques for interpretable (see section 4.1) and explainable (see section 4.2) ml with examples of several recent advancements. summary of reviewed methods is provided in tables 1 and 2. we conclude with a brief summary in section 5.

# 2 relevance

it is natural to question usefulness of interpretable and explainable ml, especially, given a widespread belief that there exists a trade-off between accuracy and interpretability [4]. why would a designer of an ml system consider sacrificing performance for the sake of transparency? first, it is important to note that there are many cases when interpretability is not necessary at all, in particular, when the studied problem is well-known and does not have strong consequences [5], e.g. mail sorting, movie recommendation etc. second, the perceived accuracy-interpretability trade-off may not necessarily exist in many datasets [4]. doshi-velez and kim [5] claim that a natural need for interpretability arises when a formalisation of the problem is incomplete, thus, making direct optimisation and validation impossible. they provide a few examples of such situations [5], we extend their list with references to some concrete instances:

- scientific understanding: we often want to extract (scientific) knowledge from the data, and interpretations and explanations might be the best way to achieve this [5]. interpretability can be instrumental in exploratory data analysis and discovery. this application of ml is only emerging. for example, interpretable support vector machines have been used for discovering new phases of kitaev materials [13]; in quantum chemistry, neural networks have been applied to predict the quantum mechanical wavefunction [14], allowing for its analytical differentiable representation. in computational linguistics, pimentel et al. [15] have leveraged nlp models alongside with an information-theoretic approach to quantify the relationship between form and meaning of words and test the long-standing saussure's hypothesis. these are just few examples of machine-learning-assisted scientific discovery, a comprehensive survey by raghu and schmidt [16] contains a plethora of scientific deep learning applications.

- safety: interpretability can assist developers in understanding when an ml system can or will fail [5]. we have already mentioned the pneumonia prediction example from caruana et al. [9], where interpretability allowed revealing confounding between a predictor variable and the response. another noteworthy example is the manifold - an in-house visualisation and debugging system for ml models developed at uber [7, 17].  
- ethics: it might be difficult to embed all ethical and fairness constraints into an ml system [5]. likewise with safety, interpretations and explanations may help to 'debug' unfair models. for example, based on explanation methods, the propublica analysis of the correctional offender management profiling for alternative sanctions (compas) recidivism model [4, 18] has revealed that the compas could be racially biased<sup>1</sup>.  
- mismatched objectives: often the objective function of the ml model and the end-goal are mismatched [5]. for instance, in a clinical dataset, accurate medical diagnosis may be missing for a substantial group of conservatively treated patients, if the final diagnosis is confirmed only surgically. in this case, the objective function does not provide complete information about performance, and interpreting the model can yield additional insights.

from the legal perspective, interpretable and explainable ml is more amenable to the eu general data protection regulation (gdpr) [7] that states data subjects' right to an explanation of algorithmic decisions and right to be informed. it is worth mentioning that the gdpr does not prohibit black box predictive models [7] and that the right to an explanation is not legally binding [19]. this, however, as wachter et al. note in [19], does not undermine social and ethical value of providing interpretations and explanations.

# 3 evaluation of interpretability

despite the abundance of methodological research, literature on evaluation metrics for interpretable and explainable ml is scarce [7]. there appears to be no uniform standard for quantitative in silico evaluation. doshi-velez and kim [5] provide the following classification of evaluation criteria:

- application-grounded evaluation [5] requires evaluating a method on an exact task together with human experts, representative of the target audience. for example, the best way of evaluating an explainable machine-learning-based decision support system for diagnosis would be to ask doctors to perform diagnosis assisted by the system [5] and compare their performance to a reasonable baseline. similar evaluation methods are widely accepted, for example, in the field of human-computer interaction [20] and, arguably, if implemented correctly, provide the strongest evidence of success.  
- human-grounded evaluation [5] can be seen as a relaxed version of the application-grounded evaluation. it requires conducting experiments with human users performing a, possibly, simplified task reminiscent of the target application [5]. a good example of human-grounded evaluation is the experiment conducted by ribeiro et al. in [21]. they recruited human subjects on amazon mechanical turk and compared their ability to choose the best text classification model based on explanations provided by different techniques [21]. in this case, human users were not experts in the subject area of texts.  
- functionally-grounded evaluation is, arguably, most appropriate for early feasibility studies and is the simplest to implement, since it requires no human subject experiments [5]. these methods use some formal mathematical definition of interpretability as a proxy measure [5]. for example, shrikumar et al. [22] performed an experiment evaluating different attribution methods based on the reduction in classification accuracy on the mnist dataset after masking important features identified by an attribution method.

in general, evaluation of interpretability remains largely an open problem. identifying good proxy metrics and developing rigorous criteria and desiderata for such measures is an important direction for the further research, according to doshi-velez and kim [5].

# 4.1 interpretable models

interpretable models are usually constrained and structured to reflect physical constraints, monotonicity, additivity, causality, sparsity and/or other desirable properties [4,7]. lipton [6] notes that a high-dimensional linear model is not more intrinsically interpretable than a compact neural network; on the other hand, a sparse linear model is comprehensible and easy to visualise. two desirable properties, according to [6], are simulatability and decomposability, i.e. a model must be comprehensible in a limited time and its inputs and parameters should be intuitively meaningful. below we provide several recent examples of machine learning models that fall into this broad category.

# 4.1.1 falling rule lists

rule-based classification algorithms have been known for a long time. one could argue that these well-established techniques are intrinsically interpretable. while single  $if$ -then rules are indeed comprehensible, inductive logic programming [25], for instance, yields an unordered set of conjunctive rules; on the other hand, decision trees [26] are not monotonic (thus, require additional mental effort) and rely on greedy partition procedures, which only explore a narrow solution subspace. wang and rudin [27] propose a new rule-based binary classification model that tries to address mentioned shortcomings - falling rule lists (flr). the model is motivated by the wide adoption of risk scores and risk stratification systems in the medical domain.

a falling rule list is a list of if-then rules s.t. (i) during classification, rules have to be applied in the order given by the list, and (ii) the probability of the case is monotonically decreasing within the list. wang and rudin provide an example of an flr for predicting the risk of malignancy in mammographic masses [27]. the restricted form of flrs makes them more comprehensible than decision trees and is natural for practical decision-making in a clinical setting. flrs are learnt using a bayesian modelling approach, wherein monotonicity and sparsity constraints are encoded in the prior distribution. the simulated annealing procedure is used to sample from the posterior distribution and obtain the map estimator [27]. wang and rudin argue

that, in practice, the sacrifice of performance due to a very restricted form is often not considerable, yet gains in interpretability are substantial.

chen and rudin [28] further relax the original optimisation problem of learning flrs by introducing softly falling rule lists. as opposed to [27], rather than having hard monotonicity constraints, they introduce a non-monotonicity penalty term. this formulation is likely better accommodated to noisy real-world datasets, where all sparse solutions might happen to be infeasible. another interesting extension are causal falling rule lists [29] that apply flrs to quantify treatment effects in the potential outcomes framework [30].

# 4.1.2 optimised risk scores

another interesting line of research, likewise motivated by medical risk scoring, are supersparse linear integer models (slim), introduced by ustun and rudin in [31]. as opposed to flrs [27-29] the key focus of which is monotonicity, slims represent sparse decision boundaries, i.e. relying on a limited number of predictor variables. moreover, interpretability is facilitated by learning a linear scoring function with integral coefficients.

roughly, slims are a special case of the following optimisation problem (see [31] for the full formulation):

$$
\min  _ {\boldsymbol {\beta}} \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \mathbb {1} _ {\left\{y _ {i} \boldsymbol {\beta} ^ {t} \mathbf {x} _ {i} \leq 0 \right\}} + \lambda_ {0} \| \boldsymbol {\beta} \| _ {0} + \lambda_ {1} \| \boldsymbol {\beta} \| _ {1}, \text {s . t .} \boldsymbol {\beta} \in \mathcal {b}, \tag {1}
$$

where  $\beta \in \mathcal{b}$  is an integer-valued coefficient vector with  $\mathcal{b} = \{l, l + 1, \dots, u - 1, u\}^p$ ,  $l < u$ ;  $(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)$  are labelled data points; and  $n$  is the size of the training set. the integer linear program (ilp) defined in equation 1 enjoys the advantages of directly minimising the 0-1 loss and the  $\ell_0$  penalty instead of convex surrogate measures, commonly adopted in statistics and machine learning literature, cf. [32]. given recent improvements in ilp solvers, such problems have become tractable even for datasets with 1000s of data points: ustun and rudin [31] report a running time of under 10 minutes for  $n = 1922$ .

in addition to the theoretical guarantees, the ilp formulation above has a benefit of easily incorporating and enforcing additional constraints [31], beyond integrality and sparsity. for example, introducing desirable 'either-or' or 'if-then' conditions on features or preferences for (not) using certain predictors. ustun and rudin also introduce a range of extensions/generalisations of slims. particularly interesting are personalised slims - a soft version with scoring rules differing for individual data points. as opposed to slims that are globally interpretable, personalised slims are only locally interpretable. the authors also present rule-based adaptations [31]. further algorithmic improvements to this approach are made by ustun and rudin in [33].

# 4.1.3 generalised additive models

as mentioned before, decomposability is a desirable property of interpretable ml models [6]. a noteworthy class of decomposable functions are additively separable functions [34]. we say that function  $f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{p})$  is additively separable if we can rewrite it in the form  $f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{p}) = \sum_{j = 1}^{p}u_{j}(x_{j})$  [34]. hastie and tibshirani [35] introduce the class of generalised additive models (gam) that rely on this additivity property. in particular, for  $p$  predictor variables, a gam is given by

$$
g (y) = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {p} s _ {j} \left(x _ {j}\right), \tag {2}
$$

where  $g(\cdot)$  is a link function,  $s_j(\cdot)$  are smooth functions [35], which we will refer to as shape functions [8]. this class can be seen as an extension of the linear model that preserves the additivity, but allows introducing nonlinearities in individual predictors by choosing appropriate shape functions.

lou et al. [8] argue in favour of the intelligibility of gams: this class of models ignores interactions between predictor variables, therefore, influence of each predictor is easily comprehensible and can be visualised. they conduct extensive experimental comparison among different methods for fitting gams and choices of  $s_j(\cdot)$ . they consider least squares, gradient boosting (with slight adjustments to the original procedure in [3]), and backfitting. in addition to the standard use of spline-based shape functions (referred to as scatterplot smoothers in [35]), they consider single, bagged, boosted, and boosted bagged decision trees [8].

building on [8], caruana et al. [9] propose a simple, yet more performant extension by including two-way interaction terms, referred to as  $\mathrm{ga}^2\mathrm{m}$  (cf. equation 2):

$$
g (y) = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {p} s _ {j} \left(x _ {j}\right) + \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {p} \sum_ {\substack {k = 1 \\ k \neq j}} ^ {p} s _ {j k} \left(x _ {j}, x _ {k}\right), \tag{3}
$$

where  $s_{jk}(x_j, x_k)$  are pairwise interaction terms. caruana et al. argue that pairwise interactions are still intelligible, since they can be visualised using heat maps [9]. the authors provide a pneumonia risk prediction case study showing that  $\mathrm{ga}^2\mathrm{ms}$  can perform on par with uninterpretable techniques in tabular datasets, while being more amenable to verification and debugging.

another interesting extension of additive regression modelling are sparse additive models (spam), proposed by ravikumar et al. in [36]. this model class combines ideas of hastie and tibshirani [35] with sparse linear modelling for high-dimensional regression problems, e.g. [32]. in addition to shape functions  $s_j(\cdot)$ , ravikumar et al. [36] introduce a weight vector  $\pmb{\beta} \in \mathbb{r}^p$  multiplied with outputs of  $s_j(\cdot)$  and penalise its norm in the loss function. in this way, spams rely on a sparse subset of features and still preserve the additive structure of the classical gams.

# 4.1.4 sparse-input neural networks

in many high-dimensional problems, e.g. in genomic data analysis [37], social network modelling [38] etc., sparsity is an important inductive bias, which allows deducing parsimonious interpretations. we have already mentioned sparsity as a desirable property when describing supersparse linear integer models in section 4.1.2. recently, there have been renewed efforts in leveraging sparsity-inducing regularisation to understand and control the behaviour of neural networks models [39-42].

feng and simon [39] provide a thorough analysis, both theoretical and empirical, of sparse-input neural networks (spinns) in the context of  $p \gg n$  problems [43]. spinns are trained by solving the following optimisation problem:

$$
\min  _ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ell \left(y _ {i}, f _ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} \left(\mathbf {x} _ {i}\right)\right) + \lambda_ {0} \sum_ {a = 2} ^ {l} \left\| \mathbf {w} _ {a} \right\| _ {2} ^ {2} + \lambda \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {p} \omega_ {\alpha} \left(\mathbf {w} _ {1, j}\right), \tag {4}
$$

where  $\pmb{\theta} = \{\mathbf{w}_a\}_{a=1}^l$  are weight matrices;  $\mathbf{w}_{1,j}$  refers to the  $j$ -th column of the input layer weight matrix; and  $\omega_{\alpha}(\pmb{\beta}) = (1 - \alpha)\|\pmb{\beta}\|_1 + \alpha\|\pmb{\beta}\|_2$  with  $\alpha \in (0,1)$  is the sparse group lasso penalty. here, parameter  $\alpha$  controls the trade-off between the element-wise lasso and the group lasso penalties. feng and simon [39] prove probabilistic, finite-sample generalisation guarantees for this model class and demonstrate performance gains empirically for high-dimensional data with higher-order interactions, when compared to other nonparametric models. tank et al. [41] and khanna and tan [42] leverage similar penalties in the context of autoregressive time series modelling and granger-causal inference (see section 4.1.11).

# 4.1.5 knockoff features

the ultimate goal of spinns [39] is "deep" feature selection. lu et al. [40] propose another solution: deep feature selection using paired-input nonlinear knockoffs (deeppink) which leverages knockoff filters to facilitate interpretability and sparsity in deep neural networks at the input level. an important advantage of this technique over spinns [39] is that it provides a false discovery rate (fdr) control [44] when selecting significant features.

knockoff filters were originally proposed by barber and candès in [45]. in brief, knockoff filters are a variable selection procedure that controls the fdr exactly in a linear model in finite sample settings, whenever there are at least as many observations as predictor variables. the key idea is to construct knockoff features mimicking the dependency structure of the original features; augment the original dataset with knockoffs; and compare statistics for each original feature and its knockoff, e.g. the absolute value of the coefficient. in this way, variables with genuine signals can be identified while controlling for the fdr. candès et al. [46] propose 'model-  $x$  ' knockoffs which rely on the assumption that the joint distribution of covariates is known, without assuming anything about the distribution of the response variable conditional on predictors. the key limitation of [46] is that the generation of knockoff features is based on a known multivariate gaussian

distribution. jordan et al. [47] alleviate this issue by introducing the knockoffgan - a generative adversarial network (gan) [48] for knockoff generation capable of learning complex dependency structures. along similar lines, following the model- $x$  framework, romano et al. [49] propose constructing knockoff features with deep generative models utilising the maximum mean discrepancy (mmd) [50].

# 4.1.6 varying-coefficient models

while gams [35] generalise the linear model by allowing for nonlinearities in predictor variables (see section 4.1.3), varying-coefficient models (vcm), proposed by hastie and tibshirani in [23], offer a different sort of generalisation. in a vcm, covariate coefficients vary smoothly with so called 'effect modifiers' — variables  $r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_p$ :

$$
g (y) = \beta_ {0} + \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {p} x _ {j} \beta_ {j} \left(r _ {j}\right), \tag {5}
$$

wherein  $\beta_{j}(\cdot)$  are unspecified smooth functions corresponding to the varying coefficients of predictor variables. the choice of variables  $r_1, \ldots, r_p$  depends on a particular application; for example,  $r_j$  may coincide with predictors  $x_j$  or correspond to some exogenous variables. in dynamical systems, time can be a single effect modifier, producing time-varying coefficients.

clearly, vcms are only locally interpretable, since coefficients vary across data points. nevertheless, this trade-off may be desirable in pursuit of a more flexible model. some of the interpretable ml models discussed further [51,52] bear a striking resemblance to vcms: they generalise this classical framework to unstructured data sources by parameterising  $\beta_{j}(\cdot)$  with neural networks.

# 4.1.7 contextual explanation networks

the 'credit assignment problem' in multilayer neural networks is difficult [53], largely limiting the interpretability of this family of models. quantification and explanation of contributions of individual inputs to the resulting predictions is not straightforward, due to entangled interactions in downstream layers [41, 53]. nevertheless, there has been a substantial effort to explain neural network models post hoc, i.e. after training: [54], [22], and [55] are just a few examples (see section 4.2.1 for an overview of similar techniques).

to this end, al-shedivat et al. [51] propose contextual explanation networks (cen) - a class of neural network architectures that jointly predict and explain their predictions without requiring additional model introspection. cens are defined as deep probabilistic models for learning  $\mathbf{p}_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c})$ , parameterised by  $\mathbf{w}$ , where  $c \in \mathcal{c}$  are context variables,  $\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{x}$  are attributes, and  $\mathbf{y} \in \mathcal{y}$  are targets, to be predicted from  $\mathbf{x}$  and  $\mathbf{c}$ . given  $\mathbf{x}$ ,  $\mathbf{y}$ , and  $\mathbf{c}$ , the probabilistic model defined in [51] is of the following form:

$$
\mathbf {y} \sim \mathrm {p} (\mathbf {y} | \mathbf {x}, \boldsymbol {\theta}),
$$

$$
\boldsymbol {\theta} \sim \mathrm {p} _ {\mathrm {w}} (\boldsymbol {\theta} \mid \mathbf {c}),
$$

$$
\mathbf {p} _ {\mathrm {w}} (\mathbf {y} \mid \mathbf {x}, \mathbf {c}) = \int \mathbf {p} (\mathbf {y} \mid \mathbf {x}, \boldsymbol {\theta}) \mathbf {p} _ {\mathrm {w}} (\boldsymbol {\theta} \mid \mathbf {c}) d \boldsymbol {\theta}, \tag {6}
$$

where  $\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol {\theta})$  is a predictor parameterised by  $\pmb{\theta}$ , explicitly relating attributes to targets.  $\pmb{\theta}$  can be seen as an explanation that is specific to the context given by  $\mathbf{c}$ . in practice,  $\mathbf{p}_{\mathbf{w}}(\boldsymbol {\theta}|\mathbf{c})$  is replaced with an encoder neural network and the predictive distribution  $\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol {\theta})$  is specified by an interpretable function, e.g. the linear model  $f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathrm{softmax}\bigl (\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\bigr)$ .

the relationship between vcms [23] and cens [51] is clear: contextual variables  $\mathbf{c}$  can be seen as effect modifiers for attributes  $\mathbf{x}$  (cf. equation 5). the key contribution of cens is to cast the classical generalisation of linear models into a probabilistic framework while parameterising coefficients with neural networks. al-shedivat et al. demonstrate the efficacy of their approach on classification and survival analysis [56] tasks and show that cens are still interpretable in datasets with noisy features where post hoc explanation techniques can often be inconsistent and misleading.

# 4.1.8 self-explaining neural networks

another interesting class of functions was introduced by alvarez-melis and jaakkola in [52]. similar to al-shedivat et al. [51], they propose an intrinsically interpretable neural network model that allows disentangling contributions of individual predictor variables or basis concepts. self-explaining neural networks (senn) [52] are motivated by explicitness, faithfulness, and stability properties - three desiderata for interpretability. senns act like a simple model locally, but can be highly complex and nonlinear globally:

$$
f (\mathbf {x}) = \theta (\mathbf {x}) ^ {\top} \mathbf {x}, \tag {7}
$$

where  $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{r}^p$  are predictors; and  $\theta(\cdot)$  is a neural network with  $p$  outputs.  $\theta(\mathbf{x})$  are generalised coefficients for data point  $\mathbf{x}$ . without further restrictions the model in equation 7 is not more interpretable than a simple multilayer neural network. we want the model to be locally linear: it needs to hold that  $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x}) \approx \theta(\mathbf{x}_0)$ , for all  $\mathbf{x}$  in the neighbourhood of  $\mathbf{x}_0$ . under this constraint, individual components of  $\theta(\mathbf{x})$  are interpretable and adaptive coefficients.

further extensions of the model given by equation 7 are possible. for example, we can introduce basis concepts  $h(\mathbf{x}) : \mathbb{r}^p \to \mathbb{r}^k$  and use them instead of the raw predictors:  $f(\mathbf{x}) = \boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbf{x})^\top \boldsymbol{h}(\mathbf{x})$ . furthermore, a general link function  $g(\cdot)$  can be used, resulting in

$$
f (\mathbf {x}) = g \left(\theta (\mathbf {x}) _ {1} h (\mathbf {x}) _ {1}, \dots , \theta (\mathbf {x}) _ {k} h (\mathbf {x}) _ {k}\right), \tag {8}
$$

where  $z_{j} = \theta (\mathbf{x})_{j}h(\mathbf{x})_{j}$  is the influence score of the  $j$ -th concept.

in practice, a senn given by equation 8 is trained by minimising the following gradient-regularised loss function [52], which balances performance with interpretability:

$$
\mathcal {l} _ {y} (f (\mathbf {x}), y) + \lambda \mathcal {l} _ {\theta} (f (\mathbf {x})), \tag {9}
$$

where  $\mathcal{l}_y(f(\mathbf{x}), y)$  is a loss term for the ground classification or regression task, e.g. the mean squared error or the cross entropy;  $\lambda > 0$  is a regularisation parameter; and  $\mathcal{l}_{\theta}(f(\mathbf{x}))$  is the gradient penalty:

$$
\mathcal {l} _ {\theta} (f (\mathbf {x})) = \left\| \nabla_ {\mathbf {x}} f (\mathbf {x}) - \boldsymbol {\theta} (\mathbf {x}) ^ {\top} \mathbf {j} _ {\mathbf {x}} ^ {h} (\mathbf {x}) \right\| _ {2}, \tag {10}
$$

where  $\mathbf{j}_{\mathbf{x}}^{h}$  is the jacobian of  $h(\cdot)$  w.r.t.  $\mathbf{x}$ .

following are important restricting assumptions, crucial for enforcing interpretability:

1.  $g(\cdot)$  (see equation 8) is monotonic and additively separable in its arguments (see section 4.1.3);  
2.  $\frac{\partial g}{\partial z_i} > 0$  with  $z_i = \theta(\mathbf{x})_i h(\mathbf{x})_i$ , for all  $i$ ;  
3.  $\pmb{\theta}(\cdot)$  is locally difference-bounded by  $h(\cdot)$ , i.e. for every  $\mathbf{x}_0$ , there exist  $\delta > 0$  and  $l \in \mathbb{r}$  s.t. if  $\| \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_0 \|_2 < \delta$ , then  $\| \pmb{\theta}(\mathbf{x}) - \pmb{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_0) \|_2 \leq l \| h(\mathbf{x}) - h(\mathbf{x}_0) \|_2$ ;  
4.  $\{h(\mathbf{x})_i\}_{i = 1}^k$  are interpretable representations of  $\mathbf{x}$  
5.  $k$  is small.

the class of functions described by assumptions 1-5 is fairly flexible, e.g. generalised linear models [57] and the nearest neighbour classifier satisfy these assumptions [52]. the key advantage of senns stems from the richness of complex neural network architectures used for  $\theta (\cdot)$  and  $h(\cdot)$ .

quinn et al. [58] further extend senns to compositional data analysis (coda), such as high-throughput sequencing data. in coda, the log-ratio transform is often applied with some reference value in the denominator. quinn et al. validate their framework on 25 microbiome datasets, demonstrating benefits of nonlinearity and personalised interpretability [58] achieved by senns.

like cens [51], senns [52] are clearly related to varying-coefficient models [23] (see section 4.1.6). the key difference being that in senns, regressors themselves act as effect modifiers and that the framework is augmented with interpretable basis concepts, defined on top of the raw inputs. certainly, all three model classes discussed above [23, 51, 52] hold a promise of local interpretability while providing room for predictively powerful models.

# 4.1.9 attentive mixtures of experts

in natural language processing, the attention mechanism has become a powerful tool for exploring relationships between inputs and outputs of deep learning models [59], utilised both for interpretability and performance. nevertheless, several works critique the naïve use of attention for model interpretation [60, 61], showing empirically that it is often uncorrelated with gradient information and other natural feature importance measures. schwab et al. [62] propose a mixture of experts model with attentive gates for learning feature importance values alongside with making predictions, introducing an auxiliary objective to mitigate the aforementioned shortcomings of the naïve attention.

let us consider predicting a target variable  $y$  based on features  $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{r}^p$ . the attentive mixture of experts (ame) model, proposed by schwab et al. [62], consists of several connected experts. the model is given by the following equations:

$$
f (\mathbf {x}) = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {p} \underbrace {g _ {j} \left(\mathbf {h} _ {a l l}\right)} _ {a _ {j}} \underbrace {e _ {j} \left(x _ {j}\right)} _ {c _ {j}},
$$

$$
\mathbf {h} _ {a l l} = \left[ h _ {1}, c _ {1}, h _ {2}, c _ {2}, \dots , h _ {p}, c _ {p} \right], \tag {11}
$$

$$
a _ {j} = \frac {\exp \left(\mathbf {u} _ {j} ^ {\top} \mathbf {u} _ {s , j}\right)}{\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {p} \exp \left(\mathbf {u} _ {k} ^ {\top} \mathbf {u} _ {s , k}\right)}, \quad \text {f o r} 1 \leq j \leq p,
$$

$$
\mathbf {u} _ {j} = \sigma \left(\mathbf {w} _ {j} \mathbf {h} _ {a l l} + b _ {j}\right), \quad \text {f o r} 1 \leq j \leq p,
$$

where  $c_{j} = e(x_{j})$  is the output of the  $j$ -th expert;  $a_{j}$  is the output of the attentive gating network  $g_{j}(\cdot)$  and corresponds to the importance of the  $j$ -th feature;  $h_{j}$  denotes a hidden state from the  $j$ -th expert;  $\mathbf{u}_{j}$  is a projected representation of  $\mathbf{h}_{all}$ ;  $\mathbf{u}_{s,j}$  is a per-expert learnable context vector; and  $\sigma(\cdot)$  is an activation function.

the ame is trained end-to-end by minimising a loss function augmented with an auxiliary objective. the auxiliary objective encourages the importance score  $a_{j}$  to reflect the decrease in error associated with the contribution of the  $j$ -th expert, similar in spirit to the definition of the rf variable importance [24]:

$$
\delta \varepsilon_ {\mathbf {x}, j} = \varepsilon_ {\mathbf {x} \backslash \{j \}} - \varepsilon_ {\mathbf {x}}, \quad \text {f o r} 1 \leq j \leq p, \tag {12}
$$

where  $\varepsilon_{\mathbf{x} \setminus \{j\}}$  and  $\varepsilon_{\mathbf{x}}$  denote the prediction error of the model without the  $j$ -th feature and of the full model, respectively. desired attribution scores  $\delta \varepsilon_{\mathbf{x}, j}$  are normalised to produce

$$
\omega_ {\mathrm {x}, j} = \frac {\delta \varepsilon_ {\mathrm {x} , j}}{\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {p} \delta \varepsilon_ {\mathrm {x} , k}}, \quad \text {f o r} 1 \leq j \leq p. \tag {13}
$$

the auxiliary loss term is then given by the discrepancy between the normalised desired attribution scores and the outputs of attentive gating networks:

$$
\mathcal {l} _ {a u x} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} d \left(\boldsymbol {\omega} _ {\mathrm {x} _ {i}}, \boldsymbol {a} _ {\mathrm {x} _ {i}}\right), \tag {14}
$$

where  $d(\cdot, \cdot)$  is the discrepancy measure, e.g. the kullback-leibler divergence;  $\omega_{\mathbf{x}_i}$  and  $a_{\mathbf{x}_i}$  are normalised desired attribution scores and the outputs of attentive gating networks, respectively, for the  $i$ -th data point.

the attentive mixture of experts [62] successfully overcomes limitations of the naively trained attention mechanism by introducing regularisation, which forces learning importance scores reflective of the prediction error. next to cens [51] and senns [52], ames are yet another class of locally interpretable neural network architectures which produce individualised explanations for each data point at prediction time. the relationship between predictions and explanations is, arguably, more opaque within ames than within the previously described model classes.

# 4.1.10 symbolic regression

symbolic regression is a problem of inferring an analytic form for an unknown function which can only be queried [63, 64]. while neural networks do provide analytic representations, symbolic regression usually seeks parsimonious equations, e.g. making further restrictions to polynomial forms. thus, symbolic regression can be leveraged to learn interpretable functional relationships [65] from raw data. for example, when predicting target  $y$  based on features  $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{r}^p$ , a mathematical expression  $g(\mathbf{x}) = x_1 + 2\cos(x_2) + \exp(x_3) + 0.1$  could be a candidate solution for symbolic regression [65]. the optimisation problem can be formalised as follows:

$$
\min  _ {g \in \mathfrak {g}} \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ell (g (\mathbf {x} _ {i}), y _ {i}), \tag {15}
$$

where  $\mathfrak{g}$  is a set of succinct mathematical expressions and  $\ell (\cdot ,\cdot)$  is the loss function, e.g. if  $y$  is continuouslyvalued, a possible choice is  $\ell (g(\mathbf{x}_i),y_i) = (g(\mathbf{x}_i) - y_i)^2$ . some of the traditional approaches to symbolic regression include genetic programming [63] and simulated annealing [66]. in section 4.2.2, we discuss how symbolic modelling can be leveraged to explain black box models post hoc by approximating e.g. a neural network with a symbolic metamodel [67].

# 4.1.11 time series analysis

interpretable models can be instrumental in understanding and exploring relationships in multivariate time course data. inference of time-varying relationships has been addressed before with a variety of techniques which usually rely on sparse autoregressive models. an iconic example is the work by song et al. [68] who propose time-varying dynamic bayesian networks (tv-dbn) based on autoregression with kernelised weights and  $\ell_1$  regularisation. a similar approach is described by kolar et al. in [69] where the authors propose using  $\ell_1$ -regularised temporally-smoothed logistic regression for estimating time-varying dependency networks.

both of the approaches [68, 69] are closely related to the inference of granger causality (gc) [70, 71], the key difference being that gc relationships are directed and rely on the temporal precedence of the cause before its effect. as mentioned before, one of the desiderata for interpretable and explainable ml is causality (see section 1). gc has become a popular tool for exploratory analysis in economics [72], neuroscience [73], and other domains which require understanding multivariate dynamic stochastic systems. the latest approaches to inferring granger-causal relationships leverage neural networks [41, 42, 74-76] and are often based on interpretable regularised autoregressive models [41, 42, 75], reminiscent of the lasso granger method [77].

in general, the problem of relational learning [78] from time series data is an area of ongoing research. extensions and adaptations of the existing approaches to different scenarios, such as replicate- and time-varying dependency structures, data scarcity, and high dimensionality, can be of interest to both the ml community and application domain areas. for instance, in the medical domain, inferring relationships from longitudinal data can aid in discovering and understanding sequential events in patient disease trajectories [79] and exploring influence of interventions [80].

# 4.1.12 interpretable representation learning

sometimes it might be desirable to learn low-dimensional embeddings, or representations, in an unsupervised, weakly- or semi-supervised manner rather than explore relationships between raw variables, as most methods discussed in the previous sections do. representations can be useful in several (usually unknown at the time of representation learning) downstream applications. a desirable property that is targeted by some techniques in the representation learning domain is interpretability, which is usually attained by enforcing some constraints on representations.

for example, infogan [81], an information-theoretic extension of gans, proposed by chen et al. has been empirically shown to be able to learn disentangled representations, i.e. representations wherein separate sets of dimensions are uniquely correlated with salient semantically meaningful features of data. similar results can be achieved with variational autoencoders (vae), in particular,  $\beta$ -vae [82] introduces statistical independence constraints on embedding dimensions via a factorising prior distribution. such representations, for example, allow for a controlled generation of synthetic data. in theory, learning disentangled representations in a completely unsupervised manner without introducing inductive biases has been shown to be fundamentally impossible [83]. the latter result, nevertheless, does not diminish the opportunity to learn disentangled

representations by injecting inductive biases and implicit or explicit forms of supervision [83].

one line of research in this direction focuses on learning structured representations of sequential data [84]. for instance, li and mandt [85] propose a sequential vae architecture that disentangles representations into static and dynamic parts, providing partial control over content and dynamics of generated sequences. in [86], hsu et al. explore a somewhat similar problem and approach. there are several other works targeting the disentanglement of static and dynamic components in representations [84]. on the other hand, miladinovic et al. [87] propose disentangled state space models and consider a slightly different setting wherein sequences are observed in different environments, or domains. their aim is to discern domain-specific and domain-invariant factors of variation.

we refer the interested reader to the paper by tschannen et al. [84] for an exhaustive (slightly outdated) overview of advancements in autoencoder-based representation learning. many of the approaches discussed in [84] strive towards some form of interpretability in learnt representations, although often do not state that explicitly.

interpretable representation learning certainly goes beyond disentanglement. for example, manduchi et al. [88] propose deep probabilistic self-organising maps (dpsom) – a vae-based neural network architecture for probabilistic clustering. self-organising maps (som), originally proposed by kohonen in [89], provide a natural and interpretable discrete visualisation of high-dimensional data. thus, dpsom [88] learns rich representations for probabilistic clustering which can be presented in a human-comprehensible form. manduchi et al. showcase the dpsom by visualising patient state trajectories throughout time.

another noteworthy example of interpretable representation learning are concept bottleneck models [90,91] re-explored by koh et al. in [92]. as opposed to previous techniques, concept bottleneck models are completely supervised. for predicting target  $y$  based on features  $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{r}^p$ , a bottleneck model is given by  $f(g(\mathbf{x}))$ , where  $g: \mathbb{r}^p \to \mathbb{r}^k$  and  $f: \mathbb{r}^k \to \mathbb{r}$ . in concept bottlenecks,  $f(\cdot)$  relies completely on concepts  $\mathbf{c} = g(\mathbf{x})$ , and  $g(\cdot)$  is learnt in a supervised manner using labelled interpretable concepts. koh et al. explore a range of strategies for training such models [92]. in practice,  $f(\cdot)$  and  $g(\cdot)$  are parameterised by neural networks. an important advantage of concept bottlenecks is that at prediction time, an expert, e.g. a medical doctor, can intervene on incorrectly inferred concepts. arguably, applicability of concept bottleneck models is limited to areas and tasks where vast domain knowledge is available and where instances can be labelled by experts.

to summarise, the problem of interpretability in representation and, more broadly, unsupervised learning is still under-explored, despite a growing body of research [88, 92-94]. as can be seen from previous sections, many techniques focus exclusively on classification and regression tasks, while deep clustering, generative modelling, and representation learning have attracted comparatively less attention. with the emergence of new socially consequential application domains for these problems, a need for interpretable techniques is becoming apparent.

# 4.2 explanation methods

we now turn towards a completely different family of methods. according to rudin [4], explainable ml focuses on providing explanations for existing black box models by the means of training another surrogate model post hoc. explanations can take various forms: textual, visual, symbolic; even a data point from the training set or a synthetic data point can serve as an explanation [6]. an explanation can be global, i.e. characterise the whole dataset, e.g. random forest variable importance [24], or local, i.e. explain individual classification or regression outcomes [7], e.g. generalised coefficients  $\theta (\mathbf{x})$  in senns (see section 4.1.8 and equations 7 and 8) can be seen as a local explanation for data point  $\mathbf{x}$ . it can be model-specific, i.e. capable of explaining only a restricted class of models, or model-agnostic, i.e. applicable to an arbitrary model [7]. carvalho et al. summarise following desirable properties of an explanation technique [7]:

- faithfulness: an explanation model should be faithful w.r.t. the black box model it tries to explain, i.e., predictions produced by the two models should agree as much as possible.  
- consistency & stability: explanations for different models tackling the same task should be consistent, and explanations for similar data points should be similar as well. these properties might be difficult to attain, especially, considering the rashomon effect $^{3}$  [95,96] which occurs when multiple equally feasible explanations exist for one phenomenon.

- comprehensibility: an explainee should be able to easily comprehend explanations. this property is related to simulatability posited by lipton in [6] for interpretable models (see section 4.1).  
- certainty & novelty: explanations should convey (un-)certainty about predictions and should warn an explainee if the data point considered is 'far away' from the support of the training set.  
- representativeness: explanations should 'cover' training data evenly. this property is particularly relevant for prototype-based explanation techniques, e.g. [97] or [98], and, arguably, is of interest from the perspective of fairness (see section 1).

# 4.2.1 attribution methods

attribution methods are, arguably, the commonest family of explanation techniques. sundararajan et al. [99] define an attribution as follows. for a function  $f: \mathbb{r}^p \to [0,1]$  and an input  $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{r}^p$ , an attribution for  $\mathbf{x}$  w.r.t. some baseline  $\mathbf{x}_0$  is given by  $a_f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_0) = (a_1 \dots a_p)^\top \in \mathbb{r}^p$  wherein  $a_i$  is the contribution of  $x_i$  to the prediction made for  $\mathbf{x}$  by  $f(\cdot)$ . note that some of the methods described below do not require a reference sample  $\mathbf{x}_0$ . we will use this notation throughout the remainder of this section.

ancona et al. [100] distinguish two fundamentally different categories of attribution methods: sensitivity methods which quantify how strongly the output of the function  $f(\cdot)$  changes if an input variable is perturbed; and salience methods which quantify marginal effects of variables on the output of  $f(\cdot)$  compared to a baseline<sup>4</sup>. most of the recent attribution techniques focus specifically on explaining (deep) neural network models. many of these techniques implicitly or explicitly rely on gradient information to produce attributions [100]. in the remainder of this section, we describe a few popular and archetypal examples of attribution techniques.

lime. ribeiro et al. [21] propose local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (lime) which identify interpretable data representations faithful to a given black box classifier. the explanation is defined by the following optimisation problem:

$$
\xi (\mathbf {x}) = \underset {g \in \mathfrak {g}} {\arg \min } \mathscr {l} (f, g, \pi_ {\mathbf {x}}) + \omega (g), \tag {16}
$$

where  $\mathfrak{g}$  is a class of explanation models;  $\mathcal{l}(\cdot, \cdot, \cdot)$  is a fidelity function which quantifies unfaithfulness of  $g(\mathbf{x})$  in approximating  $f(\cdot)$  within the proximity of  $\mathbf{x}$ , defined by  $\pi_{\mathbf{x}}$ ; and  $\omega(\cdot)$  is a complexity penalty. essentially,  $\mathcal{l}(\cdot, \cdot, \cdot)$  is a locality-aware loss function and, in practice, is minimised in a model-agnostic manner. usually  $\mathfrak{g}$  is chosen to be a restricted class of models that are intrinsically interpretable (see section 4.1), e.g. linear models, gams etc. during training, instances are sampled around each data point  $\mathbf{x}_i$  weighted by  $\pi_{\mathbf{x}_i}$ . in addition to local explanations produced by  $\xi(\cdot)$ , ribeiro et al. [21] introduce a procedure for obtaining a global understanding of the model  $f(\cdot)$ . given a limited budget, this procedure picks a number of explanations based on greedy submodular optimisation [101] and aggregates them into global variable importance statistics.

deeplift. shrikumar et al. [22] take a different approach to attribution, introducing an efficient method for disentangling contributions of inputs in a neural network - deep learning important features (deeplift). as opposed to lime, deeplift relies on comparisons to a reference (baseline) data point.

let  $t$  be the target neuron of interest (usually one of the output neurons) and let  $\eta_1, \eta_2, \dots, \eta_k$  be intermediate neurons (potentially, from several layers) that suffice to compute  $t$ . let  $\delta t = t - t_0$  be the difference from the reference. we then seek to assign contribution scores  $c_{\delta \eta_i \delta t}$  so that they satisfy the summation-to-delta property:

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {k} c _ {\delta \eta_ {i} \delta t} = \delta t. \tag {17}
$$

an intuitive interpretation is that  $c_{\delta \eta_i \delta t}$  is the amount of 'blame' for the difference in outputs assigned to a difference in intermediate neurons. importantly,  $c_{\delta \eta_i \delta t}$  are not restricted to be 0 when  $\frac{\partial t}{\partial \eta_i} = 0$  and, thus, arguably, address some of the limitations of gradient-based measures.

furthermore, by analogy to the partial derivative, shrikumar et al. [22] define the multiplier as follows:

$$
m _ {\delta \eta_ {i} \delta t} = \frac {c _ {\delta \eta_ {i} \delta t}}{\delta t}. \tag {18}
$$

in practice, we are not necessarily interested in contributions of hidden layer units. therefore, we consider the following definition of multipliers for inputs, which is consistent with the summation-to-delta property given by equation 17:

$$
m _ {\delta x _ {i} \delta t} = \sum_ {j} m _ {\delta x _ {i} \delta \eta_ {j}} m _ {\delta \eta_ {j} \delta t}, \tag {19}
$$

where  $\eta_{j}$  denote hidden units. equation 19 is informally referred to as the chain rule for multipliers. the authors propose several propagation rules for assigning  $c_{\delta \eta_j\delta t}$  which alongside with the summation-to-delta and chain rule properties facilitate the attribution w.r.t. to an appropriate baseline sample. key challenges in applying the deeplift framework are adapting it to more complex or specialised neural network architectures and finding an informative reference sample (or multiple ones) [22].

shap. lundberg and lee [102] present a framework of shapley additive explanations (shap). this framework builds on shapley regression values, inspired by the game theoretic concept of shapley values [103]. for the  $j$ -th variable, the shapley regression value is given by

$$
\phi_ {j} = \sum_ {\mathcal {s} \subseteq \mathcal {f} \backslash \{j \}} \frac {| \mathcal {s} | ! \left(| \mathcal {f} | - | \mathcal {s} | - 1\right) !}{| \mathcal {f} | !} \left\{f _ {\mathcal {s} \cup \{j \}} \left(\mathbf {x} _ {\mathcal {s} \cup \{j \}}\right) - f _ {\mathcal {s}} \left(\mathbf {x} _ {\mathcal {s}}\right) \right\}, \tag {20}
$$

where  $\mathcal{f} = \{1,2,\dots,p\}$  is the set of all predictor variables;  $\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{s}}$  is a vector composed of the components of  $\mathbf{x}$  that are in  $\mathcal{s} \subseteq \mathcal{f}$ ; and  $f_{\mathcal{s}}(\cdot)$  is a function fitted in predictor variables in the set  $\mathcal{s}$ . in practice, equation 20 does not have to be evaluated exactly and can be approximated by randomly sampling subsets  $\mathcal{s} \subseteq \mathcal{f}$ .

lundberg and lee [102] propose a model-agnostic kernel approximation of shapley regression values described above. they also demonstrate that both lime [21] and deeplift [22] are special cases of the shap framework that resort to model-specific approximations of equation 20.

integrated gradients. sundararajan et al. [99] introduce the attribution method of integrated gradients that is motivated by two fundamental axioms: sensitivity axiom posits that (i) if an input differs from a baseline in one variable and has a prediction outcome different from the baseline, then the differing variable should be assigned with a non-zero attribution; (ii) if  $f(\cdot)$  is constant in some variable, then this variable should be given zero attribution; implementation invariance states that for two functionally equivalent black box models, attributions should be identical. integrated gradients satisfy point (i) of sensitivity and implementation invariance. for the  $j$ -th variable and baseline  $\mathbf{x}_0$ , the integrated gradient is given by

$$
\operatorname {i g} _ {j} ^ {f} (\mathbf {x}) = \left(x _ {j} - x _ {0 j}\right) \int_ {\alpha = 0} ^ {1} \frac {\partial f \left(\mathbf {x} _ {0} + \alpha [ \mathbf {x} - \mathbf {x} _ {0} ]\right)}{\partial x _ {j}} d \alpha . \tag {21}
$$

observe that  $\mathrm{ig}^f (\mathbf{x})$  is an integral of gradients along the straight path between  $\mathbf{x}$  and  $\mathbf{x}_0$ . yet another important property satisfied by integrated gradients is completeness: if  $f$  is differentiable almost everywhere,  $\sum_{j = 1}^{p}\mathrm{ig}_{j}^{f}(\mathbf{x}) = f(\mathbf{x}) - f(\mathbf{x}_0)$ .

definition in equation 21 can be generalised further by considering some non-straight path between  $\mathbf{x}$  and  $\mathbf{x}_0$ . path integrated gradients are defined for specified paths  $\gamma = (\gamma_1, \dots, \gamma_p): [0, 1] \to \mathbb{r}^p$ :

$$
\mathrm {i g} _ {j} ^ {f \gamma} (\mathbf {x}) = \int_ {\alpha = 0} ^ {1} \frac {\partial f (\gamma (\alpha))}{\partial \gamma_ {j} (\alpha)} \frac {\partial \gamma_ {j} (\alpha)}{\partial \alpha} d \alpha . \tag {22}
$$

path integrated gradients are unique attributions that fulfil points (i) and (ii) of sensitivity, implementation invariance, and completeness. in fact, path integrated gradients correspond to a generalisation of shapley values proposed by aumann and shapley [104] in the context of infinite games within the cooperative game theory.

choosing paths of integration in an informed manner has been a topic of fruitful research, e.g. jha et al. [105] propose enhanced integrated gradients and demonstrate that nonlinear paths can improve interpretability on

the case study of rna splicing prediction. a noteworthy extension of integrated gradients is discussed in [106] by dhamdhere et al. who introduce a notion of conductance which quantifies a 'flow' of attribution through an individual hidden unit. merrill et al. [107] further extend integrated gradients to a wider range of model classes by considering partially differentiable functions.

full-gradient representations of neural networks. srinivas and fleuret [108] attempt to reconcile local and global perspectives on variable importance and introduce full-gradient (fullgrad) saliency maps which assign importance to both input variables and feature detectors (intermediate units) within a neural network. the framework is motivated by two relaxed notions of local and global attribution: weak dependence and completeness.

a saliency map is a function  $s(\mathbf{x}) = \sigma(f, \mathbf{x}) \in \mathbb{r}^p$ .  $s(\cdot)$  is said to be weakly dependent if for a piecewise linear function

$$
f (\mathbf {x}) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l} \mathbf {w} _ {0} ^ {\top} \mathbf {x} + b _ {0},   \mathbf {x} \in \mathcal {u} _ {0} \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf {w} _ {b} ^ {\top} \mathbf {x} + b _ {b},   \mathbf {x} \in \mathcal {u} _ {b}, \end{array} \right.,
$$

where  $\mathcal{u}_b\subset \mathbb{r}^p$ $0\leq b\leq b$  , are open and connected sets,  $s(\mathbf{x})$  is constant in  $\mathbf{x}$  within  $\mathcal{u}_b$  , for all  $b$  . according to [108],  $s(\cdot)$  is complete if there exists a function  $\phi (\cdot ,\cdot)$  s.t.  $\phi (s(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x}) = f(\mathbf{x})$  , for all  $f(\cdot)$  and  $\mathbf{x}$  . it can be shown that, for a general piecewise linear  $f(\cdot)$  , there exists no saliency map  $s(\cdot)$  that is both weakly dependent and complete.

for a neural network with  $relu$  activations and biases, implicit and explicit,  $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{r}^f$ , the full-gradient representation is specified by

$$
f (\mathbf {x}; \mathbf {b}) = \nabla_ {\mathbf {x}} f (\mathbf {x}; \mathbf {b}) ^ {\top} \mathbf {x} + \nabla_ {\mathbf {b}} f (\mathbf {x}; \mathbf {b}) ^ {\top} \mathbf {b}. \tag {23}
$$

note that for non-relu networks this representation can be approximated by linearising activation functions. a full gradient is, generally, given by a pair  $g = \left( \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x}), f^{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{x}) \right) \in \mathbb{r}^{p + f}$ . if the neural network contains only relu nonlinearities without batch-norm, then  $g$  is weakly dependent and complete, thus, striking a perfect balance between locality and globality. in addition to he theoretical result above, srinivas and fleuret [108] provide a practical approximation for the full-gradient representation of convolutional neural networks.

although attribution methods have become an active and important area of research, their general applicability and usefulness have been scrutinised and criticised. for instance, rudin [4] argues that explanation and, in particular, attribution methods cannot be completely faithful w.r.t. the original black box model and that attributions often do not provide sufficient information about how the model actually works, they rather tell us where the model looks. kumar et al. [109] criticise shapley-value-based explanations, such as described in [21, 22, 102], for their reliance on the additivity axiom [103] and lack of human-groundedness and contrastiveness.

# 4.2.2 symbolic metamodels

in section 4.1.10, we described symbolic regression as an approach to learning interpretable mathematical expressions from raw data. similar to lime [21] (see section 4.2.1), symbolic regression can be used for metamodelelling already learnt opaque predictive models. to this end, alaa and van der schaar [67] propose an elegant parameterisation of the symbolic regression problem that admits optimisation by gradient descent, in contrast to genetic programming [63] and simulated annealing [66] techniques which require searching through a discrete solution space.

according to alaa and van der schaar [67], the symbolic metamodelelling problem reduces to the following (cf. equation 15):

$$
\min  _ {g \in \mathfrak {g}} \ell (g, f) = \min  _ {g \in \mathfrak {g}} \int_ {\mathcal {x}} (g (\mathbf {x}) - f (\mathbf {x})) ^ {2} d \mathbf {x}, \tag {24}
$$

where  $\ell (\cdot ,\cdot)$  is a metamodelling loss and  $\mathfrak{g}$  is a class of succinct mathematical expressions. in this section, we assume  $d$  -dimensional data points, i.e.  $\mathbf{x}\in \mathbb{r}^d$  . we seek a parameterisation of  $\mathfrak{g}$  which would make the optimisation problem in equation 24 'easier'; in particular, given parameterisation  $\mathfrak{g} = \{g(\cdot ,\theta):\theta \in \theta \}$  , the problem above reduces to  $\min_{\theta \in \theta}\ell (g(\cdot ,\theta),f(\cdot))$

by kolmogorov-arnold superposition theorem [67, 110, 111], function  $g(\cdot)$  can be rewritten in the following form:

$$
g (\mathbf {x}) = \sum_ {i = 0} ^ {r} g _ {i} ^ {\text {o u t}} \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {d} g _ {i, j} ^ {\text {i n}} \left(x _ {j}\right)\right), \tag {25}
$$

where  $g_{i,j}^{\mathrm{in}}(\cdot)$  and  $g_{i}^{\mathrm{out}}(\cdot)$  are continuous basis functions. observe that if  $r = 1$ , then  $g(\cdot)$  is a gam [35] (see section 4.1.3). equation 25 yields parameterisation  $g(\mathbf{x};\theta) = g\big(\mathbf{x};\big\{g_{i,j}^{\mathrm{in}}\big\}_{i,j},\big\{g_{i}^{\mathrm{out}}\big\}_{i}\big)$ . alaa and van der schaar [67] further parameterise basis functions themselves by representing them as meijer  $g$ -functions [112], i.e. in the form of a linear integral in complex plane  $s$ :

$$
g _ {p, q} ^ {m, n} \binom {a _ {1}, a _ {2}, \dots , a _ {p}} {b _ {1}, b _ {2}, \dots , b _ {q}} | x) = \frac {1}{2 \pi i} \int_ {\mathscr {l}} \frac {\prod_ {j = 1} ^ {m} \gamma \left(b _ {j} - s\right) \prod_ {j = 1} ^ {n} \gamma \left(1 - a _ {j} + s\right)}{\prod_ {j = m + 1} ^ {q} \gamma \left(1 - b _ {j} + s\right) \prod_ {j = n + 1} ^ {p} \gamma \left(a _ {j} + s\right)} x ^ {s} d s, \tag {26}
$$

where  $\gamma(\cdot)$  is the gamma function;  $\mathcal{l}$  is the integration path;  $\mathbf{a}_p = \left(a_1 a_2 \ldots a_p\right)$  and  $\mathbf{b}_q = \left(b_1 b_2 \ldots b_q\right)$  are real-valued parameters that, alongside with  $m$  and  $n$ , determine poles and zeros of the integrand. most familiar functional forms are special cases of meijer g-functions. in the end, parameterisation  $g(\mathbf{x}; \theta)$  is given by:

$$
g (\mathbf {x}; \theta) = \sum_ {i = 0} ^ {r} g _ {p, q} ^ {m, n} \left(\theta_ {i} ^ {\text {o u t}} \left| \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {d} g _ {p, q} ^ {m, n} \left(\theta_ {i, j} ^ {\text {i n}} \right| x _ {j}\right)\right). \tag {27}
$$

an important property of meijer g-functions is their closure under differentiation. this allows searching  $\mathfrak{g}$  efficiently using the gradient descent procedure.

symbolic metamodelling [67] and other symbolic regression techniques [64-66] are a compelling alternative to attribution methods (see section 4.2.1), especially, when a parsimonious analytical representation of the learnt function is desirable. the parameterisation proposed by alaaa and van der schaar [67] is an interesting and promising reformulation of this classical problem.

# 4.2.3 counterfactual explanations

in some applications, it might be of key interest to provide human-friendly explanations [7] which are comprehensible to a wide nonspecialist audience. techniques discussed so far were mostly addressing the question "why this prediction was made?", counterfactual explanations, on the other hand, try to answer the question "why this prediction was made instead of another one?" [7]. such techniques focus on producing contrastive and actionable local explanations, which can be useful in various real-world settings, e.g. when suggesting lifestyle changes to a patient to reduce her risks or providing reasons for the low creditworthiness of a company.

wachter et al. [19] formalise counterfactual explanations in the context of explainable ml. they propose solving the following optimisation problem to find a counterfactual explanation  $\mathbf{x}'$  for a data point  $(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)$  and a black box model  $f(\cdot)$ :

$$
\min  _ {\mathbf {x} ^ {\prime}} d \left(\mathbf {x} _ {i}, \mathbf {x} ^ {\prime}\right) + \lambda \ell \left(f \left(\mathbf {x} ^ {\prime}\right), y ^ {\prime}\right), \tag {28}
$$

where  $d(\cdot, \cdot)$  is an appropriate distance function;  $y'$  is chosen to be meaningfully different from  $y_i$  and  $\ell(f(\mathbf{x}', y'))$  quantifies how 'different' output for  $\mathbf{x}'$  is from the  $y'$  chosen, e.g. mse for regression or hinge loss for classification;  $\lambda$  is a parameter controlling the slackness on the constraint  $f(\mathbf{x}') = y'$ . the problem given by equation 28 is reminiscent of generating adversarial perturbations. perturbations are encouraged to be sparse by penalising  $d(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}')$ . in particular, wachter et al. propose using the following distance function:

$$
d \left(\mathbf {x}, \mathbf {x} ^ {\prime}\right) = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {p} \frac {\left| x _ {j} - x _ {j} ^ {\prime} \right|}{\mathrm {m a d} _ {j}}, \tag {29}
$$

where  $\mathrm{mad}_j$  denotes the median absolute deviation of the  $j$ -th variable. distance function in equation 29 favours sparse changes in  $\mathbf{x}'$  compared to  $\mathbf{x}$  and accounts for variability of individual features.

mothilal et al. [113] extend the framework introduced by wachter et al. [19] focusing on providing multiple diverse counterfactual explanations. in particular, for a data point  $\mathbf{x}_i$ , explanations  $\mathbf{c}_1,\dots,\mathbf{c}_k$  are found by

solving the optimisation problem below (cf. equation 28):

$$
\min  _ {\mathbf {c} _ {1}, \dots , \mathbf {c} _ {k}} \frac {1}{k} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {k} \ell (f (\mathbf {c} _ {k}), y ^ {\prime}) + \frac {\lambda_ {1}}{k} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {k} d (\mathbf {x} _ {i}, \mathbf {c} _ {k}) - \lambda_ {2} \det  (\mathbf {s}), \tag {30}
$$

where  $s_{k,l} = \frac{1}{1 + d(\mathbf{c}_{k},\mathbf{c}_{l})}$ , and  $\operatorname*{det}(\mathbf{s})$  quantifies diversity among counterfactual explanations  $\mathbf{c}_1,\dots,\mathbf{c}_k$ . in addition, mothilal et al. [113] propose quantitative evaluation metrics for counterfactual explanation techniques. validity quantifies how many of the proposed explanations are actually counterfactual. in the setting of classification, validity is given by

$$
\frac {1}{k} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {k} \mathbb {1} _ {\{f \left(\mathbf {c} _ {k}\right) \neq y _ {i} \}}. \tag {31}
$$

proximity measures the 'closeness' of explanations to the original data point:

$$
- \frac {1}{k} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {k} d \left(\mathbf {c} _ {k}, \mathbf {x} _ {i}\right). \tag {32}
$$

sparsity quantifies how sparse are the perturbations of  $\mathbf{x}_i$  induced by the set of explanations:

$$
1 - \frac {1}{k p} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {k} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {p} \mathbb {1} _ {\left\{c _ {k, j} \neq x _ {i, j} \right\}}. \tag {33}
$$

finally, diversity evaluates how diverse proposed explanations are:

$$
\delta \left(\mathbf {c} _ {1}, \dots , \mathbf {c} _ {k}\right) = \frac {1}{k ^ {2}} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {k - 1} \sum_ {l = k + 1} ^ {k} d \left(\mathbf {c} _ {k}, \mathbf {c} _ {l}\right). \tag {34}
$$

approaches of wachter et al. [19] and mothilal et al. [113] rely on performing gradient descent and, thus, assume that the black box model  $f(\cdot)$  is differentiable. karimi et al. [114] generalise this framework and propose model-agnostic counterfactual explanations (mace). they transform the original optimisation problem into a sequence of boolean satisfiability problems (sat) and leverage powerful satisfiability modulo theories (smt) solvers to solve these. an important advantage of mace is its complete agnosticism to the choice of the black box model or distance function and ability to incorporate plausibility constraints that allow injecting domain-specific knowledge.

the problem of counterfactual explanation (see equations 28 and 30) naturally admits a generative model as a solution. recently, several papers have utilised deep generative models [115-117] to address problems similar to the ones considered by wachter et al. [19] and mothilal et al. [113]. chang et al. [115] introduce saliency maps based on counterfactual generation with masking for explaining image classifiers. liu et al. [117] leverage gans to generate minimal change counterfactual examples for image classifiers. last but not least, mahajan et al. propose a vae-based counterfactual generative model that focuses on feasibility and preservation of causal constraints, introducing regularisation derived from a structural causal model (scm) [118].

another perspective on counterfactual explanations is provided by algorithmic recourse, surveyed in detail by karimi et al. in [119]. algorithmic recourse focuses on explaining the decisions and recommending further actions to "individuals who are unfavourably treated by automated decision-making systems" [119]. in [120], karimi et al. criticise counterfactual explanations for the lack of actionability and provide a causal perspective of algorithmic recourse by considering interventions rather than explanations. to avoid infeasible or costly recommendations resulting from naïve counterfactual explanations [19, 113] karimi et al. [120] propose finding minimal cost structural interventions resulting in a favourable outcome. while this approach certainly offers an interesting and, possibly, more user-centred perspective, the core limitation of algorithmic recourse, as outlined in [120] and [121], is the unrealistic assumption of a known causal structure.

# 5 summary

in this literature review, we provided a survey of interpretable and explainable machine learning methods (see tables 1 and 2 for the summary of the techniques), described commonest goals and desiderata for these techniques, motivated their relevance in several fields of application, and discussed their quantitative evaluation. interpretability and explainability still remain an active area of research, especially, in the face of recent rapid progress in designing highly performant predictive models and inevitable infusion of machine learning into other domains, where decisions have far-reaching consequences.

for years the field has been challenged by a lack of clear definitions for interpretability or explainability, these terms being often wielded "in a quasi-mathematical way" [6, 122]. for many techniques, there still exist no satisfactory functionally-grounded evaluation criteria and universally accepted benchmarks, hindering reproducibility and model comparison. moreover, meaningful adaptations of these methods to 'real-world' machine learning systems and data analysis problems largely remain a matter for the future. it has been argued that, for successful and widespread use of interpretable and explainable machine learning models, stakeholders need to be involved in the discussion [4, 122]. a meaningful and equal collaboration between machine learning researchers and stakeholders from various domains, such as medicine, natural sciences, and law, is a logical next step within the evolution of interpretable and explainable ml.
