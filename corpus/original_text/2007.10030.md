# Modern Random Access: an Age of Information Perspective on Irregular Repetition Slotted ALOHA

Andrea Munari, Senior Member, IEEE

Abstract—Age of information (AoI) is gaining attention as a valuable performance metric for many IoT systems, in which a large number of devices report time-stamped updates to a central gateway. This is the case, for instance, of remote sensing, monitoring, or tracking, with broad applications in the industrial, vehicular, and environmental domain. In these settings, AoI provides insights that are complementary to those offered by throughput or latency, capturing the ability of the system to maintain an up-to-date view of the status of each transmitting device. From this standpoint, while a good understanding of the metric has been reached for point-to-point links, relatively little attention has been devoted to the impact that link layer solutions employed in IoT systems may have on AoI. In particular, no result is available for modern random access protocols, which have recently emerged as promising solutions to support massive machine-type communications. To start addressing this gap we provide in this paper the first study of the AoI of a scheme in this family, namely irregular repetition slotted ALOHA (IRSA) [1]. By means of a Markovian analysis, we track the AoI evolution at the gateway, prove that the process is ergodic, and derive a compact closed form expression for its stationary distribution. Leaning on this, we compute exact formulations for the average AoI and the age violation probability. The study reveals non-trivial design trade-offs for IRSA and highlights the key role played by the protocol operating frame size. Moreover, a comparison with the performance of a simpler slotted ALOHA strategy highlights a remarkable potential for modern random access schemes in terms of information freshness.

Index Terms—age of information, grant-free access, irregular repetition slotted ALOHA, IoT, machine-type communications

# I. INTRODUCTION

The relentless rise of the internet of things (IoT) is calling for novel design approaches at all layers of the protocol stack [2], [3]. In particular, the need to provide connectivity to the vast population of low-power, low-complexity devices encountered in massive machine-type communications (mMTC) is leading to profound evolutions of medium access protocols [4]. These setups are indeed characterised by transmissions of short packets in a sporadic and unpredictable fashion, which render traditional grant-based policies highly inefficient due to the excessive cost of overhead. To effectively support such use cases, grant-free access protocols are instead gaining traction. Based on variations of the well-known ALOHA [5], these approaches represent the de-facto choice for IoT in commercial solutions (e.g., LoRa [6], SigFox [7]) and standards (e.g., NB-IoT, LTE-M [8]).

In parallel to this, the need to support massive sets of devices has led to the development of a new family of protocols,

often labelled as modern random access [9]. These schemes combine novel protocol design ideas with advanced physical layer techniques to go beyond the interference bottleneck of ALOHA. Following different strategies, see, e.g. [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], they have been proven to be competitive to grant-based solutions in terms of spectral efficiency, without the need for channel negotiation procedures. A relevant example is offered by irregular repetition slotted ALOHA (IRSA) [1], which foresees nodes to independently transmit multiple copies of their packets over a frame of predefined length, and resorts to successive successive interference cancellation (SIC) to recover information at the receiver side. Thanks to its performance and to the good level of maturity it reached, the scheme became part of the ETSI DVB-RCS2 standard for return-link satellite communications [17], and is receiving attention as a promising solution in next-generation mMTC scenarios [3].

In parallel to the design of advanced grant-free protocols, the past few years have also witnessed a steadily growing interest towards the definition of novel performance metrics for IoT. Many scenarios, in fact, see the presence of a large number of devices which report time-stamped updates to a central gateway. This is the case, for instance, of remote sensing, industrial monitoring or asset tracking, with broad applications in several domains. In these settings, the main goal is often not to optimise spectral efficiency, but rather to provide the gateway with a fresh and up-to-date view of the status of each transmitting device, e.g. to drive decisions or feedback loops. These remarks have triggered a florid line of study, aiming at the design of transmission policies based on the semantic of information [18], [19], [20]. The pioneering concept in the field has been that of age of information (AoI), originally formalised in [21], [22]. The metric is defined assuming time-stamped messages of equal importance, and captures knowledge freshness at the receiver by measuring the time elapsed since the generation of the latest received update. Despite its simplicity, AoI pinpoints a fundamental performance tradeoff, as it depends both on the traffic generation pattern (frequent messages offering fresher information), and on the time spent by an update in the network (higher traffic leading to delivery of older information). As a result, the metric is intrinsically different from classical performance criteria like throughput, as well as from latency, which focuses on a single packet and only captures its network time. In view of its specificity, AoI has been studied

Interesting results are also emerging in IoT setups where multiple devices share the same channel to report to a common destination. Among these, optimal scheduling policies are derived in [25] considering packets of different durations to be delivered over noisy channels, whereas joint sampling and orthogonal transmission strategies are studied in [26] and in [27] under energy constraints. Distributed and low-complexity scheduling solutions are proposed in [28] limiting the negotiation overhead, and in [29] meeting throughput requirements, whereas [30] tackles the behaviour of AoI under orthogonal and non-orthogonal multiple access.

On the other hand, research has lately started to focus on models that deal with the additional degree of complexity introduced by having terminals that share the channel via grant-free protocols. Initial and key steps have been taken with the exact characterisation of the average AoI of slotted ALOHA (SA) [31], [32]. Both works reveal how maximising throughput also leads to the minimum AoI under symmetric channel conditions and for the classical destructive collision model. In the presence of feedback, important improvements were unleashed in [33], forcing devices to only send updates which would induce a relevant reduction to the AoI. Recent works have also started to look into fully asynchronous random access, deriving AoI metrics for pure ALOHA [34].

Despite the lively research on the field, however, the behaviour of modern random access from an AoI viewpoint is still unexplored, leaving an important gap. In fact, understanding whether solutions which are extremely promising in terms of spectral efficiency are also capable to provide good AoI performance represents a key point to gauge their suitability to a broader set of mMTC applications. Motivated by this, the present paper together with its conference version [35], takes a step to address this relevant question, and provides the first AoI study of an advanced grant-free solution. Specifically, we focus on IRSA [1], and consider a setting where a large number of devices send time-stamped status updates to a common destination. In this setup, we provide the following key contributions:

- resorting to a Markovian analysis, we track the AoI process, prove its ergodicity and derive a compact closed-form expression for its stationary distribution. Leaning on this, we derive the average network AoI;  
- we then study the behaviour of the age distribution tail, and derive an exact closed-form expression for the probability that the AoI exceeds a threshold. This age-violation probability metric provides fundamental insights in many practical IoT settings. This is the case, for instance of dynamic system control in an industrial environment, where decision-making based on stale information may have dire consequences;  
- the Markovian analysis is also applied to a SA-based system, leading to simple formulations for average AoI – in accordance with [31], [32] – and age-violation probability. A thorough comparison between the performance achievable with the different grant-free access schemes highlights strong gains offered by modern random access

# II. SYSTEM MODEL AND PRELIMINARIES

We focus on a system with  $n$  users sharing a wireless channel to attempt delivery of status updates towards a common receiver (sink), as illustrated in Fig. 1. Time is divided in slots of equal duration  $T_{s}$ , and all terminals are assumed to be synchronised to such pattern. Leaning on this, we measure all time-related quantities in (fraction of) slots, i.e. normalise them to  $T_{s}$ , and set without loss of generality  $T_{s} = 1$ .

At each time slot, every user may independently become active with probability  $\rho$ , generating a packet addressed to the sink and marked with a time-stamp that reports the start of the current slot. Such a model is representative of wireless networks in which devices are equipped with sensors to monitor physical quantities, and notify a gateway by sending updates containing a field that specifies the time at which a new measurement was taken. Notable instances of this scenario are given by industrial IoT applications as well as environmental

or agricultural MTC settings. A model generalisation to bursty traffic profiles will be presented in Sec. V.

We shall assume all messages to be equally important, and are interested in having a system that collects and maintains an up-to-date picture of the status of each device. To this aim, we keep track of the current age of information for user  $i$  at the sink, defined as

$$
\delta^ {(i)} (t) := t - \sigma^ {(i)} (t) \tag {1}
$$

where  $\sigma^{(i)}(t)$  denotes the time-stamp of the last received update from node  $i$  as of time  $t$ .<sup>2</sup> It is relevant to observe that the stochastic process  $\delta^{(i)}(t)$  is driven both by the activation pattern of the devices and by the ability to timely deliver updates offered by the employed medium access policy. Specifically, the current AoI for a node grows linearly with time, being reset only when a time-stamped packet is decoded. This leads to a typical sawtooth profile exemplified, e.g., in Fig. 2. For our study, we focus in particular on the average network AoI

$$
\Delta := \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \Delta^ {(i)} \tag {2}
$$

where  $\Delta^{(i)}$  is the time average of the current AoI of the  $i$ -th node, i.e.

$$
\Delta^ {(i)} := \lim  _ {t \rightarrow \infty} \frac {1}{t} \int_ {0} ^ {t} \delta^ {(i)} (\tau) d \tau . \tag {3}
$$

Moreover, we are interested in the long term behaviour of the process, and evaluate the probability for the current AoI of a user to exceed a threshold value, as will be formally introduced in Sec. III with the notion of age violation probability.

With an eye on mMTC applications, we compare the performance of two grant-free access protocols: SA – taken as a reference benchmark in view of its widespread application -, and IRSA. Following a common and well established modelling assumption for slotted systems, e.g. [5], [10], we regard collisions as destructive, so that the superposition of two or more concurrent transmissions over the same slot prevents decoding at the receiver. Conversely, a slot where a single packet is present always leads to successful decoding. For both schemes, no feedback nor retransmission policies are considered, so that a packet is transmitted at most once.<sup>3</sup> Finally, throughout our discussion we will also refer to the aggregate throughput S, defined as the average number of packets per slot decoded at the sink.

# A. Slotted ALOHA

Following SA, a node accesses the channel to send an update as soon as this is generated. Accordingly, at every slot the device successfully delivers a packet to the sink with probability

$$
\xi_ {\mathrm {s a}} := \rho (1 - \rho) ^ {\mathrm {n} - 1}. \tag {4}
$$

<sup>3</sup>We note that the lack of feedback is encountered in many mMTC applications, either to reduce downlink bandwidth consumption or to limit devices complexity and energy consumption.

# B. Irregular repetition slotted ALOHA

Under IRSA operations [1], access to the medium is organised in frames of duration  $m$  slots each. Users are frame-synchronous, and the first transmission opportunity for a new packet is granted only at the start of the next frame. With the considered traffic generation pattern, this implies that a node may become active more than once before being able to access the medium. Aiming to provide the sink with the most up-to-date information, we assume a device in such a situation to transmit the last generated update only, discarding all the others.<sup>4</sup>

An example of the protocol operations is presented and discussed in Fig. 3. As illustrated, a terminal that has become active at least once during an m-slot period will initiate a transmission at the start of the subsequent frame. In this case, the node sends  $\ell$  identical replicas of its update, uniformly placed at random over the m available slots. The number of copies is drawn from a probability distribution  $\{\Lambda_{\ell}\}$ , so that  $\ell$  replicas are sent with probability  $\Lambda_{\ell}$ . Each copy contains a pointer to the positions of its twins, specifying in which slots of the frame they were sent. At the sink side, the decoding process initiates once the whole frame has been buffered. Specifically, the receiver starts by looking for slots containing a single packet. For any such slot, the corresponding update is decoded. Furthermore, using the pointers contained in the packet, the receiver performs interference cancellation, removing from the incoming waveform the contribution of all replicas of the decoded update. This may in turn lead to more singleton - and decodable - slots (see Fig. 3b for an example). The SIC procedure is iterated until all users have been retrieved or no more slots with a single packet can be found. For further details, we refer the reader to [1].

For the considered traffic generation profile, the number of users that transmit over a frame follows a binomial r.v. of parameters  $(n, 1 - (1 - \rho)^{m})$ . Accordingly, the channel load  $G$ , defined as the average number of devices accessing the channel per slot, is

$$
\mathsf {G} = \frac {\mathsf {n} [ 1 - (1 - \rho) ^ {\mathsf {m}} ]}{\mathsf {m}}
$$

and the aggregate throughput can simply be expressed as

$$
\mathsf {S} = \mathsf {G} (1 - \mathsf {P} _ {l})
$$

where  $\mathsf{P}_l$  denotes the packet loss rate (PLR), i.e. the probability for a packet not to be decoded.

The computation of  $\mathsf{P}_l$  would in turn require to track the evolution of the SIC process over the finite-length time horizon of a frame, which is a particularly complex task. Due to this, an exact PLR formulation for IRSA has proven elusive to date despite numerous research efforts [36], [37]. To characterise the performance of the scheme we then resort to an analytical approximation of  $\mathsf{P}_l$ , combining two recent results. The first, introduced in [36], captures the behaviour of the protocol in the error floor region, i.e. for lightly loaded channels. The

second [37], instead, offers a performance estimate in the waterfall region, i.e. for medium to high levels of congestion, adapting the finite length scaling analysis of low-density parity check codes. In both cases, a closed form approximation of the packet loss rate is offered as a function of the channel load, which we denote as  $\mathsf{P}_{l,ef}(\mathsf{G})$  (error-floor region) and  $\mathsf{P}_{l,wf}(\mathsf{G})$  (waterfall region), respectively. The rather involved expressions are given for completeness in App. B, and we refer the reader to the original papers for further details. More interestingly, we report in Fig. 4 both  $\mathsf{P}_{l,ef}(\mathsf{G})$  (dash-dotted line) and  $\mathsf{P}_{l,wf}(\mathsf{G})$  (dotted line) for  $\Lambda(x) = x^3$  and  $\mathfrak{m} = 500$  slots, together with the results of Montecarlo simulations (markers). From the plot, it is evident that each expression provides a very good estimate of  $\mathsf{P}_l$  in one channel load region, and has very little effect on the other one. Leaning on this observation we then approximate the packet loss rate of IRSA for a generic channel load as

$$
\mathrm {P} _ {l} (\mathsf {G}) \simeq \mathrm {P} _ {l, e f} (\mathsf {G}) + \mathrm {P} _ {l, w f} (\mathsf {G}). \tag {5}
$$

Note in fact that, for low values of  $\mathsf{G} \mathsf{P}_{l,wf}$  adds a negligible contribution to the sum, whereas it dominates over  $\mathsf{P}_{l,ef}$  for larger channel loads. The good tightness of the approach is confirmed for different frame durations in Fig. 4, where (5) is shown by solid lines. The plot also highlights a well-known behaviour of IRSA, which will be useful to interpret some results in the remainder of the article. We can in fact observe how operating the protocol over longer frames triggers better performance, lowering the error floor and shifting the threshold for entering the waterfall region to higher channel loads [36], [37].

Let us now focus on the AoI evolution experienced by a node when IRSA is employed (see, e.g., Fig. 2b). Due to the operation mode of the protocol,  $\delta^{(i)}(t)$  grows linearly over a frame, and is reset at the end of it if an update was transmitted and successfully received at the sink, i.e. with probability

$$
\xi := \left[ 1 - (1 - \rho) ^ {\mathfrak {m}} \right] \cdot (1 - \mathrm {P} _ {l}) = \frac {\mathrm {m} S}{\mathrm {n}}. \tag {6}
$$

In such a case, the current AoI is refreshed to a value in the

Indeed, at time of reception, m slots have elapsed for the transmission process, which add up to the initial offset due to the slots between the generation of the packet and the opportunity for the node to access the channel at the start of the subsequent frame. Throughout our discussion, we describe the latter quantity via the discrete r.v.  $B$ , for which an example is given in Fig. 2b. Recalling the considered traffic generation model,  $B$  takes values in  $\{1,\ldots ,\mathfrak{m}\}$  and has probability mass function (PMF)

p _ {B} (b) = \frac {\rho (1 - \rho) ^ {b - 1}}{1 - (1 - \rho) ^ {\mathfrak {m}}}. \tag {7}

For instance, assume that  $B = \mathfrak{m}$ . The probability for this to happen is exactly  $\rho (1 - \rho)^{\mathfrak{m} - 1}$ . On the other hand, the denominator in (7) imposes the normalisation condition that at least one packet has to be generated in the frame for the node to access the channel.

# C. Notation

When analysing IRSA operated over frames of  $\mathfrak{m}$  slots, we shall decompose an integer value  $n \in \mathbb{N}_0$  as

$$
n = \alpha_ {n} + \mathfrak {m} \beta_ {n}
$$

$$
\alpha_ {n} := \operatorname {m o d} (n, \mathfrak {m}), \quad \beta_ {n} := \lfloor n / \mathfrak {m} \rfloor \tag {8}
$$

In other words, we express a generic time slot index  $n$  as the sum of  $\beta_{n}$  frames and of  $\alpha_{n}$  additional slots.

Moreover, we indicate by  $\{\pi_x\}$ ,  $x \in \mathbb{N}$ , the stationary distribution of a discrete-time, discrete-valued Markov process  $X_{n}$ , when it exists. In this case, we refer as the steady-state version of the process to a r.v.  $X$  with PMF  $\{\pi_x\}$ .

# III. A MARKOVIAN AOI ANALYSIS FOR IRSA AND SA

We start our study by considering the current AoI, introduced in (1), when the system is operated following an IRSA access policy. Without risk of confusion we drop the node

index  $i$ , and observe that the stochastic process  $\delta(t)$  grows linearly within each frame, as its value can only be refreshed at the end of the m-slot period in case of successful reception of a time-stamped update. Accordingly, we can conveniently write

$$
\delta (t) = \delta (\mathfrak {m} \cdot \lfloor t / \mathfrak {m} \rfloor) + \operatorname {m o d} (t, \mathfrak {m}) \tag {9}
$$

where the first addend captures the age at the beginning of the current frame, while the second accounts for the additional offset from the start of the frame up to the observation time. Without loss of generality, we start tracking the process (i.e., set  $t = 0$ ) right after the reception of the first update. Recalling the model introduced in Sec. II, the initial state of our system is thus in the set  $\{m + 1, \ldots, 2m\}$ , and  $\delta(t) \geq m + 1$ ,  $\forall t$ . Starting from these remarks, the evolution of the current age is completely specified by tracking the discrete time, discrete valued<sup>7</sup> process

$$
\Omega_ {k} := \delta (k m) - (m + 1), \quad k \in \mathbb {N} _ {0} \tag {10}
$$

which describes the offset of  $\delta(t)$  at the beginning of the  $k$ -th frame with respect to its minimum possible value  $\mathfrak{m} + 1$  (see Fig. 2b).

Moreover, since each node operates independently over successive frames,  $\Omega_{k}$  is Markovian, and can be described by a chain with state space  $\mathbb{N}_0$ . For such process, the one-step transition probabilities

$$
q _ {(i, j)} := \mathbb {P} \left\{\Omega_ {k + 1} = j \mid \Omega_ {k} = i \right\}
$$

can readily be computed noting that each frame sees a binary outcome. Specifically:

- no update is delivered with probability  $1 - \xi$ . In such condition the chain simply moves forward from state  $i$  to state  $j = i + m$ , tracking the linear increase of the AoI over the frame  
- on the other hand, a status update for the node is retrieved with probability  $\xi$ , given in (6). In this case, the new AoI value at the start of frame  $k + 1$  will be the sum of the m slots of frame  $k$  (needed to deliver the message with IRSA) and the additional slots elapsed between the generation of the update and the start of its transmission process, described by the r.v.  $B$  introduced in Sec. II-B. Accordingly, the Markov chain will transition from state  $i$  to state  $j = B - 1$ ,  $j \in \{0, \dots, m - 1\}$ . For instance, if the successful message originated at the beginning of frame  $k - 1$ , i.e.,  $B = m$ , the new age value at the start of frame  $k + 1$  will be  $2m$ , so that the tracked process will be  $m - 1$  slots above its minimum value. Similarly, if the device became active at the last slot of frame  $k - 1$ , we have  $B = 1$ , and the AoI will be reset to its minimum value  $m + 1$ , so that  $\Omega_{k + 1} = 0$ .

Leaning on the PMF of the r.v  $B$  given in (7), we can then write the transition probabilities for any state  $i \in \mathbb{N}_0$  as

$$
q _ {(i, j)} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \xi \cdot p _ {B} (j + 1) & j \in \{0, \dots , m - 1 \} \\ 1 - \xi & j = i + m \\ 0 & \text {o t h e r w i s e} \end{array} . \right. \tag {11}
$$

<sup>7</sup>We remark that, while the process  $\delta(t)$  takes values in  $\mathbb{R}^+$ , the current AoI at the start of a frame can only be equal to an integer number of slots, by virtue of the slotted operation of the protocol described in Sec. II-B.

The formulation in (11) allows to compute the evolution of  $\Omega_{k}$  at any point in time, given the initial condition of the system. In general, however, we are more interested in evaluating the long run behaviour of the current age, which we characterise via following result:

Proposition 1: The stochastic process  $\Omega_{k}$  is ergodic, and has steady state distribution

$$
\pi_ {\omega} = \frac {\mathrm {m S}}{\mathrm {n}} \left(1 - \frac {\mathrm {m S}}{\mathrm {n}}\right) ^ {\beta_ {\omega}} \cdot \frac {\rho (1 - \rho) ^ {\alpha_ {\omega}}}{1 - (1 - \rho) ^ {\mathrm {m}}}, \quad \omega \in \mathbb {N} _ {0} \tag {12}
$$

where  $\alpha_{\omega}$  and  $\beta_{\omega}$  are defined as per (8).

Proof: We start by observing that the Markov chain is irreducible, i.e. for any state-pair  $(i,j)$ , there exists  $n > 0$  s.t. the  $n$ -step transition probability between the two states is strictly positive. If  $j \in \{0, \ldots, m-1\}$  the statement holds already at one step from (11). Otherwise, leaning on (8), let us express  $j = \alpha_j + m\beta_j$ . Then, the transition from  $i$  to  $j$  can take place in  $\beta_j + 1$  steps, moving to state  $\alpha_j < m$  with a successful update, and then experiencing  $\beta_j$  frames without managing to deliver a packet. The event has probability  $q_{(i,\alpha_j)} \cdot (1 - \xi)^{\beta_j} > 0$ , proving the irreducibility of the chain. Furthermore, observing that each state in  $\{0, \ldots, m-1\}$  has period 1, the chain is also aperiodic, and admits thus an asymptotic probability distribution  $\{\pi_\omega\}$ . To compute this, we can write the balance equations. Using the one-step transition probabilities in (11) we obtain:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \pi_ {\omega} = \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {\infty} \pi_ {j} \cdot \xi p _ {B} (\omega + 1) \stackrel {(a)} {=} \xi p _ {B} (\omega + 1) & \omega <   \mathfrak {m} \\ \pi_ {\omega} = \pi_ {\omega - \mathfrak {m}} \cdot (1 - \xi) & \omega \geq \mathfrak {m} \end{array} \right. \tag {13b}
$$

where (a) stems from the normalisation condition  $\sum_{\omega}\pi_{\omega} = 1$ . For the expressions in (13b), decomposing the state index as in (8) and applying a simple recursion eventually leads to  $\pi_{\omega} = \pi_{\alpha_{\omega}}\cdot (1 - \xi)^{\beta_{\omega}}$ , where  $\pi_{\alpha_{\omega}}$  is given by the corresponding equation in (13a). Combining these results, we have, for any  $\omega \in \mathbb{N}_0$

$$
\pi_ {\omega} = \xi (1 - \xi) ^ {\beta_ {\omega}} \cdot p _ {B} (\alpha_ {\omega} + 1). \tag {14}
$$

As the Markov chain admits a proper stationary distribution  $(\pi_{\omega} > 0)$ , the process  $\Omega_k$  is also positive recurrent, and thus ergodic. Plugging the expressions of  $\xi$  and  $p_B(b)$  into (14) eventually leads to the statement formulation in (12).

Notably, (12) offers a compact closed-form characterisation of the asymptotic behaviour of the instantaneous AoI of a node for any IRSA degree distribution  $\{\Lambda_{\ell}\}$ . Leaning on this general result, two quantities of particular interest for system design can be derived: average AoI and age-violation probability, which we characterise in the remainder of the section.

Remark 1: The derived formulation captures the behaviour of a broader class of traffic generation patterns. Indeed, (14) only requires that i) a node successfully delivers a new update independently over each frame with probability  $\xi$ ; ii) the offset (in slots) between the time stamp of the update and the start of the frame where it is transmitted follows a generic PMF  $p_B(b)$ . For example, assume that a node becomes active over a frame with probability  $p_a$ , and that the time stamp of the

generated update is uniformly distributed. In this case, the packet is delivered with probability  $\xi = p_a(1 - \mathsf{P}_l)$ , and  $p_B(b) = 1 / \mathfrak{m}$ ,  $\forall b\in \{1,\ldots ,\mathfrak{m}\}$ . Plugging these into (14) we readily get  $\pi_{\omega} = \xi (1 - \xi)^{\beta_{\omega}} / \mathfrak{m} = (\mathsf{S} / \mathfrak{n})\cdot (1 - \mathsf{mS} / \mathfrak{n})^{\beta_{\omega}}$ . While the formulation in (12) is thus specific to the traffic model described in Sec. II, the presented approach is more general and can easily be adapted to other profiles.

# A. IRSA average AoI

A first relevant performance metric is the average AoI, formally defined in (2), for which we provide the following result.

Proposition 2: The average network AoI of IRSA, measured in slots and under the traffic model of Sec. II, is given by

$$
\Delta_ {\text {i r s a}} = \frac {\mathrm {m}}{2} + \frac {\mathrm {n}}{\mathrm {S}} + \left(\frac {1}{\rho} - \frac {\mathrm {m} (1 - \rho) ^ {\mathrm {m}}}{1 - (1 - \rho) ^ {\mathrm {m}}}\right). \tag {15}
$$

Proof: Let us focus on a generic node  $i$ , for which we aim at computing the time average introduced in (3), and express the observation interval as  $t = k\mathfrak{m} + \mathrm{mod}(t,\mathfrak{m})$ , i.e.  $k$  entire frames plus a portion of the  $(k + 1)$ -th m-slot period. Asymptotically, the contribution of the second addend becomes negligible, so that

$$
\begin{array}{l} \Delta^ {(i)} = \lim  _ {k \rightarrow \infty} \frac {1}{k m} \int_ {0} ^ {k m} \delta^ {(i)} (\tau) d \tau \\ = \lim  _ {k \rightarrow \infty} \frac {1}{k m} \sum_ {\ell = 0} ^ {k - 1} \int_ {\ell m} ^ {(\ell + 1) m} (m + 1 + \Omega_ {\ell} + \tau) d \tau . \\ \end{array}
$$

Here, the second equality follows by: i) expressing the integral as the sum of its components over the  $k$  observed frames, and ii) writing the current AoI at time  $\tau$  within the  $\ell$ -th frame as  $\delta^{(i)}(\tau) = \Omega_{\ell} + \mathsf{m} + 1 + \tau$  following (9), (10). After simple manipulations:

$$
\Delta^ {(i)} = 1 + \frac {3 m}{2} + \lim  _ {k \rightarrow \infty} \frac {1}{k} \sum_ {\ell = 0} ^ {k - 1} \Omega_ {\ell} \stackrel {(a)} {=} 1 + \frac {3 m}{2} + \sum_ {\omega = 0} ^ {\infty} \omega \pi_ {\omega} \tag {16}
$$

where (a) follows with probability 1 from the ergodicity of the Markov chain proven in Prop. 1 [38]. The average AoI for the node can then be computed by evaluating the expected value of a r.v. with PMF  $\{\pi_{\omega}\}$ . Notice that this result is fairly general, as it simply relates the calculation of the area below the curve  $\delta^{(i)}(t)$  to the stationary distribution of the process  $\Omega_k$ , describing the AoI at the start of a frame. Focusing for the moment on the specific traffic profile under consideration, the expression can be further developed using the formulation in (14). Expressing once more  $\omega \in \mathbb{N}_0$  as  $\omega = \alpha_{\omega} + \mathrm{m}\beta_{\omega}$ , we have

$$
\begin{array}{l} \sum_ {\omega = 0} ^ {\infty} \omega \pi_ {\omega} = \sum_ {\beta_ {\omega} = 0} ^ {\infty} \sum_ {\alpha_ {\omega} = 0} ^ {m - 1} \left(\alpha_ {\omega} + m \beta_ {\omega}\right) \xi (1 - \xi) ^ {\beta_ {\omega}} p _ {B} (\alpha_ {\omega} + 1) \\ = \mathfrak {m} \sum_ {\beta_ {\omega} = 0} ^ {\infty} \beta_ {\omega} \xi (1 - \xi) ^ {\beta_ {\omega}} + \sum_ {\alpha_ {\omega} = 0} ^ {\mathfrak {m} - 1} \alpha_ {\omega} p _ {B} (\alpha_ {\omega} + 1). \\ \end{array}
$$

If we now observe that

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathsf {m} \sum_ {\beta_ {\omega} = 0} ^ {\infty} \beta_ {\omega} \xi (1 - \xi) ^ {\beta_ {\omega}} = \frac {\mathsf {m} (1 - \xi)}{\xi} = \frac {\mathsf {n}}{\mathsf {S}} - \mathsf {m} \\ \sum_ {\alpha \omega = 0} ^ {m - 1} \alpha_ {\omega} p _ {B} (\alpha_ {\omega} + 1) = \frac {1}{\rho} - 1 - \frac {m (1 - \rho) ^ {m}}{1 - (1 - \rho) ^ {m}} \\ \end{array}
$$

and plug these values into (16), the right hand-side of the proposition statement follows. Recalling that all nodes in the system operate independently, we have

$$
\Delta_ {\mathrm {i r s a}} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \Delta^ {(i)} = \Delta^ {(i)}
$$

concluding the proof.

# B. IRSA age-violation probability

Aiming at an educated system design, the performance of an access scheme in terms of average AoI shall be complemented by a characterisation of the distribution tail. Consider, for example, industrial IoT settings in which feedback or actuation are triggered by the sink based on the latest available sensor measurements. In these scenarios, decisions that rely on stale information shall be avoided, and the worst-case behaviour of the system may be more relevant than its average performance. To explore this aspect, we rely on the age-violation probability, originally introduced in [39], and recently receiving increased research attention [40], [41]. Specifically, in our setup we are interested in evaluating the value assumed by the current AoI  $\delta(t)$  at the end of a generic frame  $k$ , just before a possible refresh takes this place. Let us denote this quantity as  $\delta_k'$ . Recalling that the age grows linearly within the frame, this can be expressed as the sum of the AoI value at the start of the frame, i.e. at time  $km$ , and of the  $m$  slots elapsed in the frame:  $\delta_k' = \delta(km) + m$ . Accordingly, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 1: The IRSA age violation probability,  $\zeta (\theta)$ , is the probability for the current AoI value at the end of a frame to exceed the threshold  $\theta$ , in stationary conditions. Formally,

$$
\zeta (\theta) := \lim  _ {k \to \infty} \mathbb {P} \{\delta_ {k} ^ {\prime} > \theta \}.
$$

Recalling that  $\Omega_{k}$  is defined as the offset of the current AoI at the start of the  $k$ -th frame with respect to its minimum possible value  $\mathfrak{m} + 1$ , we can conveniently write

$$
\zeta (\theta) = \mathbb {P} \left\{\Omega > \theta - 2 m - 1 \right\} \tag {17}
$$

where the r.v.  $\Omega$  with PMF corresponding to the asymptotic distribution of the process  $\Omega_{k}$ , i.e.,  $p_{\Omega}(\omega) = \pi_{\omega}$ , is introduced in accordance to Sec. II-C.

We remark that, thanks to the ergodicity of the Markov process,  $\zeta$  also corresponds to the fraction of the frames in

which the receiver experiences an AoI above a target value, offering thus and intuitive and useful design tool. Based on this definition, we prove the following result

Proposition 3: The age-violation probability  $\zeta (\theta)$  for IRSA under the traffic model of Sec. II is given by (18) reported at the bottom of the page, where the ancillary quantities  $\alpha_{\theta -2m}$  and  $\beta_{\theta -2m}$  are defined as per (8).

Proof: Recalling that the current AoI for a device is never lower than  $m + 1$  slots, and that the value grows linearly over a frame, we readily have  $\zeta(\theta) = 1$  for  $\theta < 2m + 1$ . For larger values, let us introduce for compactness the auxiliary variable  $\theta' := \theta - 2m$ . From (17), we then aim to compute  $\mathbb{P}\{\Omega > \theta'\} = \sum_{\omega > \theta'} \pi_{\omega}$ . Leaning on (8), let us now write  $\theta' = \alpha_{\theta'} + m\beta_{\theta'}$ , i.e. express  $\theta'$  as the sum of  $\beta_{\theta'}$  frames and  $\alpha_{\theta'}$  additional slots. Similarly, we express  $\omega = \alpha_{\omega} + m\beta_{\omega}$ . Using this decomposition, we observe that  $\omega > \theta'$  if either: i)  $\omega$  contains a larger number of frames than  $\theta'$  (i.e.,  $\beta_{\omega} > \beta_{\theta'}$ ), or ii)  $\beta_{\omega} = \beta_{\theta'}$  and  $\alpha_{\omega} > \alpha_{\theta'}$ . We can then write

$$
\zeta (\theta) = \sum_ {\omega = \theta^ {\prime}} ^ {\infty} \pi_ {\omega} = \sum_ {\ell = 1 + \alpha_ {\theta^ {\prime}}} ^ {m - 1} \pi_ {\ell + m \beta_ {\theta^ {\prime}}} + \sum_ {j = 1 + \beta_ {\theta^ {\prime}}} ^ {\infty} \sum_ {\ell = 0} ^ {m - 1} \pi_ {\ell + m j}
$$

where the second equality decomposes the original summation to account for conditions i) and ii). Plugging in the formulations of (14) and (7),

$$
\zeta (\theta) = \xi (1 - \xi) ^ {\beta_ {\theta^ {\prime}}} \cdot \sum_ {\ell = 1 + \alpha_ {\theta^ {\prime}}} ^ {m - 1} \frac {\rho (1 - \rho) ^ {\ell}}{1 - (1 - \rho) ^ {m}} + (1 - \xi) ^ {\beta_ {\theta^ {\prime}} + 1}
$$

observing that  $\sum_{\ell = 1}^{m - 1}p_B(\ell) = 1$  and recalling that  $\sum_{j = 1 + \beta_{\theta '}}^{\infty}(1 - \xi)^{j} = (1 - \xi)^{\theta '} / \xi$ . Solving the remaining summation and substituting the value of  $\xi$  derived in (6) leads to (18).

Before moving to an in-depth discussion of the performance achieved by IRSA, two observations are in order. First, it is relevant to stress that the presented framework offers exact closed-form expressions of both average AoI and age-violation probability. On the other hand, these quantities are a function of S, for which, as discussed, no compact characterisation is known to date. In the remainder of our study we will then evaluate the performance of the protocol resorting to the tight analytical approximation in (5), yet note how the results can be applied to any other (approximated or – if available – exact) IRSA throughput formulation.

Secondly, the analysis that we propose goes beyond the specific protocol operation of IRSA, and can capture the performance of a broader class of schemes. Indeed, equations (15) and (18) hold, under the traffic model of Sec. II, for any framed slotted ALOHA approach as long as the proper throughput characterisation is employed. From this standpoint, for instance, the framework also applies to the coded slotted ALOHA family [10], as well as to T-fold SA [42].

$$
\zeta (\theta) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \frac {\mathrm {m S}}{\mathrm {n}} \left(1 - \frac {\mathrm {m S}}{\mathrm {n}}\right) ^ {\beta_ {\theta - 2 \mathrm {m}}} \cdot \frac {(1 - \rho) ^ {1 + \alpha_ {\theta - 2 \mathrm {m}}} - (1 - \rho) ^ {\mathrm {m}}}{1 - (1 - \rho) ^ {\mathrm {m}}} + \left(1 - \frac {\mathrm {m S}}{\mathrm {n}}\right) ^ {1 + \beta_ {\theta - 2 \mathrm {m}}} & \theta > 2 \mathrm {m} \\ 1 & \text {o t h e r w i s e} \end{array} \right. \tag {18}
$$

# C. Reference Benchmark: slotted ALOHA

To better gauge the behaviour of IRSA, we complement our analysis by deriving the AoI statistics for a SA policy. In this case, the communication process is not bound to a framed structure, and an update is transmitted as soon as it is generated. For a generic time instant  $t$  within the  $k$ -th slot, i.e.  $t = k + \mathrm{mod}(t,k)$ , the current age for a node can be expressed as

$$
\delta_ {\mathbf {s a}} (t) = \delta_ {\mathbf {s a}} (k) + \operatorname {m o d} (t, k).
$$

Therefore, the AoI evolution can be completely specified by characterising the discrete-time, discrete-valued stochastic process

$$
\Psi_ {k} := \delta_ {\mathbf {s a}} (k) - 1, \quad k \in \mathbb {N} _ {0} \tag {19}
$$

which tracks the offset of the AoI at the start of slot  $k$  with respect to the minimum possible value 1 (see Fig. 2a). Also in this case, the process is Markovian, and, recalling (4), from any state  $j$  the chain may either transition to state 0 with probability  $\xi_{\mathrm{sa}}$ , or to state  $j + 1$  with probability  $1 - \xi_{\mathrm{sa}}$ . Following a reasoning similar to the one of Prop. 1, the process can readily be shown to be ergodic, and with stationary distribution  $\pi_{\psi} = \xi_{\mathrm{sa}}(1 - \xi_{\mathrm{sa}})^k$ ,  $k \in \mathbb{N}_0$ .

As done for IRSA, we can furthermore study the tail of the age distribution by introducing

Definition 2: The SA age-violation probability,  $\zeta_{\mathrm{sa}}(\theta)$ , is the probability that the steady-state version of the current AoI for a user exceeds a threshold  $\theta$ , or, equivalently, the fraction of slots that experience an age above  $\theta$  asymptotically. From (19), we have

$$
\zeta_ {\mathrm {s a}} (\theta) = \mathbb {P} \left\{\Psi > \theta - 2 \right\}
$$

where  $\Psi$  is again introduced for ease of notation as a r.v. having PMF  $p_{\Psi}(\psi) = \pi_{\psi}$ .

Accordingly, we can summarise the behaviour of the protocol with a simple result:

Proposition 4: For a SA system, under the traffic model of Sec. II, the average network AoI - expressed in slots - and the age violation probability are given by

$$
\Delta_ {\mathrm {s a}} = \frac {1}{2} + \frac {\mathrm {n}}{S _ {\mathrm {s a}}}, \quad \zeta_ {\mathrm {s a}} (\theta) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} (1 - \xi_ {\mathrm {s a}}) ^ {\theta - 1} & \theta > 1 \\ 1 & \text {o t h e r w i s e} \end{array} \right. \tag {20}
$$

Proof: The average age can be obtained via simple calculations applying the same approach presented in Prop. 2, whereas the age violation immediately follows as the complementary CDF of the geometric r.v.  $\Psi$ .

The presented result for the average AoI is in agreement with the formulations originally presented in [31], [32], although obtained in our case from the statistics of the Markov chain. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first characterisation of the age violation probability in a SA setup.

# V. AN EXTENSION OF THE FRAMEWORK TO DIFFERENT TRAFFIC PROFILES

As highlighted Sec. III, the analysis developed for IRSA relies on the assumption that the activation pattern of a node is i.i.d. over successive frames. We now relax this hypothesis, and show how the framework can be applied to a broader set of conditions. Specifically, we will lean on the general expression derived in (16), and derive the average AoI for bursty traffic.

Let us start by defining a more general update generation pattern. We assume that a node can be in one of two states: active  $(\mathcal{A})$  or silent  $(\mathcal{S})$ . Transitions between the two states can take place at the end of each frame. When silent, the node does not have any packet to deliver. Conversely, in active state the device transmits one status update over the frame. For simplicity, we assume that the update was generated right before the start of the frame where it is transmitted (i.e.,  $B = 1$ ).<sup>12</sup> We denote the probability of transition from silent

![](images/77ba9414bcef3b1d8b47bc22bf485f2a02fc0610291c732db06b924ec901329f.jpg)  
Fig. 11. Age of information value for which a target age-violation probability  $(10^{-1}$  and  $10^{-3})$  is reached for SA and IRSA, reported against  $\mathsf{n}\rho$ . In all cases,  $\mathsf{n} = 4000$ .

to active as  $\lambda$ , and the probability of transition from active to silent as  $\sigma$ . The transmission pattern of a node is then determined by a simple two-state Markov chain. For such a system, it is easy to verify that the stationary probabilities of being in active or silent state are given by  $p_{A} = \lambda / (\lambda + \sigma)$  and  $p_{S} = \sigma / (\lambda + \sigma)$ , respectively. The considered model is of particular interest for several reasons:

- first, it describes well the behaviour of many IoT systems of practical relevance. Indeed, in several applications nodes may monitor a physical quantity of interest, and trigger an update transmission only if an event is detected (e.g., exceeding a threshold). In these cases, periods of silence may be followed by a burst of transmissions once the node becomes active, as captured by the two-state Markov chain;  
- the possibility to transmit with persistency  $(1 - \sigma)$  over successive frames may also be representative of a simple link layer strategy to attempt reducing AoI. Assume for instance that no feedback from the sink is provided, as typical in many IoT scenarios. In this case, a device that wants to report to the destination may decide to generate and transmit new updates over a sequence of successive frames, in the hope that at least one of the packets is decoded at the sink. This repetition-based approach, followed for example by the Sigfox solution for IoT systems [7], triggers however a trade-off. In fact, the increased number of transmission may lead to higher channel congestion and thus reduce the success probability, with detrimental effect on AoI. As will be discussed later, the considered model allows to evaluate the effectiveness of the strategy;  
- finally, the two-state model represents an extension of the system model described in Sec II. Indeed, setting  $\lambda = p_a$  and  $\sigma = 1 - p_a$  leads back to a system in which the transmission pattern of a node is i.i.d. across frames with probability  $p_a$ . The results discussed in the first part of the paper can thus be seen as obtained for a specific configuration of the traffic profile being described.

To study the behaviour of the system, we start again by looking at the evolution of the AoI measured at the beginning of an IRSA frame. More precisely, we are interested in deriving the stationary distribution of the stochastic process  $\Omega_{k}$ , which, we

recall, is the offset of the current AoI at the start of frame  $k$  with respect to its minimum possible value  $\mathfrak{m} + 1$  (see also Fig. 2b). To achieve this goal, we now need to jointly track the evolution of  $\Omega_{k}$  and of the activity of the source  $s_k \in \{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{S}\}$ , since the probability of sending an update over successive frames is no longer independent. We therefore look at the Markov chain with state  $(\Omega_k,s_k)$ , whose one-step transition probabilities can easily be derived:

- from state  $(0, S)$  the chain can transition to state  $(\mathfrak{m}, \mathcal{A})$  with probability  $\lambda$ , or to state  $(\mathfrak{m}, S)$  with probability  $1 - \lambda$ . In fact, if the node is silent, no update will be transmitted, and the AoI will linearly grow over the frame, increasing by  $\mathfrak{m}$  slots;  
- similarly, from state  $(\ell \mathsf{m},\mathcal{S})$ $\ell >0$  , the chain can transition either to state  $((\ell +1)\mathsf{m},\mathcal{A})$  , with probability  $\lambda$  , or to state  $((\ell +1)\mathsf{m},\mathcal{S})$  , with probability  $1 - \lambda$  
- finally, from state  $(\ell, \mathcal{A})$ ,  $\ell \geq 0$ , the chain will move to state  $(0, \mathcal{A})$  with probability  $(1 - \mathsf{P}_l)(1 - \sigma)$ . This happens when the sent update is successfully received, factor  $(1 - \mathsf{P}_l)$ , and the node remains active, factor  $(1 - \sigma)$ . Along the same line, the chain transitions to state  $(0, \mathcal{S})$  with probability  $(1 - \mathsf{P}_l)\sigma$ . If, instead, the transmitted update is lost, the chain moves to state  $((\ell + 1)\mathsf{m}, \mathcal{A})$  with probability  $\mathsf{P}_l(1 - \sigma)$  or to state  $((\ell + 1)\mathsf{m}, \mathcal{S})$  with probability  $\mathsf{P}_l\sigma$ .

The Markov process is once more ergodic, and its stationary behaviour can be determined by writing the balance equations. Denoting as  $\pi_{\ell}^{A}$  and  $\pi_{\ell}^{S}$  the stationary probabilities of being in state  $(\ell \mathfrak{m},\mathcal{A})$  and  $(\ell \mathfrak{m},\mathcal{S})$ ,  $\ell \geq 0$ , respectively, and recalling the described transition probabilities, we have

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l} \pi_ {0} ^ {\mathcal {S}} = \sum_ {\ell = 0} ^ {\infty} \pi_ {\ell} ^ {\mathcal {A}} (1 - \mathrm {P} _ {l}) \sigma \stackrel {(a)} {=} p _ {\mathcal {A}} \cdot (1 - \mathrm {P} _ {l}) \sigma \\ \pi_ {0} ^ {\mathcal {A}} = \sum_ {\ell = 0} ^ {\infty} \pi_ {\ell} ^ {\mathcal {A}} (1 - \mathrm {P} _ {l}) (1 - \sigma) \stackrel {(b)} {=} p _ {\mathcal {A}} \cdot (1 - \mathrm {P} _ {l}) (1 - \sigma) \\ \pi_ {\ell} ^ {\mathcal {S}} = \pi_ {\ell - 1} ^ {\mathcal {S}} \cdot (1 - \lambda) + \pi_ {\ell - 1} ^ {\mathcal {A}} \cdot \mathrm {P} _ {l} \sigma \quad \ell > 0 \\ \pi_ {\ell} ^ {\mathcal {A}} = \pi_ {\ell - 1} ^ {\mathcal {S}} \cdot \lambda + \pi_ {\ell - 1} ^ {\mathcal {A}} \cdot \mathrm {P} _ {l} (1 - \sigma) \quad \ell > 0 \end{array} \right. \tag {22}
$$

Here, (a) and (b) follow from the fact that  $\sum_{\ell} \pi_{\ell}^{A}$  gives the stationary probability of being in active state,  $p_{A}$ , computed at the beginning of this section. In contrast to the derivation of Sec. III, a closed form solution for the system is unfortunately elusive. However, (22) readily leads to the matrix relation

$$
\begin{array}{l} \left( \begin{array}{c} \pi_ {\ell} ^ {\mathcal {S}} \\ \pi_ {\ell} ^ {\mathcal {A}} \end{array} \right) = \left( \begin{array}{c c} 1 - \lambda & \mathsf {P} _ {l}   \sigma \\ \lambda & \mathsf {P} _ {l} (1 - \sigma) \end{array} \right) ^ {\ell} \left( \begin{array}{c} \pi_ {0} ^ {\mathcal {S}} \\ \pi_ {0} ^ {\mathcal {A}} \end{array} \right) \\ = \binom {1 - \lambda} {\lambda} \left. \begin{array}{c} \mathrm {P} _ {l} \sigma \\ \mathrm {P} _ {l} (1 - \sigma) \end{array} \right) ^ {\ell} \binom {\sigma} {1 - \sigma} \frac {\lambda (1 - \mathrm {P} _ {l})}{\lambda + \sigma} \tag {23} \\ \end{array}
$$

which allows an easy numerical computation of  $\pi_{\ell}^{S}$  and  $\pi_{\ell}^{A}$  for any  $\ell \geq 0$ . Finally, the sought stationary distribution of  $\Omega_{k}$  can simply be obtained as  $\pi_{\ell} = \pi_{\ell}^{S} + \pi_{\ell}^{A}$ .

This result can conveniently be used within the analytical framework developed for IRSA in Sec. III. Let us focus, for instance, on the average AoI. As shown in the proof of Prop. 2,

Such a result offers a non-trivial design hint, suggesting how the drop of some generated updates may be beneficial in terms of AoI under congested channel conditions.

# VI. CONCLUSIONS

This paper offered the first analytical characterisation of a class of modern random access scheme from an information freshness standpoint. Specifically, focusing on the irregular repetition slotted ALOHA (IRSA) protocol, the evolution of the current AoI for a status update source has been tracked by means of a Markov chain, which was proven to be ergodic. Leaning on a closed-form expression for the stationary distribution of such process, simple exact expressions for the average AoI and for the age violation probability were discussed. The presented analysis revealed how IRSA can significantly outperform slotted ALOHA with respect to both metrics, and pinpointed some fundamental trade-offs that drive the behaviour of the protocol, capturing the impact of frame length and replica number distribution. The compact formulations introduced in the article offer a simple yet effective tool for proper system design, as illustrated in the numerical example provided in App. A.

# APPENDIX A

To illustrate how the derived framework can serve as a useful system design tool, consider the following example. Let us focus on an IoT setup where devices monitor a physical quantity, and report the measurements to a central unit over a shared channel. Transmissions are performed at a bitrate of 3.125 kbps, and the transmission of a packet takes  $T = 136$  ms (duration of a slot in our case). Such parameters are compatible with the LoRaWAN standard, considering a payload of 32 bytes, explicit header mode and a bandwidth of 125 kHz [43]. Assume furthermore that each device performs a new reading - and transmits a status update - on average every  $A = 10$  minutes. In this configuration, we are interested in evaluating how many devices can be supported so that the average AoI does not exceed the target value  $\Delta' = 10.5$  minutes. Note that this is a particularly demanding design. Indeed, we aim to keep an average AoI for each node which is very close to its average update generation rate (an ultimate lower bound), and want to do so having all devices share the channel in a fully uncoordinated fashion. For SA, the design aspect can be targeted by solving (20) with respect to  $n$ , to obtain

$$
\mathsf {n} _ {\mathsf {s a}} \leq \left\lfloor 1 - \frac {\ln \left(\rho \left(\Delta^ {\prime} / T - 1 / 2\right)\right)}{\ln (1 - \rho)} \right\rfloor = 2 1 5 [ \text {d e v i c e s} ]
$$

where  $\Delta^{\prime}$  has been normalised to the slot duration, and  $\rho = T / A$  is the activation probability per slot corresponding to the average generation rate  $A$ . Let us now focus on IRSA. First of all, we observe that the frame-based operations pose a constraint to the minimum average AoI that can be achieved, since longer frames also entail higher latency for delivering an update. The maximum frame size that may be used to support  $\Delta^{\prime}$  can immediately be derived from (15) and (6), considering the limiting condition of having a single device  $(n = 1)$  and assuming no packet loss. In this situation, the average AoI in (15) evaluates to  $\Delta = 3m / 2 + 1 / \rho$  slots. Solving with respect

to  $\mathfrak{m}$ , we obtain that the IRSA shall be operated with frames satisfying

$$
\mathfrak {m} \leq \left\lfloor \frac {2}{3} \left(\Delta^ {\prime} / T - 1 / \rho\right) \right\rfloor = 1 4 7 [ \text {s l o t s} ].
$$

Consider then a frame of size  $m = 100$  slots. Equation (15) can then easily be solved numerically with respect to  $n$  to obtain the maximum number of nodes that can be supported without exceeding the average age constraint, to obtain  $n_{\mathrm{irsa}} \leq 2600$  [devices], with a more than ten-fold improvement over the slotted ALOHA solution.
