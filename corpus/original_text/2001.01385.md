# Identifying and Compensating for Feature Deviation in Imbalanced Deep Learning

Han-Jia Ye, Hong-You Chen, De-Chuan Zhan, and Wei-Lun Chao

Abstract—Classifiers trained with class-imbalanced data are known to perform poorly on test data of the "minor" classes, of which we have insufficient training data. In this paper, we investigate learning a ConvNet classifier under such a scenario. We found that a ConvNet significantly over-fits the minor classes, which is quite opposite to traditional machine learning algorithms that often under-fit minor classes. We conducted a series of analysis and discovered the feature deviation phenomenon — the learned ConvNet generates deviated features between the training and test data of minor classes — which explains how over-fitting happens. To compensate for the effect of feature deviation which pushes test data toward low decision value regions, we propose to incorporate class-dependent temperatures (CDT) in training a ConvNet. CDT simulates feature deviation in the training phase, forcing the ConvNet to enlarge the decision values for minor-class data so that it can overcome real feature deviation in the test phase. We validate our approach on benchmark datasets and achieve promising performance. We hope that our insights can inspire new ways of thinking in resolving class-imbalanced deep learning.

Index Terms—class-imbalance learning, long-tailed distribution, visual recognition, deep learning, over-fitting

# 1 INTRODUCTION

Convolutional neural networks (ConvNets) have led to remarkable breakthroughs in visual recognition [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], thanks to the large amount of labeled instances available for each object class of interest [6], [7]. In practice, however, we frequently encounter training data with imbalanced class distributions. For example, real-world datasets often have the so-called long-tailed distribution: a few "major" classes claim most of the instances, while most of the other "minor" classes are represented by relatively fewer instances [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]. ConvNet classifiers trained with this kind of datasets using empirical risk minimization (e.g., stochastic gradient descent on uniformly sampled instances) have been found to perform poorly on minor classes [13], [14], [15].

One common explanation of this issue is the dominance of the major class data in training [19]. Concretely, a classifier trained to minimize the average per-instance loss tends to bias itself toward predicting the major classes [20], [21], [22], [23], [24]. As a result, the learned classifier would classify most of the test and even the training data of minor classes incorrectly. To resolve this problem, a variety of approaches have been proposed to scale up the influence of minor-class data during training; e.g., by re-sampling or re-weighting [13], [25], [20], [26], [27], [16], [28], [29], [24]. It is worth mentioning that both re-sampling and re-weighting have been widely used before the deep learning era [19], [21].

In this paper, we investigate another explanation — a ConvNet classifier trained with class-imbalanced data over-fits the minor class data. This is evidenced by the huge gap between

What intrigue us are the drastically different behaviors of a single ConvNet on test data of different classes. We note that, the problem of over-fitting has been widely studied in machine learning, but mostly from a holistic perspective (i.e., over all classes) or on scarce but class-balanced training data.

We posit that such a feature deviation phenomenon explains the over-fitting problem<sup>3</sup> and accounts for the poor accuracy of a ConvNet classifier on minor class data, and naively scaling up the influence of minor classes in training may worsen the problem. We note that, feature deviation is not commonly seen in traditional machine learning that uses pre-defined features. Thus, traditional algorithms are likely suffer a different problem in class-imbalanced learning, e.g., under-fitting to minor classes (Figure 1 (b)), for which scaling up the influence of minor classes can be effective.

We study further how feature deviation affects a ConvNet classifier in making predictions. We find that when feature deviation occurs (e.g., for class  $c$ ), the test instances of class  $c$  tend to move toward regions of lower decision values (i.e.,  $\boldsymbol{w}_c^\top f_\theta(\boldsymbol{x})$ ) compared to the training instances, making them hard to be classified correctly. See Figure 3 for an illustration.

To compensate for this effect, we propose to incorporate class-dependent temperatures (CDT) in training a ConvNet. The core idea is to simulate the effect of feature deviation in training — intentionally decreasing the decision values of the minor class data — to encourage the ConvNet classifier to learn to tackle it. Concretely, let  $a_{c}$  be the temperature of class  $c$ , which is set inversely proportionally to its number of training data (i.e., minor classes have larger temperatures), we divide the decision value  $\boldsymbol{w}_{c}^{\top}f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x})$  by  $a_{c}$  in training. This in turn forces the ConvNet to increase  $\boldsymbol{w}_{c}^{\top}f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x})$  by a factor of  $a_{c}$  in minimizing the training error. As a result, even if feature deviation occurs in testing, the test instances will have higher decision values than before (i.e., than learning without CDT), more likely leading to correct predictions. The contributions of this paper are four-folded.

- We conduct extensive analysis on class-imbalanced learning. Specifically, we analyze the ConvNet's over-fitting behavior to minor classes and identify the existence of feature deviation between the training and test instances.  
- We systematically investigate the reasons behind the poor performance of ConvNets and traditional machine learning methods on minor classes, and argue that the widely-applied re-weighting and re-sampling are not effective for ConvNets.  
- We propose an effective approach to compensate for the effect of feature deviation by incorporating class-dependent temperatures (CDT) in training. We emphasize that CDT do not reduce feature deviation, but encourage the ConvNet classifier to learn to deal with it.  
- We conduct extensive experiments on multiple benchmark datasets. CDT outperforms or is on a part with state-of-the-art approaches in most of the cases.

# 2 RELATED WORK

We review approaches on learning with class-imbalanced data. There are two mainstream approaches: re-sampling based and cost-sensitive based. Please see also [19], [39], [40], [41], [23], [13], [21] for surveys or comprehensive studies.

Re-sampling based methods change the training data distribution to match the test data[42], [13], [15]. Popular ways are to over-sample the minor-class instances [13], [25], [27] or under-sample the major-class instances [13], [21], [40], [43]. [44], [20], [45], [46], [47] proposed to synthesize more minor-class instances to enlarge the diversity of a class. More advanced methods learn to transfer statistics from the major to the minor classes [48], [14], [49], [31].

Cost-sensitive based methods adjust the cost of misclassifying an instance according to its true class. One common way is to give different instance weights (i.e., the so-called re-weighting) but still apply the same instance loss function. Setting the weights by (the square roots of) the reciprocal of the number of training instances per class has been widely used [28], [50], [29], [24], [51]. In [16], Cui et al. proposed a principled way to set weights by computing the effective numbers of training instances for each class. Several recent methods [52], [53], [54], [55], [56], [57], [58] explored dynamic adjustments of the weights along the learning process via meta-learning or curriculum learning.

Instead of adjusting the instance weights, Khan et al. [22] developed several instance loss functions that reflect class imbalance. Cao et al. [30] forced minor-class instances to have large margins from the decision boundaries. Khan et al. [59] proposed to incorporate uncertainty of instances or classes in the loss function. Ren et al. [58] and Menon et al. [60] modified the softmax loss function to accommodate the label distribution shift or calibrate the biased logits toward major classes. Both Li et al. [61] and Tan et al. [62] introduced new loss functions to balance the gradients assigned to the last fully-connected layers over classes.

Our CDT also adjusts instance loss functions, and we provide comparisons to closely related work in subsection 4.2.

Learning feature embeddings with class-imbalanced data has also been studied, especially for face recognition [63], [64], [28], [65], [66]. Several recent works [67], [68], [69] combined objective functions of classifier and embedding learning to better exploit the data of minor classes. Cui et al. [70], Zhang et al. [71], and Wang et al. [72] proposed two-stage training procedures to pre-train features with imbalanced data and fine-tune the classifier with balanced data. Kang et al. [32] proposed to decouple a ConvNet classifier by its feature extractor and the subsequent linear classifier, and systematically studied different training strategies for each component. Interestingly, they found that re-weighting and re-sampling might hurt the feature quality. Our analysis in section 3 provides insights to support their observations. Zhou et al. [73] introduced bilateral-branch networks to cumulatively transit between the two training stages.

Over-fitting and under-fitting. We are unaware of much recent literature that discusses if the learned classifiers underfit minor classes, which means the classifiers also perform poorly in training [74], [75], or over-fit minor classes [76]. Cao et al. [30] observed over-fitting when over-scaling up the influence of minor classes. We empirically show that under-fitting seems to be the main problem in traditional machine learning approaches; over-fitting seems to be prevalent in deep learning approaches trained end-to-end.

Empirical observations. Several works [77], [32], [49], [78] found that the learned linear classifiers of a ConvNet tend to have larger norms for the major classes, and proposed to force similar norms in training or calibrate the norms in testing. Wu et al. [68] found that the feature norms of major-class and minor-class instances are different and proposed to regularize it by forcing similar norms. Our work is inspired by empirical observations as well, but from a different perspective. We find that learning with class-imbalanced data leads to feature deviation between training and test instances of the same class, which explains the problem of over-fitting to minor classes. A recent work [78] also observed the discrepancy between training and test feature distributions, from the perspective of how they affect classification boundaries. Our work is conducted independently from theirs<sup>5</sup> and is distinct by a detailed analysis of feature deviation from the perspective of over-fitting and a different way, CDT, for imbalanced deep learning.

Domain adaptation. Domain adaptation [35], [34], [36], [79], [80] study the problems in which the training and test data come from different domains (e.g., synthetic vs. real images, or images captured at different weathers or geo-locations). Namely, there is an inherent domain shift even before a machine learning model is trained. In this paper, we follow the standard setup of class-imbalanced learning such that the training and test data come from the same domain (i.e., the same dataset). Thus, there is no domain shift. This can be seen from Figure 4 (c): when data are class-balanced (ERM-UB) or features are pre-defined (ERM-PD), there is no trend of deviation. The feature deviation is caused by training ConvNets end-to-end with class-imbalanced data.

# 3 THE FEATURE DEVIATION PHENOMON

We present an empirical study on over-fitting to minor classes and identify the feature deviation phenomenon and its effect.

# 3.1 Background and notation

We denote a ConvNet classifier by

$$
\hat {y} = \arg \max  _ {c \in \{1, \dots , C \}} \boldsymbol {w} _ {c} ^ {\top} f _ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} (\boldsymbol {x}), \tag {2}
$$

where  $\pmb{x}$  is the input,  $f_{\theta}(\cdot)$  is the ConvNet feature extractor parameterized by  $\pmb{\theta}$ , and  $w_{c}$  is the linear classifier of class  $c$ .

Given a training set  $D_{\mathrm{tr}} = \{(x_n, y_n)\}_{n=1}^N$ , in which each class  $c$  has  $N_c$  instances, we train the ConvNet classifier (i.e.,  $\pmb{\theta}$  and  $\{w_c\}_{c=1}^C$ ) by empirical risk minimization (ERM), using the cross entropy loss

$$
- \sum_ {n} \log p \left(y _ {n} \mid \boldsymbol {x} _ {n}\right) = - \sum_ {n} \log \left(\frac {\exp \left(\boldsymbol {w} _ {y _ {n}} ^ {\top} f _ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {n}\right)\right)}{\sum_ {c} \exp \left(\boldsymbol {w} _ {c} ^ {\top} f _ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {n}\right)\right)}\right). \tag {3}
$$

We apply stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with uniformly sampled instances from  $D_{\mathrm{tr}}$ .

# 3.2 Learning with class-imbalanced data

We follow [16] to construct a long-tailed class-imbalanced  $D_{\mathrm{tr}}$  from CIFAR-10 [17]: the (most) major class contains 5,000 training instances while the (most) minor class contains 50 training instances. Without loss of generality, let us re-index classes so that the training instances decrease from class  $c = 1$  to  $c = 10$ . See Figure 1 (a) for an illustration of the data size. We learn a ConvNet classifier using the standard ResNet-32 architecture and learning rates [1], following [30], [16]. See section 5 for more details on the setup.

Figure 4 (a-b, ERM curves) shows the per-class training and test accuracy. The training accuracy is high ( $\sim 100\%$ ) [33] for all classes. The test accuracy, however, drops drastically for the minor classes. The learned classifier over-fits minor class data. (For comparison, we train a classifier using the entire class-balanced CIFAR-10. See the ERM-UB curves.)

# 3.3 Analysis on the effect of feature deviation

We find that the learned linear classifiers have larger norms (i.e.,  $\| \pmb{w}_c\|_2$ ) for the major classes than the minor classes, which confirms the observations in [77], [32], [59], [49], [78]. The ConvNet classifier is thus biased to classify instances into major classes. This observation alone, however, cannot explain over-fitting. Specifically, with the biased classifier norms toward major classes, why can a ConvNet classifier still achieve  $\sim 100\%$  accuracy for minor-class training data, but not for test data?

Feature deviation. We hypothesize that there are differences in the features (i.e.,  $f_{\theta}(\pmb{x})$ ) between the training and test instances, especially for minor classes. To verify this, we compute the feature means of training and test data for each class  $c$ , and then calculate their Euclidean distance  $dis(c)$

$$
\begin{array}{l} d i s (c) = \\ \frac {1}{R} \sum_ {r = 1} ^ {R} \| \operatorname {m e a n} \left(S _ {K} \left(\left\{f _ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {\text {t r a i n}} ^ {(c)} \right\}\right)\right) - \operatorname {m e a n} \left(\left\{f _ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {\text {t e s t}} ^ {(c)} \right\}\right) \right\}\right) \| _ {2}. \tag {4} \\ \end{array}
$$

Here,  $\{f_{\theta}(\pmb{x}_{\mathrm{train}}^{(c)})\}$  and  $\{f_{\theta}(\pmb{x}_{\mathrm{test}}^{(c)})\}$  denote the sets of feature vectors extracted from the training and test data of class  $c$ .  $S_K(\cdot)$  is an operation that sub-samples  $K$  instances uniformly, for  $R$  rounds<sup>6</sup>. Figure 4 (c) shows the results:  $dis(c)$  goes large as the number of training instances decreases. In other words, the features  $f_{\theta}(\pmb{x})$  of the training and test instances are deviated (e.g., translated) for minor classes. See Figure 2 for the t-SNE [18] visualization of the features.

The feature deviation phenomenon offers an explanation to over-fitting to minor classes. Concretely, the linear classifiers  $\{\pmb{w}_c\}_{c=1}^C$  learned to separate the training instances among classes (almost perfectly) have little to do with separating the test instances for minor classes, since the training and test features of the same minor class are not occupying the same region of the feature space.

We set  $K$  to be the minor-class size and set  $R = 1,000$ .

Let  $N_{c}$  be the number of training instances of class  $c$ , we re-weight an instance of class  $c$  by  $N_{c}^{-0.5}$ , as suggested in [18]. We consider re-sampling so that every mini-batch has the same number of instances per class. Figure 4 shows the resulting training and test set accuracy and the distance between training and test feature means per class. Both methods do not reduce the feature mean distance (i.e., feature deviation) or even aggravate it. The test accuracy thus remains poor.

# 3.5 Traditional vs. end-to-end training

We have shown that ConvNet classifiers trained end-to-end with class-imbalanced data suffers feature deviation and over-fitting to minor classes. In traditional machine learning, features are usually pre-defined and are not learned from the training data at hand. In this scenario, will feature deviation and over-fitting still occur? To answer this, we investigate training a ConvNet classifier using another dataset (we use Tiny-ImageNet [38]). We apply the learned feature extractor  $f_{\theta}(\cdot)$  to the imbalanced CIFAR-10, for both training and test instances. We then train a logistic regression classifier using features of the training instances and apply it to the test instances. Figure 4 (ERM-PD) shows the results. We see no trend of feature deviation. The resulting classifier performs

poorly on the minor classes, not only in testing but also in training, essentially suffering under-fitting to minor classes.

We thus argue that, while learning with class-imbalanced data hurts the accuracy on minor classes for both traditional machine learning and recent deep learning, the underlying reasons are drastically different. Indeed, we show that reweighting is effective to alleviate under-fitting for logistic regression (in subsection 7.3) but not for ConvNet which suffers over-fitting. It is therefore desirable to develop solutions for a ConvNet classifier (or other deep classifiers) by incorporating the insights from feature deviation.

# 4 CLASS-DEPENDENT TEMPERATURES (CDT)

We present a novel approach to learn a ConvNet classifier with class-imbalanced data, inspired by our observations of feature deviation. Specifically, according to subsection 3.3, feature deviation makes the diagonal decision values of minor classes (see Figure 5 (a)) drop significantly from the training data to the test data. For test instances of some minor classes (e.g., class  $c = 9$  and  $c = 10$ ), the diagonal values are close to or even lower than some of the off-diagonal values, making it hard to classify them correctly.

One way to remedy this issue is to prevent feature deviation, which, however, appears difficult based on our earlier studies. We therefore take an alternative way, seeking to design a training objective that can compensate for the effect of feature deviation — i.e., compensate for the reduced diagonal decision values of minor classes in testing — so that the learned classifier can classify minor class data correctly.

To this end, we propose to make the training process aware of feature deviation, by simulating the effect of feature deviation directly on the training instances. Observing that feature deviation decreases decision values, we artificially reduce the decision values  $\boldsymbol{w}_c^\top f_\theta(\boldsymbol{x})$  of a training instance by a factor (temperature)  $a_c$ , resulting in a new objective

$$
- \sum_ {n} \log \left(\frac {\exp \left(\frac {\boldsymbol {w} _ {y _ {n}} ^ {\top} f _ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} (\boldsymbol {x} _ {n})}{a _ {y _ {n}}}\right)}{\sum_ {c} \exp \left(\frac {\boldsymbol {w} _ {c} ^ {\top} f _ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} (\boldsymbol {x} _ {n})}{a _ {c}}\right)}\right). \tag {5}
$$

We set  $a_{c}$  inversely proportionally to the number of training instances  $N_{c}$  — i.e., minor classes have larger  $a_{c}$  — reflecting the fact that minor classes suffer larger feature deviation. Training a ConvNet classifier to minimize Equation 5 therefore forces  $\boldsymbol{w}_{c}^{\top} f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x})$  to be enlarged by a factor of  $a_{c}$  for  $\boldsymbol{x}$  belonging to class  $c$ , so as to compensate for the effect of feature deviation that will occur on the test data.

Without loss of generality, we set  $a_{c} = \left(\frac{N_{\mathrm{max}}}{N_{c}}\right)^{\gamma}$ , in which  $\gamma \geq 0$  is a hyperparameter and  $N_{\mathrm{max}}$  is the largest number of training instances of a class in  $D_{\mathrm{tr}}$ . That is,  $a_{c} = 1$  for the (most) major class and  $a_{c} > 1$  for the other classes if  $\gamma > 0$ . When  $\gamma = 0$ , we recover the conventional ERM objective. We note that, it is quite common to set a class-dependent coefficient proportionally or inversely proportionally to  $N_{c}^{\gamma}$  [13], [25], [30], [28], [78], [29] (with a different  $\gamma$ ).

Testing. CDT simulates the effect of feature deviation on the training data, which encourages the ConvNet classifier to learn to deal with it by generating larger decision values

for minor class data. In the test phase, we apply the same classification rule as in Equation 2,

$$
\hat {y} = \arg \max  _ {c \in \{1, \dots , C \}} \boldsymbol {w} _ {c} ^ {\top} f _ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} (\boldsymbol {x}). \tag {6}
$$

Namely, we remove the factor  $a_{c}$ . This is because the test data themselves suffer real feature deviation, and hence there is no need to further simulate feature deviation on them.

# 4.1 Analysis on CDT

Figure 5 (b) shows the confusion matrices of applying our learned ConvNet classifier with CDT  $(\gamma = 0.3)$  to the training and test instances, using Equation 6. The experimental setup follows Figure 1. On the training data, minor classes now have larger diagonal decision values than major classes. On the test data, due to the unavoidable feature deviation, the diagonal decision values drop for minor classes in comparison to those on the training data. However, the diagonal values of all classes are much balanced than in Figure 5 (a). As a result, the test accuracy increases for not only minor classes but also all classes on average (see Table 1 and Figure 4).

# 4.2 Discussion and comparison to related work

Our approach shares similar influences to the classifier's decision values with [30], [58], [60]. However, our motivations are quite different — we conduct analysis to identify feature deviation and its effect and propose CDT to compensate for it. In contrast, [30] is proposed according to a margin-based generalization bound, [58] is proposed to accommodate the label distribution shift between training and testing, and [60] is proposed to overcome the undesirable bias towards dominant labels. Our ways of adjusting the decision values are also different from [30], [58], [60], as summarized in a recent comprehensive study [82]. We therefore believe that our insights and approach are complementary to them and are valuable add-on to the community<sup>9</sup>.

# 5.2 Implementation Details

ERM and class-dependent temperatures (CDT). For all the experiments, we use mini-batch stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with momentum  $= 0.9$  as the optimization solver. The softmax cross-entropy loss (Equation 3) is used for ERM and our CDT. The weight decay is  $2 \times 10^{-4}$ .

We also incorporate the Deferred Re-Weighting (DRW) [30] in the training phase, which is orthogonal to CDT and usually introduces further improvements. DRW turns to minimize a re-weighted objective after  $60\%$  training epochs, in which the minor-class instances have larger weights. We follow [30] to set the weights according to [16]. The weight for instances of class  $c$  is  $\frac{1 - \beta}{1 - \beta^{N_c}}$  with  $\beta = 0.9999$ .

Training details for imbalanced CIFAR. We use ResNet-32 [1] for all the CIFAR experiments, following [30], [16]. The batch size is 128. The initial learning rate is linearly warmed up to 0.1 in the first 5 epochs, and decays at the 160th and the 180th epochs by 0.01, respectively. The model is trained for 200 epochs by default. For some experiments with the step imbalance, we found that 200 epochs are not enough for the model to converge and we train for 300 epochs in total and adjust the learning rate schedule accordingly. We follow [30] to perform data augmentation. The  $32 \times 32$  CIFAR images are padded to  $40 \times 40$  and randomly flipped horizontally, and then are randomly cropped back to  $32 \times 32$ .

We also additionally consider the training protocol proposed in [58], where strong augmentations like RandAugment [83] are used in the training phase. In this case, larger batch size (512) and longer training epochs (500) are used.

We also decrease the learning rate from 0.2 with a cosine schedule. The improved training strategy leads to higher performance for both ERM and CDT.

Training details for imbalanced Tiny-ImageNet. We use ResNet-18 [1] for Tiny-ImageNet, following [30]. It is trained for 400 epochs with a batch size of 128. The initial learning rate is 0.1 and decays by 0.1 at the 250th, 320th, and 380th epochs. Images are padded 8 pixels on each side and randomly flipped horizontally, and are randomly cropped back to  $64 \times 64$ .

Training details for iNaturalist. We follow [16], [73] to use ResNet-50 [1] for iNaturalist. We train the model for 180 epochs with a batch size of 128. The learning rate is 0.05 at first. It decays at the 120th and the 160th by 0.1. For pre-processing and data augmentation, we follow [1], [73]. We normalize the images by subtracting the RGB means computed on the training set. In training, the images are resized to  $256 \times 256$  and flipped horizontally, and are randomly cropped back to  $224 \times 224$ .

# 5.3 Hyperparameter  $\gamma$

The only hyperparameter that we need to specifically tune for CDT is  $\gamma$ , which defines the temperature  $a_{c} = \left(\frac{N_{\max}}{N_{c}}\right)^{\gamma}$  in Equation 5. As will be shown in Figure 7, increasing  $\gamma$  eventually reduces the training accuracy of major classes. We use this property to set a range of  $\gamma$  for different experiments. For CIFAR-10,  $\gamma \in [0.0, 0.4]$ . For CIFAR-100 and TinyImageNet,  $\gamma \in [0.0, 0.2]$ . For iNaturalist,  $\gamma \in [0.0, 0.1]$ . We then select  $\gamma$  using a held-out validation set from the training data (we hold out  $K = 3$  instances per class). We remove classes with fewer than  $K$  training instances in this process.

# 8 DISCUSSIONS

# 8.1 Imbalanced learning for deep learning and traditional machine learning

In our pursuit to resolve imbalanced deep learning, we found that many existing works follow the strategies or principles of traditional machine learning to design algorithms. After a careful analysis we found that the reasons for poor minor-class accuracy are different: traditional machine learning suffers under-fitting; deep learning suffers over-fitting. It appears that such a difference is usually overlooked in imbalanced deep learning, and many algorithms are still designed to implicitly fit the minor class instances more.

We note that, the machine learning concept that small training data leads to over-fitting mainly applies to class-

balanced learning (i.e., every class has small data). In imbalanced learning, traditional machine learning approaches like logistic regression with pre-defined features suffer under-fitting to minor classes (see subsection 3.5 and Figure 10). In this case, re-weighting and re-sampling are effective: they make the model fit minor class instances more (see Figure 10). In imbalanced deep learning, however, we found that empirical risk minimization (ERM) already led to over-fitting to minor classes. While the seminal work by [33] on balanced learning may explain it, many imbalanced deep learning methods still apply re-weighting and over-sampling, which tend to further fit minor-class data. In other words, imbalanced deep learning is usually treated in the same way as imbalanced traditional machine learning, without noting that the reasons for poor accuracy are drastically different.

# 8.2 CDT vs. re-weighting and post-calibration

CDT is different from re-weighting. Let  $(\pmb{x},y)$  be a training instance and  $l(\pmb{W}^{\top}f_{\pmb{\theta}}(\pmb{x}),y)$  be the loss function, re-weighting times  $a_{y}$  to the loss (i.e.,  $a_{y}\times l(\pmb{W}^{\top}f_{\pmb{\theta}}(\pmb{x}),y))$ , where minor classes have larger  $a_{y}$ . In contrast, CDT divides each  $\pmb{w}_{c}$  in  $\pmb{W}$  by  $a_{c}$ . As ConvNets can fit training data well (i.e., achieve low  $l(\pmb{W}^{\top}f_{\pmb{\theta}}(\pmb{x}),y))$ , training with or without re-weighting will not change the decision values much. In contrast, CDT forces  $\pmb{w}_{c}^{\top}f_{\pmb{\theta}}(\pmb{x})$  to be larger by  $a_{c}$  to compensate for the decision value drop in testing due to feature deviation.

CDT and post-calibration (i.e.,  $\tau$ -normalization [32]) adjust  $\boldsymbol{w}_c^\top f_\theta(\boldsymbol{x})$  differently. CDT divides those of minor classes more in training (see Equation 5 of the main paper), encouraging the classifier to learn to compensate for it. In contrast, post-calibration divides those of major classes more in testing. Specifically,  $\tau$ -normalization applies the following decision rule

$$
\hat {y} = \arg \max  _ {c} \frac {\boldsymbol {w} _ {c} ^ {\top}}{\| \boldsymbol {w} _ {c} \| _ {2} ^ {\tau}} f _ {\boldsymbol {\theta}} (\boldsymbol {x}), \tag {7}
$$

where  $\tau \in [0,1]$  is a hyperparameter. We note that, major classes usually have larger  $\| \pmb{w}_c\| _2$

# 8.3 The reason for feature deviation

The image distributions of the training and test images are similar in the datasets we experimented with. The deviation is mainly caused by training ConvNets end-to-end with few minor-class images. These can be seen from Figure 5 (c): when the feature extractor is trained with balanced and sufficient data (ERM-UB) or pre-defined from other datasets (ERM-PD), there is no trend of feature deviation.

# 9 CONCLUSION

Classifiers trained with class-imbalanced data are known to perform poorly on minor classes. We perform a comprehensive analysis and find that the feature deviation phenomenon plays a crucial role in making a ConvNet classifier over-fit minor-class data. Such a phenomenon, however, is rarely observed in traditional machine learning approaches that use pre-defined features. To compensate for the effect of feature deviation — which pushes test data of minor classes toward regions of low decision values — we propose to incorporate class-dependent temperatures (CDT) in learning

a ConvNet classifier. CDT is simple to implement and achieves promising results on benchmark datasets. We hope that our findings and analysis would inspire more advanced algorithms in class-imbalanced deep learning.
