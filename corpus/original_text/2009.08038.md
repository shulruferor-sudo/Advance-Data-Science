# Reconfigurable Intelligent Surface (RIS) Assisted Wireless Coverage Extension: RIS Orientation and Location Optimization

Shuhao Zeng, Student Member, IEEE, Hongliang Zhang, Member, IEEE, Boya Di, Member, IEEE, Zhu Han, Fellow, IEEE, and Lingyang Song, Fellow, IEEE

Abstract-Recently, reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RIS) have attracted a lot of attention due to their capability of extending cell coverage by reflecting signals toward the receiver. In this letter, we analyze the coverage of a downlink RIS-assisted network with one base station (BS) and one user equipment (UE). Since the RIS orientation and the horizontal distance between the RIS and the BS have a significant influence on the cell coverage, we formulate an RIS placement optimization problem to maximize the cell coverage by optimizing the RIS orientation and horizontal distance. To solve the formulated problem, a coverage maximization algorithm (CMA) is proposed, where a closed-form optimal RIS orientation is obtained. Numerical results verify our analysis.

Index Terms—Reconfigurable intelligent surface assisted communication, coverage analysis, RIS placement optimization

# I. INTRODUCTION

Reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RIS) are an emerging wireless communication technology and is capable of shaping the propagation environment into a desired form [1], [2]. The RIS is inlaid with a large number of reflecting elements whose electromagnetic (EM) response can be tuned dynamically with onboard positive-intrinsic-negative (PIN) diodes [3]. By controlling the EM responses of the elements, the received signal strength can be optimized [4].

In the literature, RIS-assisted wireless networks have been studied to increase coverage and improve link quality. Existing works on RIS-assisted networks can be roughly divided into point-to-point [5], [6] and multi-user networks [7], [8]. In [5], the authors considered an RIS-assisted point-to-point network without direct link, where the network coverage, the signal-to-noise-ratio (SNR) gain, and the delay outage rate of this network were analyzed. The authors in [6] maximized the sum rate of a point-to-point RIS-assisted multi-input single-output system by jointly optimizing the beamformer at the transmitter and continuous phase shifts of the RIS. In [7], a multi-user network assisted by an RIS was investigated, where the digital beamformer and the RIS configuration are jointly optimized to maximize the sum rate. # II. SYSTEM MODEL

In this section, we first introduce the RIS-assisted network model. The channel model is then constructed.

# A. Scenario Description

As shown in Fig. 1, we consider a narrow-band downlink network with one UE and one BS. Due to the dynamic wireless environment involving unexpected fading, the link between the UE and the BS can be unstable or even fall into a complete outage [9]. To solve this problem, we introduce an RIS to assist the communication. To describe the topology of the system, a Cartesian coordinate is adopted, where the  $xoy$  plane coincides with the RIS surface, and the  $z$ -axis is vertical to the RIS. Based on the  $xoy$  plane, the space can be divided into two sides. To ensure that the RIS can reflect the signal from the BS towards the UE, we assume that the UE and the BS are in the same side of the RIS, i.e.,  $z > 0$  in the coordinate system.

The RIS is composed of  $M \times N$  sub-wavelength elements, each with the size of  $s_M \times s_N$ . As shown in Fig. 1, the RIS element contains several PIN diodes [10]. When the biased voltages applied to the PIN diodes change, the RIS element will change the reflection phase shift accordingly. Define  $\Gamma$  and  $\varphi^{m,n}$  as the reflection amplitude change and phase shift of the  $(m,n)$ -th element, respectively<sup>1</sup>. The reflection

# B. Channel Model

The channel from the BS to the UE is composed of  $MN$  RIS-based channels and a direct link, where the  $(m,n)$ -th RIS-based channel represents the channel from the BS to the UE via the  $(m,n)$ -th RIS element $^2$ .

The channel gain for the  $(m,n)$ -th RIS-based channel can be expressed as [9],

$$
h _ {m, n} = \frac {\lambda \sqrt {G s _ {M} s _ {N}}}{(4 \pi) ^ {\frac {3}{2}} \sqrt {D _ {m , n} ^ {\alpha} d _ {m , n} ^ {\alpha}}} e ^ {- j \frac {2 \pi}{\lambda} (D _ {m, n} + d _ {m, n})}, \tag {1}
$$

where  $\lambda$  is the wavelength corresponding to the carrier frequency,  $G$  is antenna gain,  $s_M$  and  $s_N$  are the size of one RIS element,  $\alpha$  represents the pathloss exponent,  $D_{m,n}$  and  $d_{m,n}$  are the distance between the BS and the  $(m,n)$ -th RIS element, and the distance between the  $(m,n)$ -th RIS element and the UE, respectively. Since the BS is far away from the RIS, its distances from different RIS elements are approximately the same, i.e.,  $D_{m,n} = D$ , where  $D$  is the distance between the BS and the center of the RIS. Besides, in order to derive the area of the cell coverage, we focus on finding the cell edge, and thus, it can be assumed that the UE is in the far field of the RIS. Therefore, we have  $d_{m,n} = d$ , where  $d$  is the distance between the UE and the center of the RIS. As a result, the pathloss is common to all RIS elements, i.e.,  $GD_{m,n}^{-\alpha}d_{m,n}^{-\alpha} = GD^{-\alpha}d^{-\alpha}\triangleq PL_R$

For the direct link, the channel model can be written as

$$
h _ {D} = \frac {\lambda \sqrt {G}}{4 \pi d _ {B U}} e ^ {- j \frac {2 \pi}{\lambda} d _ {B U}}, \tag {2}
$$

where  $d_{BU}$  represents the distance between the BS and the UE. Based on (1) and (2), the channel from the BS to the UE is given by

$$
h = \sum_ {m, n} \Gamma_ {m, n} h _ {m, n} + h _ {D}, \tag {3}
$$

Therefore, the received signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) can be expressed as  $\gamma = \frac{P|h|^2}{\sigma^2}$ , where  $P$  denotes the transmit power of the BS, and  $\sigma^2$  is the variance of the additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) received at the UE.

# III. CELL COVERAGE ANALYSIS

Before analyzing the cell coverage, we first introduce the definition.

Definition 1. The cell coverage is defined as an area where the received SNR at the UE is larger than a certain threshold  $\gamma_{th}$  [12], [13], i.e.,  $\gamma \geq \gamma_{th} = \gamma_s L_{mar},$  (4)

where  $\gamma_{s}$  represents UE sensitivity, and  $L_{mar}$  is margin for penetration loss.

In the following, we first find the optimal phase shifts of the RIS that maximizes the SNR. Then, the cell coverage is analyzed.

# A. Optimal Phase Shifts of RIS

To improve the system performance, the phase shifts are optimized to maximize the SNR. Define  $\eta_{R} = \frac{P}{\sigma^{2}}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(4\pi)^{3}}\left(\cos^{2}(\theta_{i})s_{M}s_{N}\right)$ ,  $\eta_{D} = \frac{P}{\sigma^{2}}\frac{\lambda^{2}G}{(4\pi)^{2}}$ , and  $\eta_{X} = 2\frac{P}{\sigma^{2}}\frac{\lambda^{2}\sqrt{G}}{(4\pi)^{\frac{5}{2}}}\left(\cos (\theta_{i})\sqrt{s_{M}s_{N}}\right)$ . The maximum SNR can be derived, given in the following theorem.

Theorem 1. When the phase shifts are set as

$$
\varphi_ {m, n} = \left(\frac {2 \pi}{\lambda} d _ {B U} - \frac {2 \pi}{\lambda} \left(D _ {m, n} + d _ {m, n}\right)\right) \mod 2 \pi , \tag {5}
$$

the SNR is maximized, and its value can be given by

$$
\gamma = \eta_ {R} M ^ {2} N ^ {2} P L _ {R} + \eta_ {D} d _ {B U} ^ {- 2} + \eta_ {X} M N \frac {\sqrt {P L _ {R}}}{d _ {B U}}. \tag {6}
$$

Proof. Based on (3), the SNR can be rewritten as

$$
\begin{array}{l} \gamma = \eta_ {R} P L _ {R} \sum_ {m, m ^ {\prime}, n, n ^ {\prime}} e ^ {j (\varphi_ {m, n} + \theta_ {m, n} - \varphi_ {m ^ {\prime}, n ^ {\prime}} - \theta_ {m ^ {\prime}, n ^ {\prime}})} + \eta_ {D} d _ {B U} ^ {- 2} \\ + \eta_ {X} \frac {\sqrt {P L _ {R}}}{d _ {B U}} \sum_ {m, n} \cos \left(\varphi_ {m, n} + \theta_ {m, n} - \frac {2 \pi}{\lambda} d _ {B U}\right), \tag {7} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\theta_{m,n} = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} (D_{m,n} + d_{m,n})$  We can find that when the phase shifts are set as shown in (5),  $\sum_{m,m^{\prime},n,n^{\prime}}e^{j(\phi_{m,n} + \theta_{m,n} - \phi_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}} - \theta_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}})}$  and  $\sum_{m,n}\cos (\varphi_{m,n} + \theta_{m,n} - \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} d_{BU})$  are simultaneously maximized, with the values being  $M^2 N^2$  and  $MN$  respectively [9]. Therefore, Theorem 1 holds.

# B. Cell Coverage Analysis

In this section, the cell coverage in a given direction is investigated first. The area of the cell coverage is then derived.

1) Cell Coverage in a given Direction: As shown in Fig. 2, we use  $\phi$  to represent the angle between the given direction and the direction from the BS to the RIS, where  $\phi \in [0,2\pi)$ . To derive the cell coverage in direction  $\phi$ , we first analyze how SNR  $\gamma$  changes with the horizontal distance between the BS and the UE, denoted by  $d_{BU}^{h}$ .

When  $d_{BU}^{h}$  is shorter than the horizontal distance between the BS and the RIS, denoted by  $D^{h}$ , it is hard to find out the influence of  $d_{BU}^{h}$  on SNR  $\gamma$ . This is because when  $d_{BU}^{h}$  becomes larger, the horizontal distance between the UE and the RIS, denoted by  $d^{h}$ , first decreases and then increases. To avoid discussing the trend of  $\gamma$  with respect to  $d_{BU}^{h}$ , we require that SNR  $\gamma$  is always above threshold  $\gamma_{th}$  when  $d_{BU}^{h} < D^{h}$ . Note that  $\gamma$  is lower bounded by  $\eta_{D} d_{BU}^{-2}$ , which decreases as the UE moves away from the BS. Therefore, by assuming that when  $d_{BU}^{h} = D^{h}$ ,  $\eta_{D} d_{BU}^{-2}$  does not fall below  $\gamma_{th}$ , i.e.,

$$
\eta_ {D} \left(\sqrt {\left(D ^ {h}\right) ^ {2} + \left(H _ {B} - H _ {U}\right) ^ {2}}\right) ^ {- 2} \geq \gamma_ {t h}, \tag {8}
$$

where  $H_{B}$  and  $H_{U}$  are the height of the BS and the UE, respectively, the SNR is guaranteed to exceed  $\gamma_{th}$ .

When  $d_{BU}^{h}$  exceeds  $D^{h}$ , it is obvious that  $\gamma$  strictly decreases as the UE moves away from the BS. Besides,  $\gamma$  approaches to 0 when the UE is sufficiently far away from the BS.

Based on above discussions, it can be found that the horizontal distance  $d_{BU}^{h}$  with  $\gamma = \gamma_{th}$  is unique, which is denoted by  $d_{th}(\phi)$ . Define  $g$  as the function of  $\gamma$  with respect to the angle  $\phi$  and the horizontal distance  $d_{BU}^{h}$ , i.e.,  $\gamma = g(\phi, d_{BU}^{h})$ . We then have

$$
g \left(\phi , d _ {t h} (\phi)\right) = \gamma_ {t h}, \forall \phi . \tag {9}
$$

The expression of  $g$  is given by

$$
\begin{array}{l} g (\phi , d _ {B U} ^ {h}) = \eta_ {R} M ^ {2} N ^ {2} P L _ {R} (\phi , d _ {B U} ^ {h}) + \eta_ {D} \left(d _ {B U} (\phi , d _ {B U} ^ {h})\right) ^ {- 2} \\ + \eta_ {X} M N \sqrt {P L _ {R} \left(\phi , d _ {B U} ^ {h}\right)} / d _ {B U} \left(\phi , d _ {B U} ^ {h}\right), \tag {10} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $PL_R(\phi, d_{BU}^h) = GD^{-\alpha}\Big((D^h - d_{BU}^h \cos \phi)^2 + (d_{BU}^h \sin \phi)^2 + (H_R - H_U)^2\Big)^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}$ , and  $d_{BU}(\phi, d_{BU}^h) = \sqrt{(d_{BU}^h)^2 + (H_B - H_U)^2}$ . We can also obtain that SNR  $\gamma$  exceeds threshold  $\gamma_{th}$  only if  $d_{BU}^h \leq d_{th}(\phi)$ .

Recall that the UE and the BS are in the same side of the RIS. Therefore, the cell coverage in some directions cannot achieve  $d_{th}(\phi)$ . Specifically, define  $\psi$  as the angle between the RIS orientation and direction from the BS to the RIS, where  $\psi \in (0,\pi)$ , and we have the following two cases,

-  $\phi \in [0, \psi) \cup (\psi + \pi, 2\pi)$ : To guarantee that the UE and the BS are in the same side of the RIS, the horizontal distance between the BS and the UE has to satisfy

$$
d _ {B U} ^ {h} \leq l (\phi), \tag {11}
$$

where  $l(\phi) = D^{h} / (\cos (\phi) - \sin (\phi)\cot (\psi))$  denotes the distance between the BS and the RIS in direction  $\phi$ , as shown in Fig. 2. Therefore, the cell coverage is given by

$$
c (\phi) = \min  \left(d _ {t h} (\phi), l (\phi)\right). \tag {12}
$$

-  $\phi \in [\psi, \psi + \pi]$ : The UE and the BS are always in the same side of the RIS. Therefore, the cell coverage is

$$
c (\phi) = d _ {t h} (\phi). \tag {13}
$$

To have a better understanding of the cell coverage in directions  $\phi \in [0,\psi)\cup (\psi +\pi ,2\pi)$ , we compare  $d_{th}(\phi)$  with  $l(\phi)$  in the following remark.

Remark 1. When  $\phi \in [0,\psi)\cup (\psi +\pi ,2\pi)$ , there are two angles satisfying  $d_{th}(\phi) = l(\phi)$ , denoted by  $\phi_u$  and  $\phi_l$ , i.e.,

$$
d _ {t h} (\phi_ {l}) = l (\phi_ {l}), \phi_ {l} <   \pi , \tag {14}
$$

$$
d _ {t h} \left(\phi_ {u}\right) = l \left(\phi_ {u}\right), \phi_ {u} > \pi . \tag {15}
$$

Besides,  $d_{th}(\phi) > l(\phi)$  only when  $\phi \in [0,\phi_l)\cup (\phi_u,2\pi)$ .

Proof. See Appendix A.

2) Area of cell coverage: Based on (12), (13), and Remark 1, we can derive the area of the cell coverage, given in the following theorem.

Theorem 2. The area of the cell coverage is given by

$$
S = \int_ {\phi_ {l}} ^ {\phi_ {u}} \frac {1}{2} d _ {t h} ^ {2} (\phi) d \phi + \frac {1}{2} \sin \left(\phi_ {l} - \phi_ {u}\right) l \left(\phi_ {l}\right) l \left(\phi_ {u}\right). \tag {16}
$$

Proof. The area of the cell coverage can be expressed as  $S = \int_{0}^{2\pi} \frac{c^2(\phi)}{2} d\phi$ . According to Remark 1, when  $\phi \in [0, \phi_l) \cup (\phi_u, 2\pi)$ ,  $c(\phi) = l(\phi)$ . Otherwise,  $c(\phi) = d_{th}(\phi)$ . Therefore, we have

$$
S = \int_ {\phi_ {l}} ^ {\phi_ {u}} \frac {d _ {t h} ^ {2} (\phi)}{2} d \phi + \int_ {0} ^ {\phi_ {l}} \frac {l ^ {2} (\phi)}{2} d \phi + \int_ {\phi_ {u}} ^ {2 \pi} \frac {l ^ {2} (\phi)}{2} d \phi . \tag {17}
$$

Define  $F(\phi) = \frac{(D^h)^2}{2}\frac{\sin\phi}{\cos\phi - \sin\phi\cot\psi}$ . Since  $F'(\phi) = \frac{l^2(\phi)}{2}$ , we have

$$
\int_ {0} ^ {\phi_ {l}} \frac {l ^ {2} (\phi)}{2} d \phi + \int_ {\phi_ {u}} ^ {2 \pi} \frac {l ^ {2} (\phi)}{2} d \phi = \frac {\sin \left(\phi_ {l} - \phi_ {u}\right) l \left(\phi_ {l}\right) l \left(\phi_ {u}\right)}{2}, \tag {18}
$$

which ends the proof.

# IV. RIS PLACEMENT OPTIMIZATION

In this section, we first formulate a coverage maximization problem. A coverage maximization algorithm (CMA) is then proposed to solve the formulated problem.

# A. Coverage Maximization Problem Formulation

Since the horizontal distance  $D^{h}$  between the RIS and the BS, and the orientation  $\psi$  of the RIS will influence the cell coverage, our aim is to maximize the area of the cell coverage  $S$  by jointly optimizing  $(D^{h},\psi)$ , as formulated below:

$$
\max  _ {D ^ {h}, \psi} S \quad s. t. (8). \tag {19}
$$

# B. Coverage Maximization Algorithm Design

To solve the formulated problem, we first find the optimal RIS orientation for any horizontal distance  $D^{h}$ . The result is given by the following theorem.

Theorem 3. Regardless of the horizontal distance, the optimal RIS orientation is always  $\psi = \frac{\pi}{2}$ , i.e., the RIS is deployed vertical to the direction from the BS to the RIS.

Proof. See Appendix B.

Then, we optimize the horizontal distance  $D^{h}$  given the optimal RIS orientation  $\psi$ , i.e.,

$$
\max  _ {D ^ {h}} S \quad s. t. (8). \tag {20}
$$

It can be found that  $D^{h}$  has an influence on  $\phi_{l}, \phi_{u}$ , and  $d_{th}(\phi)$  in the objective function. However, it is hard to depict

the influence in a closed-form expression. Inspired by the technique for solving the max-min problem [14], by regarding  $\phi_l$ ,  $\phi_u$ , and  $d_{th}(\phi)$  as additional optimization variables, their closed-form expressions are no longer needed. However, due to the integral in the objective function, an infinite number of additional variables are needed. To cope with this challenge, we discretize the integral in the objective function as below,

$$
\int_ {\phi_ {l}} ^ {\phi_ {u}} \frac {1}{2} d _ {t h} ^ {2} (\phi) d \phi \approx \sum_ {i = 0} ^ {K - 1} \frac {1}{2} d _ {t h} ^ {2} \left(\phi_ {l} + i \Delta\right) \Delta , \tag {21}
$$

where the integral interval is divided equally into  $K$  parts, each of which has a dimension of  $\Delta = \frac{\phi_u - \phi_l}{K}$ . For simplicity, we use  $y_{i}$  to replace  $d_{th}(\phi_l + i\Delta)$ . Therefore, horizontal distance optimization problem (20) can be transformed into its equivalent form,

$$
\max  _ {\substack {\phi_ {l}, \phi_ {u}, D ^ {h} \\ \{y _ {0}, \dots , y _ {K} \}}} \sum_ {i = 0} ^ {N - 1} \frac {1}{2} y _ {i} ^ {2} \Delta + \frac {\sin \left(\phi_ {l} - \phi_ {u}\right) l \left(\phi_ {l}\right) l \left(\phi_ {u}\right)}{2} \tag{22a}
$$

$$
s. t. g \left(\phi_ {l} + i \Delta , y _ {i}\right) = \gamma_ {t h}, i = 0, \dots , K, \tag {22b}
$$

(8), (14), and (15),

where constraint (22b) is derived based on (9). Denote an optimal solution for problem (22) by  $(\phi_l^*,\phi_u^*,(D^h)^*,\pmb {y}^*)$  where  $\pmb{y}^{*} = \{y_{0}^{*},\dots ,y_{K}^{*}\}$ . We have  $\left((D^{h})^{*},\frac{\pi}{2}\right)$  is optimal for RIS deployment problem (19). This is because problem (22) is equivalent to (20). Therefore,  $(D^{h})^{*}$  is optimal for horizontal problem (20). Besides, in (20), the horizontal distance is optimized given the RIS orientation as  $\frac{\pi}{2}$ , and thus,  $(\left(D^{h}\right)^{*},\frac{\pi}{2})$  is an optimal solution for RIS deployment problem (19).

Problem (22) is a constrained optimization problem, which can be solved by the interior point method [14]. The basic idea can be summarized as follows: in each iteration, we approximate problem (22) by removing inequality constraints in (22), and adding an additional logarithmic barrier function consisting of the inequality constraint functions to the objective function of (22). As such, the approximated problem is then solved utilizing the Newton method.

Based on the conclusion that the received SNR at the receiver will be maximized when the RIS is close to the receiver or transmitter [9], the RIS is placed close to the BS at the edge of the far field in scheme BSS.

# VI. CONCLUSION

In this letter, we have considered a downlink RIS-assisted network with one BS and one UE. The coverage of this network has been analyzed, and a problem has been formulated to maximize the cell coverage by optimizing the RIS placement. To solve the formulated problem, we have proposed the algorithm CMA. From the analysis and simulation, we can conclude that the RIS should be deployed vertical to the direction from the BS to the RIS. Besides, we should place the RIS at a moderate distance from the BS.

First, we show that as  $\phi$  approaches to  $\pi$ ,  $d_{th}(\phi)$  will become smaller while  $l(\phi)$  will be larger. Based on cosine theorem, the horizontal distance between the UE and the RIS can be expressed as  $d^{h} = \sqrt{(D^{h})^{2} + (d_{BU}^{h})^{2} - 2D^{h}d_{BU}^{h}\cos(\phi)}$ . Therefore, when  $\phi$  approaches to  $\pi$ ,  $d^{h}$  will become larger, which leads to a larger distance between the UE and the RIS, and thus, a lower SNR  $\gamma$ . Therefore,  $d_{th}(\phi)$  becomes smaller.

# APPENDIX B PROOF OF THEOREM 3

To show that  $\psi^{*} = \frac{\pi}{2}$  is optimal, we compare  $\psi^{*}$  with another RIS orientation  $\psi^{(0)}$ . As shown in Fig. 6(a), the cell coverage under  $\psi^{*}$  and that under  $\psi^{(0)}$  correspond to the yellow region and green region, respectively, where the area of the two regions are denoted by  $S^{*}$  and  $S^{(0)}$ , respectively. It is worthwhile noting that  $d_{th}^{*}$  is larger than  $d_{th}^{(0)}$  since as  $\psi$  deviates from  $\frac{\pi}{2}$ , reflection amplitude  $\Gamma$  decreases, and thus, the SNR at the UE degrades. In the following, we show that  $S^{(0)} \leq S^{*}$ , from which we can obtain that  $\frac{\pi}{2}$  is the optimal RIS orientation.

Note that  $S^{*} - S^{(0)} > \Delta S^{*} - \Delta S^{(0)}$ , where  $\Delta S^{*}$  and  $\Delta S^{(0)}$  are the areas of the two sectors shown in Fig. 6(b). Therefore, we will focus on comparing  $\Delta S^{*}$  with  $\Delta S^{(0)}$  in the following. To compare  $\Delta S^{*}$  with  $\Delta S^{(0)}$ , we arbitrarily select two opposite radius of the sectors, denoted by  $r^{*}$  and  $r^{(0)}$ , respectively, and we use  $P^{*}$  and  $P^{(0)}$  to represent the intersection between  $r^{*}$  and  $d_{th}^{(0)}$ , and that between  $r^{(0)}$  and

$d_{th}^{(0)}$ , respectively. Compared with the case where the UE is in  $P^{(0)}$ , the UE is closer to the BS when it is in  $P^{*}$ . However, SNR  $\gamma$  is equal to  $\gamma_{th}$  when the UE is in  $P^{(0)}$  or  $P^{*}$ . Therefore, when the UE is in  $P^{(0)}$ , it is closer to the RIS compared with the case where the UE is in  $P^{*}$ , i.e.,  $r^{(0)} < r^{*}$ . As a result, we have  $\Delta S^{(0)} < \Delta S^{*}$ , which indicates that  $S^{*} \geq S^{(0)}$ .
