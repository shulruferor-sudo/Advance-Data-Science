# Associating Uncertainty to Extended Poses for on Lie Group IMU Preintegration with Rotating Earth

Martin Brossard, Axel Barrau, Paul Chauchat, and Silvère Bonnabel

Abstract—The recently introduced matrix group  $\mathrm{SE}_2(3)$  provides a  $5\times 5$  matrix representation for the orientation, velocity and position of an object in the 3-D space, a triplet we call "extended pose". In this paper we build on this group to develop a theory to associate uncertainty with extended poses represented by  $5\times 5$  matrices. Our approach is particularly suited to describe how uncertainty propagates when the extended pose represents the state of an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU). In particular it allows revisiting the theory of IMU preintegration on manifold and reaching a further theoretic level in this field. Exact preintegration formulas that account for rotating Earth, that is, centrifugal force and Coriolis force, are derived as a byproduct, and the factors are shown to be more accurate. The approach is validated through extensive simulations and applied to sensor-fusion where a loosely-coupled fixed-lag smoother fuses IMU and LiDAR on one hour long experiments using our experimental car. It shows how handling rotating Earth may be beneficial for long-term navigation within incremental smoothing algorithms.

Index Terms—mobile robotics, uncertainty propagation, Lie group, preintegration, sensor-fusion, Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU).

# I. INTRODUCTION

The main contribution of this paper is to provide practical techniques to associate uncertainty to the triplet

$$
\left\{\text {o r i e n t a t i o n} \mathbf {R} \in S O (3), \text {v e l o c i t y} \mathbf {v} \in \mathbb {R} ^ {3}, \text {p o s i t i o n} \mathbf {p} \in \mathbb {R} ^ {3} \right\}
$$

of a moving platform equipped with an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) for use in navigation and estimation problems.

A  $4 \times 4$  transformation matrix may model the pose of a platform, i.e. its orientation and its position. This has been popularized in computer vision [1], probabilistic robotics [2] and pose-graph SLAM [3]. In particular, Gaussian distributions in exponential coordinates of the Lie group  $SE(3)$  provide practical tools for propagating pose uncertainty or fusing multiple measurements of a pose in one estimate, see the pioneering work of [4] and its follow-up [5].

When one uses an IMU, a sensor that embeds gyrometers and accelerometers, one manipulates the pose of the sensor and its velocity that we refer in this paper to as an extended pose. An extended pose cannot be modeled as an element of  $SE(3)$ . As a result, the IMU propagation equations are not amenable to the framework of [4,5].

# A. Relations with  $SE(3)$  based Uncertainty Propagation

One contribution of [4], and [5] also, consists in compounding pose uncertainties based on a discrete-time integration of the  $SE(3)$  kinematic equation as

$$
\mathbf {T} _ {i + 1} = \mathbf {T} _ {i} \boldsymbol {\Upsilon} _ {i}, \tag {1}
$$

where  $\mathbf{T}_i$  is a global pose and  $\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}_i$  is a local increment, a scheme which is not applicable to IMU kinematic equations. We generalize (1) and propagate an extended pose as

$$
\mathbf {T} _ {i + 1} = \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i} \Phi (\mathbf {T} _ {i}) \boldsymbol {\Upsilon} _ {i}, \tag {2}
$$

where  $\mathbf{T}_i$  is a global extended pose,  $\Gamma_i$  is a global increment,  $\Upsilon_{i}$  a local increment, and  $\Phi (\cdot)$  a function that depends only on the time between instants  $i$  and  $i + 1$ . The scheme (1) models a robot driving in the environment where  $\Upsilon_{i}$  is given by, e.g., differential wheel speeds or visual relative pose estimates. To derive kinematic model (2), we build on a novel representation of extended poses by  $5\times 5$  matrices, namely the group  $SE_2(3)$ , introduced in the recent paper [10] for navigation.

To express how pose uncertainty evolves, [5] associates uncertainty to poses as

$$
\mathbf {T} _ {i} = \hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {i} \exp (\boldsymbol {\xi} _ {i}), \quad \boldsymbol {\Upsilon} _ {i} = \hat {\boldsymbol {\Upsilon}} _ {i} \exp (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i}), \tag {3}
$$

where  $\hat{\mathbf{T}}_i$  and  $\hat{\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}}_i$  are noise-free variables,  $\exp (\cdot)$  is the  $SE(3)$  exponential map, and  $\xi_{i}\sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{6},\pmb{\Sigma}_{i})$  and  $\eta_{i}\sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{6},\mathbf{Q})$  are uncertainties, a.k.a. errors or noises. In a probabilistic context, the compound (1) is then rewritten as

$$
\hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {i + 1} \exp (\boldsymbol {\xi} _ {i + 1}) = \hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {i} \exp (\boldsymbol {\xi} _ {i}) \hat {\mathbf {Y}} _ {i} \exp (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i}). \tag {4}
$$

Given nominal values and associated uncertainties  $\{\hat{\mathbf{T}}_i,\mathbf{\Sigma}_i\}$ $\{\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_i,\mathbf{Q}\}$  [5] shows how to compute  $\{\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{i + 1},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i + 1}\}$

By similarly leveraging (3), we investigate in this paper how extended pose uncertainties propagate through (2) as

$$
\hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {i + 1} \exp (\boldsymbol {\xi} _ {i + 1}) = \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i} \Phi \left(\hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {i} \exp (\boldsymbol {\xi} _ {i})\right) \hat {\mathbf {Y}} _ {i} \exp (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i}),
$$

where naturally  $\exp (\cdot)$  denotes the exponential map of  $SE_2(3)$ , which is an extension of  $SE(3)$  suited to robot state estimation involving IMUs. The obtained formulas have very concrete implications for preintegration, see Section V.

# B. Links and Differences with Existing Literature

In robotics, it is well established that estimating uncertain spatial relationships is fundamentally important for state-estimation [11,12], robot control [13], or active SLAM [14].

The pioneering works [15,16] notices that mobile robots dispersion under the effect of sensor noise resembles more a "banana" than a standard ellipse, which is accurately approximated with Gaussian distributions in Lie exponential coordinates of  $SE(2)$  [17]. This paves the ways for defining uncertainty on manifolds, see e.g. [18,19], and for Gaussians on  $SE(3)$  [16,18]. [4] obtained the first results about uncertainty propagation on  $SE(3)$  in a general and non-parametric context, which are complemented by the results of [5] in discrete time. The approach is extended to continuous time in [20]-[22], and to correlated pose uncertainties in [23].

Preintegrating IMU is an alternative to the standard inertial measurement integration which has de facto been adopted in optimization-based estimation framework such as GTSAM [24] and OKVIS [25]. It was initiated by [26] and later improved in [6,7,27,28] notably for avoiding singularities due to the use of Euler angles. IMU preintegration is adapted, e.g., for legged robot odometry [29,30], differential drive motion model [31], unknown time offset [32], wheel odometry [33,34], and covariance preintegration [35]. This paper generalizes and goes beyond the manifold representation of [6]. Indeed the latter is concerned with the manifold structure of Lie group  $SO(3)$  only, and treats the remainder of the state linearly, whereas we embed the whole state into the group  $SE_2(3)$ . Beyond the "on-manifold" approach we hence also benefit from the fact the very structure of  $SE_2(3)$  proves much more accurate for describing IMU-related equations and uncertainty propagation. The choice of the  $SE_2(3)$  Lie group formalism takes its roots in invariant Kalman filtering, see [10,36], which has led to more accurate and consistent extended Kalman filters and has yet prompted a high-end industrial product [37].

The work of [38], later extended in a visual-inertial navigation system in [39], introduces preintegration in its continuous form, [40] proposes asynchronous preintegration with Gaussian processes, [41] addresses continuous preintegration as a higher-order Taylor expansion, [42] describes a scheme based on switched linear systems, and [43,44] provide closed-form expressions for computing analytically the preintegration factors. This work improves the Euler integration scheme of [6] to limit discretization errors, whose integration schemes remain compatible with our approach based on exact discretization.

This paper is an extension of our preliminary conference paper [8], see also early ideas in [45], that contains in-depth discussions, comprehensive technical derivations, numerical and real experiments, and publicly available implementation.

# A.  $SE_2(3)$ , the Lie Group of Extended Poses

Estimating the orientation  $\mathbf{R}$ , velocity  $\mathbf{v}$  and position  $\mathbf{p}$  of a rigid body in space is a common problem in robotic navigation and perception. We represent in this paper rotation  $\mathbf{R}$  with the special orthogonal group

$$
S O (3) := \left\{\mathbf {R} \in \mathbb {R} ^ {3 \times 3} \mid \mathbf {R R} ^ {T} = \mathbf {I} _ {3}, \det  \mathbf {R} = 1 \right\}.
$$

The set of poses may be defined using homogeneous matrices of the special Euclidean group

$$
S E (3) := \left\{\mathbf {T} = \left[ \begin{array}{c c} \mathbf {R} & \mathbf {p} \\ \hline \mathbf {0} _ {3} ^ {T} & 1 \end{array} \right] \in \mathbb {R} ^ {4 \times 4} \middle | \begin{array}{c} \mathbf {R} \in S O (3) \\ \mathbf {p} \in \mathbb {R} ^ {3} \end{array} \right\}.
$$

This paper describes extended poses through the following group first introduced to the best of our knowledge in [10]

$$
S E _ {2} (3) := \left\{\mathbf {T} = \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} \mathbf {R} & \mathbf {v} & \mathbf {p} \\ \hline \mathbf {0} _ {2 \times 3} & \mathbf {I} _ {2} \end{array} \right] \in \mathbb {R} ^ {5 \times 5} \middle | \begin{array}{c} \mathbf {R} \in S O (3) \\ \mathbf {v}, \mathbf {p} \in \mathbb {R} ^ {3} \end{array} \right\}.
$$

$SO(3)$ ,  $SE(3)$  and  $SE_2(3)$  are matrix Lie groups where matrix multiplication provides group composition of two elements, and matrix inverse provides element inverse. The linear operator  $\wedge$  maps elements  $\pmb{\xi} \in \mathbb{R}^9$  to the Lie algebra of  $SE_2(3)$

$$
\boldsymbol {\xi} ^ {\wedge} := \left[ \begin{array}{l} \phi \\ \boldsymbol {\nu} \\ \boldsymbol {\rho} \end{array} \right] ^ {\wedge} := \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} \phi_ {\times} & \boldsymbol {\nu} & \boldsymbol {\rho} \\ \hline \mathbf {0} _ {2 \times 3} & \mathbf {0} _ {2 \times 2} \end{array} \right] \in \mathfrak {s c} _ {2} (3), \tag {5}
$$

where  $\phi \in \mathbb{R}^3$ ,  $\pmb {\nu}\in \mathbb{R}^3$ ,  $\pmb {\rho}\in \mathbb{R}^3$ , and

$$
\phi_ {\times} := \left[ \begin{array}{l} \phi_ {1} \\ \phi_ {2} \\ \phi_ {3} \end{array} \right] _ {\times} := \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} 0 & - \phi_ {3} & \phi_ {2} \\ \phi_ {3} & 0 & - \phi_ {1} \\ - \phi_ {2} & \phi_ {1} & 0 \end{array} \right] \tag {6}
$$

denotes the skew symmetric matrix associated with cross product with  $\phi$ , which maps also a  $3 \times 1$  vector to an element of  $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ , the Lie algebra of  $SO(3)$ .

1) Exponential, Logarithm, & Adjoint Operator: the exponential map conveniently maps an element  $\pmb {\xi}\in \mathbb{R}^9$  to  $SE_{2}(3)$

$$
\exp (\boldsymbol {\xi}) := \exp_ {m} \left(\boldsymbol {\xi} ^ {\wedge}\right) = \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {\infty} \frac {1}{k !} \left(\boldsymbol {\xi} ^ {\wedge}\right) ^ {k}, \tag {7}
$$

and its local inverse, the logarithm map, defined as

$$
\log (\mathbf {T}) = \left(\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {\infty} (- 1) ^ {k + 1} \frac {\left(\mathbf {T} - \mathbf {I} _ {5}\right) ^ {k}}{k}\right) ^ {\vee}, \tag {8}
$$

where  $\vee$  is the inverse of  $\wedge$ , enables to map the small perturbation  $\exp (\pmb {\xi})\in SE_2(3)$  to  $\mathbb{R}^9$  such that

$$
\boldsymbol {\xi} = \log (\exp (\boldsymbol {\xi})). \tag {9}
$$

We conveniently define the adjoint operator

$$
\operatorname {A d} _ {\mathbf {T}} := \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} \mathbf {R} & \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} & \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} \\ \mathbf {v} _ {\times} \mathbf {R} & \mathbf {R} & \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} \\ \mathbf {p} _ {\times} \mathbf {R} & \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} & \mathbf {R} \end{array} \right] \tag {10}
$$

as an operator acting directly on  $\mathbb{R}^9$ . The following relations prove extremely useful:

$$
\mathbf {T} \exp (\boldsymbol {\xi}) \mathbf {T} ^ {- 1} = \exp \left(\mathrm {A d} _ {\mathbf {T}} \boldsymbol {\xi}\right), \tag {11}
$$

$$
\Rightarrow \mathbf {T} \exp (\boldsymbol {\xi}) = \exp \left(\operatorname {A d} _ {\mathbf {T}} \boldsymbol {\xi}\right) \mathbf {T}, \tag {12}
$$

$$
\mathrm {A d} _ {\mathbf {T}} \mathrm {A d} _ {\mathbf {T}} = \mathrm {A d} _ {\mathbf {r T}}. \tag {13}
$$

2) Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff (BCH) Formula: the BCH formula provides powerful tools to manipulate uncertainty on Lie groups, which can be used to compound two matrix exponentials

$$
\log_ {m} \left(\exp_ {m} (\mathbf {A}) \exp_ {m} (\mathbf {B})\right) = \mathbf {A} + \mathbf {B} + \frac {1}{2} [ \mathbf {A}, \mathbf {B} ]
$$

$$
+ \frac {1}{1 2} ([ \mathbf {A}, [ \mathbf {A}, \mathbf {B} ] ] + [ \mathbf {B}, [ \mathbf {B}, \mathbf {A} ] ]) - \frac {1}{2 4} [ \mathbf {B}, [ \mathbf {A}, [ \mathbf {A}, \mathbf {B} ] ] ] + \dots
$$

in terms of infinite series, where the Lie bracket is given by

$$
[ \mathbf {A}, \mathbf {B} ] := \mathbf {A B} - \mathbf {B A}.
$$

In the particular case of  $SE_2(3)$ , we have

$$
[ \boldsymbol {\xi} ^ {\wedge}, \boldsymbol {\eta} ^ {\wedge} ] := \boldsymbol {\xi} ^ {\wedge} \boldsymbol {\eta} ^ {\wedge} - \boldsymbol {\xi} ^ {\wedge} \boldsymbol {\eta} ^ {\wedge} = (\boldsymbol {\xi} ^ {\wedge} \boldsymbol {\eta}) ^ {\wedge}, \tag {14}
$$

where the "curlywedge"  $\mathbf{\Omega}$  operator is defined as

$$
\boldsymbol {\xi} ^ {\text {人}} := \left[ \begin{array}{l} \phi \\ \boldsymbol {\nu} \\ \rho \end{array} \right] ^ {\text {人}} := \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} \phi_ {\times} & \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} & \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} \\ \boldsymbol {\nu} _ {\times} & \phi_ {\times} & \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} \\ \boldsymbol {\rho} _ {\times} & \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} & \phi_ {\times} \end{array} \right], \tag {15}
$$

which is  $9 \times 9$ . We note that

$$
\log \left(\exp (\boldsymbol {\xi}) \exp (\boldsymbol {\eta})\right) = \boldsymbol {\xi} + \boldsymbol {\eta} + \frac {1}{2} \boldsymbol {\xi} ^ {\wedge} \boldsymbol {\eta}
$$

$$
+ \frac {1}{1 2} \left(\boldsymbol {\xi} ^ {\wedge} \boldsymbol {\xi} ^ {\wedge} \boldsymbol {\eta} + \boldsymbol {\eta} ^ {\wedge} \boldsymbol {\eta} ^ {\wedge} \boldsymbol {\xi}\right) - \frac {1}{2 4} \boldsymbol {\eta} ^ {\wedge} \boldsymbol {\xi} ^ {\wedge} \boldsymbol {\xi} ^ {\wedge} \boldsymbol {\eta} + \dots , \tag {16}
$$

showing  $\exp (\pmb {\xi})\exp (\pmb {\eta})\neq \exp (\pmb {\xi} + \pmb {\eta})$  . We thus require approximations to compute the logarithm of a product of exponentials. If we assume, e.g., that  $\pmb{\eta}$  is a small noise while  $\pmb{\xi}$  is non-negligible, we get

$$
\log \left(\exp (\boldsymbol {\xi}) \exp (\boldsymbol {\eta})\right) = \boldsymbol {\xi} + \mathcal {J} _ {\boldsymbol {\xi}} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {\eta} + O \left(\left\| \boldsymbol {\eta} \right\| ^ {2}\right), \tag {17}
$$

where  $\mathcal{I}_{\xi}^{-1}$  is the  $9\times 9$  inverse left-Jacobian of  $SE_2(3)$ . If both  $\pmb{\xi}$  and  $\pmb{\eta}$  are small quantities, we recover the first-order approximation

$$
\log \left(\exp (\boldsymbol {\xi}) \exp (\boldsymbol {\eta})\right) = \boldsymbol {\xi} + \boldsymbol {\eta} + O \left(\| \boldsymbol {\xi} \| ^ {2}, \| \boldsymbol {\eta} \| ^ {2}\right). \tag {18}
$$

Closed-form expressions of  $\exp(\cdot)$ ,  $\log(\cdot)$  and Jacobians are given in Appendix.

Remark 1 (Overloading Operators). We overload the  $\exp(\cdot)$ ,  $\log(\cdot)$ , and Jacobian in the sense that they can be applied to both  $3 \times 1$  and  $9 \times 1$  vectors for respectively  $SO(3)$  and  $SE_2(3)$  exponential, logarithm and Jacobian.

# B. Uncertainty & Random Variables on  $SE_2(3)$

Let  $\hat{\mathbf{T}}\in SE_2(3)$  represent a noise-free value, and  $\pmb {\xi}\in \mathbb{R}^9$  a small perturbation. The approach based on additive uncertainty "T+ is not valid as these quantities are not vector elements. In contrast, we define a random variable on  $SE_{2}(3)$

$$
\mathbf {T} := \hat {\mathbf {T}} \exp (\boldsymbol {\xi}), \tag {19}
$$

where  $\hat{\mathbf{T}}$  is a noise-free "mean" of the distribution and  $\xi \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_9, \Sigma)$  is a zero-mean multivariate Gaussian in  $\mathbb{R}^9$ . We thus describe statistical dispersion of extended poses with the exponential map. This approach is referred to as "concentrated Gaussian" [18,47], and has been largely advocated for Lie groups, see [5,10,36,48].

Remark 2 (Left and Right Perturbations). We apply perturbations in (19) on the right rather than the left as in [5]. Both are valid, right perturbations are more convenient to propagate extended pose uncertainties in the context of this paper, and one can move from right perturbations to left perturbations and vice-versa through the relation (12).

# III. IMU EQUATIONS ON FLAT EARTH REVISITED

This section presents the IMU kinematic model on flat Earth (e.g. with an inertial frame of reference) and transposes it into the form (2). This was introduced in the bias- and noise-free case in [45], see proofs therein. Noises, biases and rotating Earth are progressively addressed in the following sections.

# A. IMU Kinematic Equations on Flat Earth

Let  $\mathbf{R}$  denote the rotation matrix encoding the orientation of the IMU, i.e. the rotation from the global frame to the local inertial frame, and let  $\mathbf{v}$  and  $\mathbf{p}$  denote the velocity and the position of the IMU expressed in the global frame. An IMU collects angular velocity  $\omega^m$  and proper acceleration  $\mathbf{a}^m$  measurements which relate to the corresponding true values  $\omega$  and  $\mathbf{a}$  as

$$
\boldsymbol {\omega} ^ {m} = \boldsymbol {\omega} + \mathbf {b} ^ {\boldsymbol {\omega}} + \boldsymbol {\eta} ^ {\boldsymbol {\omega}}, \tag {20}
$$

$$
\mathbf {a} ^ {m} = \mathbf {a} + \mathbf {b} ^ {\mathbf {a}} + \boldsymbol {\eta} ^ {\mathbf {a}}. \tag {21}
$$

The measurements are corrupted both by the time-varying biases  $\mathbf{b}^{\omega}$  and  $\mathbf{b}^{\mathbf{a}}$ , and zero-mean white Gaussian noises  $\pmb{\eta}^{\omega}$

and  $\eta^{\mathrm{a}}$ . The motion equations of the IMU sensor on flat Earth write

$$
\dot {\mathbf {R}} = \mathbf {R} \left(\boldsymbol {\omega} ^ {m} - \mathbf {b} ^ {\boldsymbol {\omega}} - \boldsymbol {\eta} ^ {\boldsymbol {\omega}}\right) _ {\times}, \tag {22}
$$

$$
\dot {\mathbf {v}} = \mathbf {R} \left(\mathbf {a} ^ {m} - \mathbf {b} ^ {\mathbf {a}} - \boldsymbol {\eta} ^ {\mathbf {a}}\right) + \mathbf {g}, \tag {23}
$$

$$
\dot {\mathbf {p}} = \mathbf {v}, \tag {24}
$$

where  $\mathbf{g} = [0 0 - 9.81]^T \in \mathbb{R}^3$  is the global gravity vector.

# B. Revisiting IMU Equations for Extended Poses

Following [45], we may rewrite the model (22)-(24) in the form (2) as follows. First, we associate a matrix  $\mathbf{T}_t \in SE_2(3)$  to the extended pose  $\{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{p}\}$  at time  $t$ . Then, we write  $\mathbf{T}_0$  the solution initialized at  $t_0 = 0$ , such that

$$
\mathbf {T} _ {t} = \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {t} \Phi_ {t} \left(\mathbf {T} _ {0}\right) \boldsymbol {\Upsilon} _ {t}, \tag {25}
$$

where  $\Phi_t(\cdot)$  and  $\Gamma_t$  only depend on  $t$  as

$$
\Phi_ {t} (\mathbf {T}) := \left[ \begin{array}{c c} \mathbf {R} & | \mathbf {v} | \mathbf {p} + t \mathbf {v} \\ \hline \mathbf {0} _ {2 \times 3} & \mathbf {I} _ {2} \end{array} \right], \tag {26}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {t} := \left[ \begin{array}{c c} \mathbf {I} _ {3} & t \mathbf {g} \\ \hline \mathbf {0} _ {2 \times 3} & \mathbf {I} _ {2} \end{array} \right], \tag {27}
$$

and where  $\Upsilon_{t}$  is solution to differential equations leading to

$$
\boldsymbol {\Upsilon} _ {t} := \left[ \begin{array}{c c} \Delta \mathbf {R} _ {t} & \Delta \mathbf {v} _ {t} \\ \hline \mathbf {0} _ {2 \times 3} & \mathbf {I} _ {2} \end{array} \right], \tag {28}
$$

where

$$
\Delta \mathbf {R} _ {t} = \exp \left(\int_ {0} ^ {t} \boldsymbol {\omega} d t\right) = \exp \left(\int_ {0} ^ {t} \left(\boldsymbol {\omega} ^ {m} - \mathbf {b} ^ {\boldsymbol {\omega}} - \boldsymbol {\eta} ^ {\boldsymbol {\omega}}\right) d t\right), \tag {29}
$$

$$
\Delta \mathbf {v} _ {t} = \int_ {0} ^ {t} \Delta \mathbf {R} _ {t} \mathbf {a} d t = \int_ {0} ^ {t} \Delta \mathbf {R} _ {t} \left(\mathbf {a} ^ {m} - \mathbf {b} ^ {\mathbf {a}} - \boldsymbol {\eta} ^ {\mathbf {a}}\right) d t, \tag {30}
$$

$$
\Delta \mathbf {p} _ {t} = \int_ {0} ^ {t} \Delta \mathbf {v} _ {t} d t. \tag {31}
$$

The reader can check differentiating the product (25) and using (26)-(28) that we recover (22)-(24).  $\Delta \mathbf{R}_t$ ,  $\Delta \mathbf{v}_t$  and  $\Delta \mathbf{p}_t$ , referred to as the preintegrated measurements in [6], are quantities based solely on the inertial measurements and do not depend on the initial state  $\mathbf{T}_0$ . This allows to define a unique factor between extended poses at arbitrary temporally distant keyframes based on a unique integration of IMU outputs.

We now consider discrete time steps with time interval  $\Delta t$ . Denoting  $\Gamma_{i} \coloneqq \Gamma_{\Delta t}$ ,  $\Phi \coloneqq \Phi_{\Delta t}$ , and  $\Upsilon_{i} \coloneqq \Upsilon_{\Delta t}$  we get

$$
\mathbf {T} _ {i + 1} = \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i} \Phi (\mathbf {T} _ {i}) \boldsymbol {\Upsilon} _ {i}, \tag {32}
$$

where  $\Gamma_{i},\mathbf{T}_{i}$ , and  $\Upsilon_{i}$  all live in  $SE_2(3)$ . The reader may readily check that  $\Phi (\Gamma \mathbf{T}) = \Phi (\Gamma)\Phi (\mathbf{T})$  and  $\Phi (\exp (\pmb {\xi})) = \exp (\mathbf{F}\pmb {\xi})$ , that we combine as

$$
\Phi (\mathbf {T} \exp (\boldsymbol {\xi})) = \Phi (\mathbf {T}) \exp (\mathbf {F} \boldsymbol {\xi}), \tag {33}
$$

where

$$
\mathbf {F} := \mathbf {F} _ {\Delta t} := \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} \mathbf {I} _ {3} & \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} & \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} \\ \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} & \mathbf {I} _ {3} & \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} \\ \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} & \Delta t \mathbf {I} _ {3} & \mathbf {I} _ {3} \end{array} \right]. \tag {34}
$$

Remark 3 (Exact Discretization). The formula (32) is an exact discretization of (22)-(24). However it involves (29)-(31) that need to be numerically solved at some point. As IMU measurements come in discrete-time at a high rate, we may call  $\Delta t$  the discretization step, assume measurements to be constant over time intervals  $\Delta t$ , and perform Euler, midpoint, or more sophisticated integration schemes [6,38,42,44].

# C. Noise Model and Approximation

An IMU actually measures noisy observations (20)-(21) and we consider IMU noise in the form of

$$
\boldsymbol {\Upsilon} _ {i} := \hat {\boldsymbol {\Upsilon}} _ {i} \exp (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i}), \quad \boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} \sim \mathcal {N} (\mathbf {0} _ {9}, \mathbf {Q}), \tag {35}
$$

where  $\mathbf{\Upsilon}_i$  refers to the true quantity and  $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_i = \hat{\mathbf{Y}}_i(\hat{\mathbf{b}}_i)$  refers to the estimated one that has been computed with estimated biases

$$
\hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i} := \left[ \begin{array}{l} \hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i} ^ {\omega} \\ \hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i} ^ {\mathbf {a}} \end{array} \right]. \tag {36}
$$

To justify (35), let us integrate IMU kinematics (22)-(24) for one time step with constant global acceleration:

$$
\hat {\mathbf {Y}} _ {i} = \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} \exp (\hat {\omega} _ {i} \Delta t) & \hat {\mathbf {a}} _ {i} \Delta t & \hat {\mathbf {a}} _ {i} \Delta t ^ {2} / 2 \\ \hline \mathbf {0} _ {2 \times 3} & & \mathbf {I} _ {2} \end{array} \right], \tag {37}
$$

where  $\hat{\omega}_i = \omega_i^m -\hat{\mathbf{b}}_i^\omega$  and  $\hat{\mathbf{a}}_i = \mathbf{a}_i^m -\hat{\mathbf{b}}_i^{\mathbf{a}}$ . This leads to the first-order in the noise terms to

$$
\Upsilon_ {i} \stackrel {(1 7)} {=} \hat {\Upsilon} _ {i} \exp \left(\underbrace {\mathbf {G} _ {i} \left[ \begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {\omega} \\ \boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {\mathrm {a}} \end{array} \right]} _ {\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i}} + O \left(\left\| \boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} \right\| ^ {2}\right)\right), \tag {38}
$$

where we define the  $9 \times 6$  matrix

$$
\mathbf {G} _ {i} = - \left[ \begin{array}{c c} \mathcal {J} _ {\hat {\omega} _ {i} \Delta t} ^ {- 1} \Delta t & \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} \\ \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} & \exp (- \hat {\omega} _ {i} \Delta t) \Delta t \\ \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} & \exp (- \hat {\omega} _ {i} \Delta t) \Delta t ^ {2} / 2 \end{array} \right], \tag {39}
$$

such that the zero-mean noise  $\pmb{\eta}_i$  has covariance

$$
\mathbf {Q} _ {i} = \mathbf {G} _ {i} \operatorname {c o v} \left(\left[ \begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {\omega} \\ \boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {\mathbf {a}} \end{array} \right]\right) \mathbf {G} _ {i} ^ {T}, \tag {40}
$$

where it is not necessary to assume that IMU noises are isotropic. We fix  $\mathbf{Q}_i = \mathbf{Q}$  through this paper instead of defining a time varying  $\mathbf{Q}_i$  for convenience of exposition.

Remark 4 (Bias Error). As in [6], we start assuming in (35) that biases are known. We then investigate how an estimated bias update affects  $\hat{\Upsilon}_i$  in Section V-D.

Remark 5 (Integration Scheme). We assume in (37) piecewise constant global acceleration as in [6] for convenience of exposition. Opting for a different integration scheme, e.g., with constant local acceleration [44], would modify the values inside (37) and (39) and leave the rest of paper unchanged (details are given in the supplementary material). There also exists polynomial structures for higher order uncertainty tracking [49], see and [50] for discussions in the Gaussian context.

The goal of the present section is to compute uncertainty of an extended pose given an initial uncertainty and IMU noises.

A. Propagating Uncertainty through Noise-Free IMU Model Let

$$
\mathbf {T} _ {i} := \hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {i} \exp (\boldsymbol {\xi} _ {i}), \quad \boldsymbol {\xi} _ {i} \sim \mathcal {N} \left(\mathbf {0} _ {9}, \boldsymbol {\Sigma} _ {i}\right), \tag {41}
$$

be our extended pose estimate with error  $\pmb{\xi}_i$ . The propagation of  $\mathbf{T}_i$  through noise-free model (32) writes

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbf {T} _ {i + 1} \stackrel {(3 2)} {=} \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i} \Phi (\mathbf {T} _ {i}) \hat {\mathbf {Y}} _ {i} \stackrel {(4 1)} {=} \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i} \Phi \big (\hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {i} \exp (\boldsymbol {\xi} _ {i}) \big) \hat {\mathbf {Y}} _ {i} \\ \stackrel {(3 3)} {=} \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i} \Phi (\hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {i}) \Phi \left(\mathbf {F} \exp (\boldsymbol {\xi} _ {i})\right) \hat {\mathbf {Y}} _ {i} \\ \stackrel {(1 2)} {=} \underbrace {\Gamma_ {i} \Phi (\hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {i}) \hat {\mathbf {Y}} _ {i}} _ {\hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {i + 1}} \exp \left(\underbrace {\operatorname {A d} _ {\hat {\mathbf {Y}} _ {i} ^ {- 1}} \mathbf {F} \boldsymbol {\xi} _ {i}} _ {\boldsymbol {\xi} _ {i + 1}}\right). \tag {42} \\ \end{array}
$$

By defining the new mean  $\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{i + 1}$ , the covariance of the discrepancy evolves without approximation as

$$
\boldsymbol {\Sigma} _ {i + 1} := \operatorname {E} \left[ \boldsymbol {\xi} _ {i + 1} \boldsymbol {\xi} _ {i + 1} ^ {T} \right] \stackrel {(4 2)} {=} \underbrace {\operatorname {A d} _ {\hat {\mathbf {Y}} _ {i} ^ {- 1}} \mathbf {F}} _ {:= \mathbf {A} _ {i}} \boldsymbol {\Sigma} _ {i} \left(\operatorname {A d} _ {\hat {\mathbf {Y}} _ {i} ^ {- 1}} \mathbf {F}\right) ^ {T}. \tag {43}
$$

This is remarkable as it proves that IMU noise-free equations, albeit nonlinear, preserve concentrated Gaussians on  $SE_2(3)$  and the moments evolve through closed-form formulas.

# B. Propagating Uncertainty through Noisy IMU Model

We now consider IMU noise in the form of (35). Our Lie group approach to extended poses allows transposing the developments of [5] exposed for the evolution model (4). The propagation of  $\mathbf{T}_i$  through noisy model writes

$$
\mathbf {T} _ {i + 1} = \underbrace {\boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i} \Phi (\hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {i}) \hat {\mathbf {Y}} _ {i}} _ {\hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {i + 1}} \exp \left(\underbrace {\operatorname {A d} _ {\hat {\mathbf {Y}} _ {i} ^ {- 1}} \mathbf {F} \boldsymbol {\xi} _ {i}} _ {\boldsymbol {\xi}}\right) \exp \left(\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i}\right), \tag {44}
$$

where we recover (42) with noise injected on the right. For our approach to hold, we require that  $\operatorname{E}[\pmb{\xi}_{i+1}] = \mathbf{0}_9$ , where

$$
\begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {\xi} _ {i + 1} \stackrel {(4 1)} {=} \log \left(\hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {i + 1} ^ {- 1} \mathbf {T} _ {i + 1}\right) \\ \stackrel {(4 4)} {=} \log (\exp (\boldsymbol {\xi}) \exp (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i})). \tag {45} \\ \end{array}
$$

Applying the BCH formula (16), we get

$$
\begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {\xi} _ {i + 1} = \boldsymbol {\xi} + \boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} + \frac {1}{2} \boldsymbol {\xi} ^ {\wedge} \boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} + \frac {1}{1 2} \left(\boldsymbol {\xi} ^ {\wedge} \boldsymbol {\xi} ^ {\wedge} \boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} + \boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {\wedge} \boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {\wedge} \boldsymbol {\xi}\right) \\ - \frac {1}{2 4} \boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {\text {人}} \boldsymbol {\xi} ^ {\text {人}} \boldsymbol {\xi} ^ {\text {人}} \boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} + O \left(\left\| \boldsymbol {\xi} _ {i + 1} \right\| ^ {5}\right) \tag {46} \\ \end{array}
$$

As  $\xi$  and  $\eta_{i}$  are uncorrelated, we have

$$
\operatorname {E} \left[ \boldsymbol {\xi} _ {i + 1} \right] = - \frac {1}{2 4} \boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {\wedge} \boldsymbol {\xi} ^ {\wedge} \boldsymbol {\xi} ^ {\wedge} \boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} + O \left(\left\| \boldsymbol {\xi} _ {i + 1} \right\| ^ {6}\right) \tag {47}
$$

since everything except the fourth-order term has zero mean. In the present paper we use "fourth-order" in the sense of [5] but it is important to note it corresponds to "second-order" in the original terminology of [4]. Thus, to third-order, we can safely assume that  $\operatorname{E}[\pmb{\xi}_{i+1}] = \mathbf{0}_9$ , and thus,  $\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{i+1}$  seems to be a reasonable way to compound the mean. Our goal is now to compute an approximation of  $\pmb{\Sigma}_{i+1}$ . Multiplying out  $\operatorname{E}[\pmb{\xi}_{i+1}\pmb{\xi}_{i+1}^T]$  to fourth-order (second-order in the sense of [4]), the resulting covariance is then

$$
\boldsymbol {\Sigma} _ {i + 1} \simeq \mathbf {A} _ {i} \boldsymbol {\Sigma} _ {i} \mathbf {A} _ {i} ^ {T} + \mathbf {Q} + \mathbf {S} _ {4 \mathrm {t h}}, \tag {48}
$$

where  $\mathbf{A}_i$  is defined in (43),  $\mathbf{Q}$  is the input noise covariance, and  $\mathbf{S}_{4\mathrm{th}}$  is the third- and fourth-order corrections to allow (48) to be correct to the fourth-order, see Appendix for its expression. This result is essentially the same as [4,5] but worked out for the time time-varying model (32) which is more complex than (4). In summary, to propagate an extended pose, we compute the mean using (44) and the covariance using (48).

This subsection presents a simple example of extended pose propagation. This can be viewed as a discrete-time integration of the IMU kinematic equations (22)-(24) with biases absent or perfectly known. To see the qualitative difference between the proposed second- and fourth-order methods, respectively without and with  $\mathbf{S}_{4\mathrm{th}}$ , let us propagate an extended pose many times in a row. We apply (32) for  $i = 0,\dots ,K - 1$  with acceleration about the  $x$ -axis and rotational noise about the  $z$ -axis as

$$
\hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {0} = \mathbf {I} _ {5}, \quad \boldsymbol {\xi} _ {0} \sim \mathcal {N} (\mathbf {0} _ {9}, \mathbf {0} _ {9 \times 9}),
$$

\operatorname {c o v} \left(\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {\omega}\right) = \operatorname {d i a g} (0, 0, \sigma^ {2}), \quad \operatorname {c o v} \left(\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {\mathbf {a}}\right) = \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3},

\begin{array}{l} \Sigma_ {\phi v} = - \frac {(K - 1)}{2} a \Delta t, \\ \Sigma_ {\phi p} = \frac {(K - 1) (2 K - 1)}{1 2} a \Delta t ^ {2}, \\ \Sigma_ {v v} = \frac {(K - 1) (2 K - 1)}{6} a ^ {2} \Delta t ^ {2}, \\ \Sigma_ {v p} = \frac {(K - 1) ^ {2} K}{8} a ^ {2} \Delta t ^ {3}, \\ \Sigma_ {p p} = \frac {(K - 1) (2 K - 1) (3 (K - 1) ^ {2} + 3 K - 4)}{1 2 0} a ^ {2} \Delta t ^ {4}. \\ \end{array}

3) the samples never cross a circle of radius  $112.5\mathrm{m}$ , as rotation uncertainty tends to reduce the travelled distance, and none of the approximate methods perfectly models this non-Gaussian behavior.

$$

$$

where the  $\boxplus$  retraction substitutes our  $SE_2(3)$  exponential map and is defined in Figure 4. This avoids the computation of  $3\times 3$  Jacobian but comes at the price of loosing the ability to model the "banana" shape of the actual dispersion.

We compute the best mean and covariance related to the  $SO(3)\times \mathbb{R}^6$  distribution by averaging Monte-Carlo samples

$$
\begin{array}{l} \hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {K} := \operatorname {E} [ \mathbf {T} _ {K} ] := \left[ \begin{array}{c c} \operatorname {E} [ \mathbf {R} _ {K} ] & \operatorname {E} [ \mathbf {v} _ {K} ] \quad \operatorname {E} [ \mathbf {p} _ {K} ] \\ \hline \mathbf {0} _ {2 \times 3} & \mathbf {I} _ {2} \end{array} \right], \\ \boldsymbol {\Sigma} := \operatorname {E} \left[ \left(\mathbf {T} _ {K} \boxminus \hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {K}\right) \left(\mathbf {T} _ {K} \boxminus \hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {K}\right) ^ {T} \right] \\ \simeq \frac {1}{N - 1} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {N} \left(\mathbf {T} _ {K, n} \boxminus \hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {K}\right) \left(\mathbf {T} _ {K, n} \boxminus \hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {K}\right) ^ {T}, \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\operatorname{E}[\mathbf{R}_K] \simeq 1 / N \exp \left( \sum_{n=1}^{N} \log \left( \hat{\mathbf{R}}_K^n \right) \right)$ ,  $\operatorname{E}[\mathbf{v}_K] \simeq 1 / N \sum_{n=1}^{N} \hat{\mathbf{v}}_K^n$  and  $\operatorname{E}[\mathbf{p}_K] \simeq 1 / N \sum_{n=1}^{N} \hat{\mathbf{p}}_K^n$ . 1) the covariance ellipse (orange line) is not a good fit for samples with large deviation as compared to the  $SE_2(3)$  distribution for the same experiment;  
2) the mean of the samples (orange dot) is distant from  $5\mathrm{m}$  of the noise-free estimate (red dot).

Let us explain why our  $SE_2(3)$ -based method improves on point 2). Consider the position extracted from the extended pose

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbf {p} _ {K} = \mathbf {T} _ {K} \left[ \begin{array}{l} \mathbf {0} _ {4} \\ 1 \end{array} \right] \stackrel {(4 1)} {=} \hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {K} \exp (\boldsymbol {\xi} _ {K}) \left[ \begin{array}{l} \mathbf {0} _ {4} \\ 1 \end{array} \right] \\ \stackrel {(1 6)} {=} \hat {\mathbf {p}} _ {K} + \boldsymbol {\rho} + \frac {1}{2} \phi_ {\times} \boldsymbol {\rho} + O \left(\| \boldsymbol {\xi} _ {K} \| ^ {3}\right). \tag {49} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\hat{\mathbf{p}}_K = [112.5\mathrm{m},0\mathrm{m},0\mathrm{m}]$  up to third-order, see (47). Let us compute the expectation of the position as

$$
\operatorname {E} \left[ \mathbf {p} _ {K} \right] \stackrel {(4 9)} {=} \hat {\mathbf {p}} _ {K} + \underbrace {\operatorname {E} [ \boldsymbol {\rho} ]} _ {= \mathbf {0} _ {3}} + \frac {1}{2} \underbrace {\operatorname {E} \left[ \phi_ {\times} \boldsymbol {\rho} \right]} _ {\neq \mathbf {0} _ {3}} + O \left(\| \boldsymbol {\xi} _ {K} \| ^ {3}\right) \neq \hat {\mathbf {p}} _ {K}, \tag {50}
$$

where we see a shift appear due to the correlation between orientation and position. Using the values of the covariance  $\pmb{\Sigma}_{K}$  of the  $SE_2(3)$  second-order distribution, we find  $\operatorname{E}[\mathbf{p}_K] = [107.5\mathrm{m},0\mathrm{m},0\mathrm{m}]$  which matches with the Monte-Carlo mean value (orange dot) of Figure 5.

3) Comparison with  $SO(3)\times \mathbb{R}^6$ $2^{nd}$ -Order Distribution: in practice, e.g., in an extended Kalman filter, the covariance  $\pmb{\Sigma}$  is recursively computed with second-order accuracy, i.e. similarly as (48) with  $\mathbf{S}_{4\mathrm{th}} = \mathbf{0}_{9\times 9}$ . We compute the covariance  $\pmb{\Sigma}$  with the second-order accuracy adapted to the  $SO(3)\times \mathbb{R}^6$  uncertainty representation. The covariance reflects null uncertainty along the  $x$ -axis due to linearization error, see the red "ellipse" in Figure 5, and see also Figure 1 where accelerometer noises are considered. We provide quantitative comparisons between  $SO(3)\times \mathbb{R}^6$  and  $SE_2(3)$  distributions in the context of preintegration in Section V-C.  
4) Summary: given its apparent simplicity, the latter instructive simulation evidences the  $SE_2(3)$  distribution is especially suited to extended pose uncertainty in the two following situations:

1) when initial uncertainty predominates input noise uncertainty, as the method is then exact, see formula (43);  
2) when orientation uncertainty dominates translational uncertainty, leading to a "banana"-shaped dispersion.

An interesting question is whether to choose the noise-free mean  $\hat{\mathbf{p}}_K$  or the "stochastic" one (50) for use in sensor-fusion or outlier detection algorithms based on the  $SO(3)\times \mathbb{R}^6$  distribution, e.g. [51]. Yet, it is left for future work.

$$

$$

Remarkably, we have an exact formula for the evolution of the discrepancy (55), which is rare in nonlinear estimation.

# V. ON LIE GROUP IMU PREINTEGRATION

This section first evidences the relations between the IMU preintegration on manifold [6] and our approach on the Lie group  $SE_2(3)$ . Then we describe how to compute the preintegrated factor along with its noise covariance matrix on  $SE_2(3)$ . Finally we address incorporation of bias updates in Lie exponential coordinates.

# A. Links with the IMU Preintegration on Manifold [6]

While (44) could be readily seen as a probabilistic constraint in a factor-graph, it would require to include states in a factor-graph at the high IMU rate. It indeed relates states at time  $t_i$  and  $t_{i+1} = t_i + \Delta t$ , where  $\Delta t$  is the sampling period of the IMU. Lupton and Sukkarieh [26] and then Forster et. al. [6] show that all measurements between two chosen instants  $t_i$  and  $t_j$  can be summarized in a single factor, which constrains the motion between times  $t_i$  and  $t_j$ .

Our approach differs from [6] in three ways:  $i$  ) the situation where the mathematical framework applies;  $ii$  ) the retraction used to compute Gaussian uncertainty; and  $iii$  ) how bias updates modify the preintegrated factor. Regarding  $i$ ), [6] addresses preintegration by computing factors between two arbitrary timestamps  $t_i$  and  $t_j$  as

$$
\Delta \mathbf {R} _ {i j} = \mathbf {R} _ {i} ^ {T} \mathbf {R} _ {j}, \tag {58}
$$

$$
\Delta \mathbf {v} _ {i j} = \mathbf {R} _ {i} ^ {T} \left(\mathbf {v} _ {j} - \mathbf {v} _ {i} - \mathbf {g} \Delta t _ {i j}\right), \tag {59}
$$

$$
\Delta \mathbf {p} _ {i j} = \mathbf {R} _ {i} ^ {T} \left(\mathbf {p} _ {j} - \mathbf {p} _ {i} - \mathbf {v} _ {i} \Delta t _ {i j} - \frac {1}{2} \mathbf {g} \Delta t _ {i j} ^ {2}\right), \tag {60}
$$

where  $\Delta t_{ij} := t_j - t_i = (j - i)\Delta t$ , which is specific to the model (22)-(24). Our preintegration factor between two extended poses is given as

$$
\boldsymbol {\Upsilon} _ {i j} \stackrel {(5 1)} {=} \left(\boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i j} \Phi_ {i j} \left(\boldsymbol {\mathrm {T}} _ {i}\right)\right) ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {\mathrm {T}} _ {j}, \tag {61}
$$

and is derived for flat Earth model in this section, but may be adapted to rotating Earth model, see Section VI.

The difference  $ii)$  is the retraction function used to represent uncertainty. While [6] opts for the  $SO(3)\times \mathbb{R}^6$  uncertainty

$$
\mathbf {T} := \hat {\mathbf {T}} \boxplus \boldsymbol {\xi}, \quad \boldsymbol {\xi} \sim \mathcal {N} (\mathbf {0} _ {9}, \boldsymbol {\Sigma}), \tag {62}
$$

where the  $\boxplus$  operator is defined in Figure 4, we advocate the uncertainty based on the  $SE_2(3)$  exponential map, see Section II-B, which proves better suited to propagate extended pose uncertainty (see Section IV-C).

Remark 6. In the implementation of [6] on GTSAM [24], the retraction updates the velocity in the navigation frame. Since the only difference is the use of an invertible matrix, this is in fact equivalent. We thus do not distinguish both retractions.

Finally, given a bias update  $\hat{\mathbf{b}}_i^+ \gets \hat{\mathbf{b}}_i + \delta \mathbf{b}$ , [6] addresses iii) by updating the preintegrated measurements using a first-order Taylor expansion as

$$
\Delta \hat {\mathbf {R}} _ {i j} (\hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i} ^ {+}) = \Delta \hat {\mathbf {R}} _ {i j} (\hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i}) \exp \left(\frac {\partial \Delta \hat {\mathbf {R}} _ {i j}}{\partial \mathbf {b}} | _ {\hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i}} \delta \mathbf {b} + O (\| \delta \mathbf {b} \| ^ {2})\right),
$$

$$
\Delta \hat {\mathbf {v}} _ {i j} (\hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i} ^ {+}) = \Delta \hat {\mathbf {v}} _ {i j} (\hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i}) + \frac {\partial \Delta \hat {\mathbf {v}} _ {i j}}{\partial \mathbf {b}} | _ {\hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i}} \delta \mathbf {b} + O (\| \delta \mathbf {b} \| ^ {2}),
$$

$$
\Delta \hat {\mathbf {p}} _ {i j} (\hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i} ^ {+}) = \Delta \hat {\mathbf {p}} _ {i j} (\hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i}) + \frac {\partial \Delta \hat {\mathbf {p}} _ {i j}}{\partial \mathbf {b}} | _ {\hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i}} \delta \mathbf {b} + O (\| \delta \mathbf {b} \| ^ {2}).
$$

To be consistent with our framework, we address the bias update as a first-order Taylor expansion in the Lie exponential coordinates as

$$
\hat {\mathbf {Y}} _ {i j} (\hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i} ^ {+}) = \hat {\mathbf {Y}} _ {i j} (\hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i}) \exp \left(\frac {\partial \hat {\mathbf {Y}} _ {i j}}{\partial \mathbf {b}} | _ {\hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i}} \delta \mathbf {b} + O (\| \delta \mathbf {b} \| ^ {2})\right).
$$

Remark 7. The implementation of [6] in GTSAM [24] takes into account the uncertainty in the bias estimates for preintegration, such that the preintegration covariance matrix preserves the correlation between the bias uncertainty and the preintegrated measurements uncertainty. We follow [6] for clarity of exposition. However our approach is fully compatible with accounting for the correlation between IMU navigation state and biases states, as is detailed in the supplementary material.

# B. Proposed IMU Preintegration

As in [6], we first assume the bias estimates are exact and fixed between  $t_i$  and  $t_j$  as

$$
\hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i} = \hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i + 1} = \dots = \hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {j - 1}. \tag {63}
$$

1) Preintegrating IMU Measurements: we relate the states at times  $t_i$  and  $t_j$  to the IMU measurement through

$$
\boldsymbol {\Upsilon} _ {i j} = \hat {\boldsymbol {\Upsilon}} _ {i j} \exp \left(\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i j}\right) \stackrel {(6 1)} {=} \left(\boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i j} \Phi_ {i j} \left(\mathbf {T} _ {i}\right)\right) ^ {- 1} \mathbf {T} _ {j}, \tag {64}
$$

which provides a measurement model where the noise terms of the individual inertial measurements is isolated in  $\pmb{\eta}_{ij}$  and where  $\hat{\Upsilon}_{ij}$  is integrated with inertial measurements through (53), which can be substituted by the integration (29)-(31). Indeed, our approach has the same preintegration measurement as [6] but the uncertainty is encoded in Lie exponential coordinates.

$$

that corresponds to integrating the uncertainty of an extended pose without initial uncertainty. We resort to (48) to compute the covariance with second-order accuracy as

\boldsymbol {\Sigma} _ {i (j + 1)} = \operatorname {A d} _ {\hat {\mathbf {Y}} _ {j} ^ {- 1}} \mathbf {F} \boldsymbol {\Sigma} _ {i j} \left(\operatorname {A d} _ {\hat {\mathbf {Y}} _ {j} ^ {- 1}} \mathbf {F}\right) ^ {T} + \mathbf {Q}, \tag {66}
$$

and starting from initial condition  $\Sigma_{ii} = \mathbf{0}_{9\times 9}$

\operatorname {c o v} \left(\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {\boldsymbol {\omega}}\right) = \alpha \sigma_ {\boldsymbol {\omega}} ^ {2} \mathbf {I} _ {3}, \quad \operatorname {c o v} \left(\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {i} ^ {\mathbf {a}}\right) = \alpha \sigma_ {\mathbf {a}} ^ {2} \mathbf {I} _ {3},
$$

where  $\sigma_{\omega}$  and  $\sigma_{\mathbf{a}}$  are the same as in simulations of [6], i.e.  $7 \cdot 10^{-4} \mathrm{rad/(s\sqrt{Hz})}$  and  $1.9 \cdot 10^{-2} \mathrm{m/(s^2\sqrt{Hz})}$ , and with  $\alpha$  a scaling parameter for testing purposes.

The factor covariance is computed using four methods:

1)  $SE_2(3)$ , which computes the covariance based on (66) with accuracy up to second-order;  
2)  $SO(3)\times \mathbb{R}^6$  [6,44], same as the method 1) but adapted to uncertainty on  $SO(3)\times \mathbb{R}^6$ , i.e. with uncertainty representation of [6] and integration scheme of [44];  
3)  $SE(3)\times \mathbb{R}^3$ , same as the method 1) but adapted to uncertainty on  $SE(3)\times \mathbb{R}^3$  (this method has not appeared before to our knowledge);  
4)  $SE_2(3)$  (Monte-Carlo), which is a slow yet accurate approach. At each time step, we draw a large number  $M = 10^6$  of random samples  $\pmb{\xi}_{ij}^{m}$  and  $\pmb{\eta}_j^m$ , propagate the resulting states, and compute the covariance as  $\pmb{\Sigma}_{i(j + 1)} = \frac{1}{M - 1}\sum_{m = 1}^{M}\pmb{\xi}^{m}\pmb{\xi}^{mT}$  with

$$
\mathbf {T} ^ {m} = \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {j} \Phi \left(\hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {i j} \exp (\boldsymbol {\xi} _ {i j} ^ {m})\right) \hat {\mathbf {Y}} _ {j} \exp (\boldsymbol {\eta} _ {j} ^ {m}),
$$

and  $\pmb{\xi}^{m} = \log (\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{i(j + 1)}^{-1}\mathbf{T}^{m})$ . It requires, e.g.,  $50M = 5 \cdot 10^{7}$  samples for one preintegration factor of length  $\Delta t_{ij} = 5\mathrm{s}$ . It indicates the best recursive distribution based on  $SE_2(3)$  one may obtain.

To evidence the fact that our approach is more consistent, we compute the average Normalized Estimation Error Squared

(NEES) as

$$
\mathrm {N E E S} = \frac {1}{9 N} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {N} \mathbf {e} _ {n} ^ {T} \boldsymbol {\Sigma} ^ {- 1} \mathbf {e} _ {n},
$$

where the error is defined as

$$
\mathbf {e} _ {n} ^ {S E _ {2} (3)} = \log \left(\hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {i j} ^ {- 1} \hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {i j} ^ {n}\right), \text {o r} \mathbf {e} _ {n} ^ {S O (3) \times \mathbb {R} ^ {6}} = \mathbf {T} _ {i j} ^ {n} \boxminus \hat {\mathbf {T}} _ {i j}.
$$

We sample  $N = 10^{6}$  Monte-Carlo noisy preintegrations  $\mathbf{T}_{ij}^n$  for each factor  $\mathbf{T}_{ij}$ , where for each Monte-Carlo realization  $n$ , we compute one realization of each noise  $\eta_{i},\dots,\eta_{j - 1}$ . If the NEES is higher than 1, the approach is overconfident, if it stays below 1, it is conservative. One usually wants to avoid overconfident estimates.

Results are displayed in Figure 7 for three noise scale parameters and increasing values of the preintegration time. We observe that:

1)  $SE_2(3)$  distribution is the most consistent approach. This becomes visible as the preintegration time increases for each level of IMU noises;  
2)  $SO(3)\times \mathbb{R}^6$  distribution poorly approximates long preintegration uncertainty, where the median value is close to the  $33\%$  percentile while the  $67\%$  percentile is much larger. It indicates that there exists some types of trajectory (that correspond to high rotation motions) where the performances of the  $SO(3)\times \mathbb{R}^6$  on manifold method might degrade. This confirms the results of Section IV-C where we see the average error should be not zero and that linearization errors do occur;  
3)  $SE(3)\times \mathbb{R}^3$  obtains similar results as  $SO(3)\times \mathbb{R}^6$ , and we see that despite the use of  $SE(3)$  which is known to describe well uncertainty associated with poses, the full  $SE_{2}(3)$  structure is desirable in the context of IMUs;  
4) all methods degrade as noise and preintegration time increase because the preintegrated measurements become non-Gaussian. Interestingly, the linearization errors of our  $SE_2(3)$ -based scheme (as opposed to Monte-Carlo estimate) are only visible for high noise ( $\alpha = 10$ ) and sufficiently high preintegration time.

# D. Bias Correction via Lie Exponential First-Order Updates

This subsection shows the impact of computing first-order bias corrections using the representation of errors based on exponential coordinates of  $SE_2(3)$ . In the context of preintegration, given a bias update  $\hat{\mathbf{b}}_i^+ \gets \hat{\mathbf{b}}_i + \delta \mathbf{b}$ , one needs to compute how the preintegrated quantities  $\hat{\Upsilon}_{ij}$  change. Assume we have computed  $\hat{\Upsilon}_{ij}(\hat{\mathbf{b}}_i)$  corresponding to bias  $\hat{\mathbf{b}}_i$  and let  $\hat{\Upsilon}_{ij}(\hat{\mathbf{b}}_i^+)$  denote the measurement associated to new bias estimation. We define the first-order update as

$$
\hat {\mathbf {Y}} _ {i j} \left(\hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i} ^ {+}\right) = \hat {\mathbf {Y}} _ {i j} \left(\hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i}\right) \exp \left(\frac {\partial \hat {\mathbf {Y}} _ {i j}}{\partial \mathbf {b}} \mid_ {\hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i}} \delta \mathbf {b} + O \left(\| \delta \mathbf {b} \| ^ {2}\right)\right). \tag {67}
$$

The derivation of the Jacobian is similar to the one we use to express the measurements as a large value plus a small perturbation. We first define the variation for one time step

$$
\begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {\Upsilon} _ {i} \left(\hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i} ^ {+}\right) = \boldsymbol {\Upsilon} _ {i} \left(\hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i} + \delta \mathbf {b}\right) \\ = \hat {\mathbf {Y}} _ {i} (\hat {\mathbf {b}} _ {i}) \exp \left(\mathbf {G} _ {i} \delta \mathbf {b} + O \left(\| \delta \mathbf {b} \| ^ {2}\right)\right) \tag {68} \\ \end{array}
$$

# VI. IMU PREINTEGRATION ON ROTATING EARTH

This section provides exact closed-form expressions for computing the right-hand side of the factors (58)-(60) when taking into account the Earth rotation, Coriolis acceleration, and centrifugal acceleration. We obtain results that are independent of the uncertainty representation and as such may be used in previous preintegration formalisms [6,44]. This allows applying factor-graph based optimization techniques to military and civilian applications that require localization over long time scales based on accurate inertial sensors.

# A. IMU Equations with Rotating Earth

Accounting for Earth rotation, (22)-(24) become [49]

$$
\dot {\mathbf {R}} = - \boldsymbol {\Omega} _ {\times} \mathbf {R} + \mathbf {R} \left(\boldsymbol {\omega} ^ {m} - \mathbf {b} ^ {\boldsymbol {\omega}} - \boldsymbol {\eta} ^ {\boldsymbol {\omega}}\right) _ {\times}, \tag {71}
$$

$$
\dot {\mathbf {v}} = \mathbf {R} \left(\mathbf {a} ^ {m} - \mathbf {b} ^ {\mathbf {a}} - \boldsymbol {\eta} ^ {\mathbf {a}}\right) + \mathbf {g} - 2 \boldsymbol {\Omega} _ {\times} \mathbf {v} - \boldsymbol {\Omega} _ {\times} ^ {2} \mathbf {p}, \tag {72}
$$

$$
\dot {\mathbf {p}} = \mathbf {v}, \tag {73}
$$

where the Earth rotation vector

$$
\Omega = \underbrace {\text {E a r t h r a t e}} _ {\simeq 7. 2 9 2 \times 1 0 ^ {- 5} \mathrm {r a d / s}} \times \left[ \begin{array}{c} \cos (\text {l a t i t u d e}) \\ 0 \\ - \sin (\text {l a t i t u d e}) \end{array} \right] \in \mathbb {R} ^ {3} \tag {74}
$$

is written in the local, i.e. geographic (north, east, down), reference frame, where the Earth rate is approximately  $15\mathrm{deg / h}$ . The term  $-2\Omega_{\times}\mathbf{v}$  is called Coriolis acceleration while the term  $-\Omega_{\times}^{2}\mathbf{p}$  is called centrifugal acceleration. To be perfectly accurate, this second term is the varying part of the centrifugal acceleration, which actually writes  $-\Omega_{\times}^{2}(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{p}_{0})$  with  $\mathbf{p}_0$  a point of the Earth rotation axis. But expanding the parenthesis we obtain a constant term  $\Omega_{\times}^{2}\mathbf{p}_{0}$  which can be simply added to  $\mathbf{g}$ . And this is already the case: the  $\mathbf{g}$  of approximate value  $9.81\mathrm{m / s^2}$  we are familiar with is actually the sum of the Newton gravitation force and the centrifugal acceleration due to Earth rotation. Hence the residual term  $-\Omega_{\times}^{2}\mathbf{p}$ .

# B. Revisiting IMU Equations with Rotating Earth

Model (71)-(73) does seemingly not lend itself to application of the form (25). However, let us introduce the variable

$$
\mathbf {T} ^ {\prime} := \left[ \begin{array}{c c} \mathbf {R} & \mathbf {v} + \boldsymbol {\Omega} _ {\times} \mathbf {p} \\ \hline \mathbf {0} _ {2 \times 3} & \mathbf {I} _ {2} \end{array} \right]. \tag {75}
$$

This trick allows embedding the auxiliary state  $\mathbf{T}_t^\prime$  into the form of (25) as

$$
\mathbf {T} _ {t} ^ {\prime} = \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {t} ^ {\prime} \Phi_ {t} \left(\mathbf {T} _ {0} ^ {\prime}\right) \mathbf {Y} _ {t}, \tag {76}
$$

where  $\Upsilon_{t}$  is the same as in (25) and  $\Gamma_t^\prime$  is obtained after solving the differential equations

$$
\dot {\boldsymbol {\Gamma}} _ {t} ^ {\prime} := \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} \dot {\boldsymbol {\Gamma}} ^ {\mathbf {R}} & \dot {\boldsymbol {\Gamma}} ^ {\mathbf {v}} & \dot {\boldsymbol {\Gamma}} ^ {\mathbf {p}} \\ \hline \mathbf {0} _ {2 \times 3} & \mathbf {I} _ {2} \end{array} \right], \tag {77}
$$

where

$$
\dot {\Gamma} ^ {R} = - \Omega_ {\times} \Gamma^ {R}, \quad \dot {\Gamma} ^ {v} = g - \Omega_ {\times} \Gamma^ {v}, \quad \dot {\Gamma} ^ {p} = \Gamma^ {v} - \Omega_ {\times} \Gamma^ {p},
$$

initialized at  $\Gamma^{\mathbf{R}} = \mathbf{I}_3$ ,  $\Gamma^{\mathbf{v}} = \mathbf{0}_3$ ,  $\Gamma^{\mathbf{p}} = \mathbf{0}_3$ , and that do not involve the state, see proofs in [8] and supplementary materials.

We solve for  $\mathbf{T}_t^\prime$  and apply our framework for preintegration as follows. First, we define an exact discrete model

$$
\mathbf {T} _ {j} ^ {\prime} = \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i j} ^ {\prime} \Phi_ {i j} \left(\mathbf {T} _ {i} ^ {\prime}\right) \boldsymbol {\Upsilon} _ {i j}, \tag {78}
$$

between instants  $t_i$  and  $t_j$ , as performed in Section IV-D, that leads to

$$
\mathbf {R} _ {j} = \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i j} ^ {\mathbf {R}} \mathbf {R} _ {i} \Delta \mathbf {R} _ {i j}, \tag {79}
$$

$$
\mathbf {v} _ {j} = \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i j} ^ {\mathbf {v}} + \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i j} ^ {\mathbf {R}} \left(\mathbf {R} _ {i} \Delta \mathbf {v} _ {i j} + \mathbf {v} _ {i} + \boldsymbol {\Omega} _ {\times} \mathbf {p} _ {i}\right) - \boldsymbol {\Omega} _ {\times} \mathbf {p} _ {j}, \tag {80}
$$

$$
\mathbf {p} _ {j} = \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i j} ^ {\mathbf {p}} + \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i j} ^ {\mathbf {R}} \left(\mathbf {R} _ {i} \Delta \mathbf {p} _ {i j} + \left(\mathbf {v} _ {i} + \boldsymbol {\Omega} _ {\times} \mathbf {p} _ {i}\right) \Delta t _ {i j} + \mathbf {p} _ {i}\right), \tag {81}
$$

where  $\Delta \mathbf{R}_{ij}$ ,  $\Delta \mathbf{v}_{ij}$ ,  $\Delta \mathbf{p}_{ij}$  are yet given through (29)-(31) while  $\Gamma_{ij}^{\mathbf{R}}$ ,  $\Gamma_{ij}^{\mathbf{v}}$ ,  $\Gamma_{ij}^{\mathbf{p}}$  are defined as

$$
\boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i j} ^ {\mathbf {R}} = \exp (- \Delta t _ {i j} \boldsymbol {\Omega}), \tag {82}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i j} ^ {\mathbf {v}} = \mathcal {J} _ {- \Delta t _ {i j}} \boldsymbol {\Omega} \Delta t _ {i j} \mathbf {g}, \tag {83}
$$

$$
\mathbf {\Gamma} _ {i j} ^ {\mathrm {P}} = \left(\frac {\Delta t ^ {2}}{2} \mathbf {I} _ {3} + a \boldsymbol {\Omega} _ {\times} + b \boldsymbol {\Omega} _ {\times} ^ {2}\right) \mathbf {g}, \tag {84}
$$

with  $\phi = \| \pmb {\Omega}\|$ $a = \phi^{-3}(\phi \Delta t_{ij}\cos (\phi \Delta t_{ij}) - \sin (\phi \Delta t_{ij}))$  and  $b = \phi^{-4}(\frac{\phi^2\Delta t_{ij}^2}{2} -\cos (\phi \Delta t_{ij}) - \phi \Delta t_{ij}\sin (\Delta t_{ij}\phi) + 1)$  To express preintegration factors as function of states, we compute

$$
\boldsymbol {\Upsilon} _ {i j} \stackrel {(7 8)} {=} \left(\boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i j} ^ {\prime} \Phi_ {i j} \left(\boldsymbol {\mathbf {T}} _ {i} ^ {\prime}\right)\right) ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {\mathbf {T}} _ {j} ^ {\prime}, \tag {85}
$$

see the similarity with (61), that we finally develop as

$$
\Delta \mathbf {R} _ {i j} \stackrel {(7 9)} {=} \left(\boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i j} ^ {\mathbf {R}} \mathbf {R} _ {i}\right) ^ {T} \mathbf {R} _ {j}, \tag {86}
$$

$$
\Delta \mathbf {v} _ {i j} \stackrel {(8 0)} {=} \mathbf {R} _ {i} ^ {T} \left(\boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i j} ^ {\mathbf {R T}} \left(\mathbf {v} _ {j} + \boldsymbol {\Omega} _ {\times} \mathbf {p} _ {j} - \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i j} ^ {\mathbf {v}}\right) - \mathbf {v} _ {i} - \boldsymbol {\Omega} _ {\times} \mathbf {p} _ {i}\right), \tag {87}
$$

$$
\Delta \mathbf {p} _ {i j} \stackrel {(8 1)} {=} \mathbf {R} _ {i} ^ {T} \left(\boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i j} ^ {\mathbf {R T}} \left(\mathbf {p} _ {j} - \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i j} ^ {\mathbf {p}}\right) - \left(\mathbf {v} _ {i} + \boldsymbol {\Omega} _ {\times} \mathbf {p} _ {i}\right) \Delta t _ {i j} - \mathbf {p} _ {i}\right). \tag {88}
$$

To summarize, preintegrating IMU with rotating Earth consists in substituting (58)-(60) by (86)-(88), and leaving the covariance noise computation unchanged, as Coriolis and centrifugal accelerations leave the left-hand side of the preintegrated factors unchanged, and thus the quantity  $\mathbf{Y}_{ij}$  in (85) is yet defined in (64). These results are obtained independently from a choice for uncertainty representation, and as such they may also be used in current implementations such as [6].

We confirm our results with a numerical example, see Figure 9, where we preintegrate bias- and noise-free IMU measurements for a long car trajectory. Similarly as in Section V-C, we differentiate the ground-truth trajectory of the sequence 1 of our real experiments (see Section VII) to obtain true inertial measurements. Then the ground-truth is recomputed with model (71)-(73) to remove integration errors. We compute the error when neglecting the rotation of the Earth  $(\Omega = 0_{3})$  versus our approach with  $\Delta t_{ij} = 5\mathrm{s}$ . We see no error at the beginning of the sequence as the car is static. Then the error committed when neglecting the rotating Earth grows, whereas our approach is exact.

# VII. REAL EXPERIMENTS

This section addresses sensor-fusion of a high-grade IMU with relative translations provided by a LiDAR in a fixed-lag smoother for long-term navigation. Building on the preintegration with rotating Earth and Coriolis effect formulas developed in Section VI, we implement our loosely-coupled approach with the GTSAM factor-graph library [24].

We gathered more than  $150\mathrm{km}$  and four hours of data divided into six sequences. The vehicle acquires data around the R&T centre of SafranTech<sup>2</sup> located at Magny-Les-Hameaux, France. The car embeds a high-grade IMU manufactured by Safran Electronics and Defense, whose increments are acquired at  $100\mathrm{Hz}$ , and a Velodyne LiDAR VLP32C which is mounted on top of the car. Table 1 indicates the IMU noise specifications that we set in our algorithms and which are two orders of magnitude lower than those used in the simulations of [6]. The 3D laser scans between keyframes are preprocessed to obtain relative transformations using scan matching algorithms. The vehicle is finally equipped with a RTK-GPS antenna which outputs positions and yaws that we consider as ground-truth. This dataset is challenging regarding the length of the sequences, the car velocity (up to  $30\mathrm{m/s}$ ), and the presence of many bumps, sharp curves and roundabouts on the way.

# A. Compared Methods & Algorithm Setting

Owing the precision of the gyroscopes, relative orientation between LiDAR's point clouds does not bring additional relevant information, and we focus only on the relative translations returned by the LiDAR. We compare three approaches for estimating the vehicle pose using the IMU and the LiDAR:

1) LiDAR, which is the compound of the relative poses estimated by the scan matching algorithms as in (1);  
2) LiDAR-IMU w/o Coriolis, which is the fusion between the relative translations given by the scan matching algorithms and the inertial signals, without considering rotating Earth and Coriolis force, i.e. assuming  $\Omega = 0_3$ ;  
3) LiDAR-IMU w Coriolis, same as method 2), but where rotating Earth and Coriolis force are considered and  $\Omega$  defined with the latitude at which each sequence starts (48.73 deg).

The LiDAR-aided approaches are described as follows. We implement a fixed-lag smoother based on iSAM2 [9] from

the GTSAM library [24]. Our GTSAM fork<sup>3</sup> computes the bias update correction with Lie exponential coordinates (67), preintegrates with rotating Earth and Coriolis force (79)-(81), and leaves the retraction of the extended pose (a.k.a. navigation state in [24]) unchanged. Biases are estimated along with extended poses through CombinedImuFactor, and initialized with the beginning of each sequence, where the car is static. Each approach performs odometry estimation, i.e., it estimates the car trajectory relative to its starting position without using global information from GNSS or LiDAR loop-closure.

Scan matching algorithms return relative orientation and translation, where the level of rotation uncertainty between LiDAR scans is much higher than the gyro's uncertainty (assuming the gyro bias is known, the gyro only drifts of less than a few degrees in one hour, which is lower than the Earth rotation rate). Therefore, we fed the factor-graph with IMU preintegrated factors, relative translation factors, and zero upward velocity factor with a relatively small standard deviation of  $1\mathrm{m / s}$  to prevent the upward velocity from drifting, as used, e.g., in [54].

We set the smoother with a lag of  $20\mathrm{s}$ , insert each factor at  $4\mathrm{Hz}$ , and define its noise parameters as follows. Then the covariance of the discrete-time noise  $\eta^{\mathrm{d}}$  is computed as a function of the sampling rate and relates to the continuous-time spectral noise  $\eta^{\mathrm{c}}$  via  $\operatorname{cov}(\eta^{\mathrm{d}}) = \operatorname{cov}(\eta^{\mathrm{c}}) / \Delta t$  [55]. Relative translations are given with an uncertainty of  $20\mathrm{cm}$  in each direction, and we make the translation factor more robust during high motions with a Huber loss. Finally, the initial orientation and position are provided from the ground-truth, and the initial velocity is null.

# C. Result Analysis & Discussion

Table 2 provides numerical results for the two first sets of evaluation metrics, and Figure 11 displays results for the third set. We observe:

1) the LiDAR estimates highly efficiently the distances and stays accurate in straight lines. However, it has difficulties in areas with tight curves, roundabouts (where visual ambiguity might play a role), or when the car is moving fast;  
2) as the LiDAR loses accuracy in decisive but rare moments such as roundabouts, the short-term results of Table 2 are correct, and one mainly tries to improve the long-term metrics;  
3) only relative translation information is sufficient to obtain a robust loosely-coupled estimation from the IMU and the LiDAR. This solves most of the problems encountered by the LiDAR during sharp curves and roundabouts;  
4) the two LiDAR inertial approaches have similar results for the shortest sequences 3 and 5, see Table 2. Indeed, for a small sequence, Coriolis force is negligible and the Earth rotation rate may be inserted in bias;  
5) taking into account Coriolis increases accuracy for long distances, see the long-term metrics in Table 2, where the one that considers Coriolis is about one hundred meter away from the ground after more than one hour of odometry, without GNSS or LiDAR loop-closure;  
6) Figure 11 shows approximately linear grow of the error with respect to time. Considering rotating Earth and Coriolis effect improves the translation metric from  $130\mathrm{m}$  to  $103\mathrm{m}$ , i.e. an improvement of  $21\%$ , with subsequences of length  $15\mathrm{min}$ .

These results show how beneficial it is to consider rotating Earth and Coriolis force in IMU preintegration theory.

However, our experiments do not show the advantage of the Lie exponential update of the bias. Indeed, the differences between the proposed bias update and the standard one remains below, e.g., parameter tuning. The benefit of bias update with exponential Lie update does not necessary require long sequences but more accurate localization systems and larger preintegration times.

In our experiments, we take into account the rotation of the Earth but not its curvature (otherwise it would be necessary to consider the variations of longitude, latitude, ellipsoidal altitude, and transport rate). This point is justified by the relatively small area covered by the trajectories, and the fact that we are estimating relative trajectories. We anticipate the proposed approach would provide even more improvements for long-term navigation with absolute measurements and long distances, e.g. for a plane, a drone, or autonomous underwater vehicles equipped with accurate inertial sensors.

# VIII. CONCLUSION

This paper presents some generic techniques to associate uncertainty to an extended pose (three dimensional orientation, velocity and position of a rigid body) and shows how to propagate associated uncertainties with fourth-order accuracy in

noise variables and second order in the associated covariances. The framework additionally provides an elegant mathematical approach that brings further maturity to the theory of preintegration on manifold. It unifies flat and rotating Earth IMU equations within a single framework, hence providing extensions of the theory of preintegration with Coriolis force. The method compares favorably against state-of-the-art in extensive simulations, and has been validated for one hour long car navigation in an efficient fixed-lag smoother that fuses IMU and relative translations provided by a LiDAR.

Looking forward, we believe these techniques open up for novel implementations of factor-graph based methods to the context of long term inertial-aided navigation systems, hence genuine industrial navigation systems. Finally, the theory could find application in other problems requiring detailed bookkeeping of extended pose uncertainties. Indeed, our approach with fine uncertainty representation may prove decisive for finely detecting GNSS outliers with preintegration [51], or when we search to accurately define the uncertainty of distant relative extended poses in an (extended) pose-graph. This is outlined by the example of Section IV-C where our approach improves the mean and the uncertainty of the estimates of existing approaches it is compared to.

A.  $SO(3)$  &  $SE_2(3)$  Closed-Form Expressions

Let  $\phi \in \mathbb{R}^3$  and  $\mathbf{R}\in SO(3)$ . The exponential, logarithm, left-Jacobian and inverse left-Jacobian of  $SO(3)$  are given as

$$
\exp (\phi) = \mathbf {I} _ {3} + \frac {\sin \phi}{\phi} \phi_ {\times} + \frac {1 - \cos \phi}{\phi^ {2}} \phi_ {\times} ^ {2}, \tag {89}
$$

$$
\log (\mathbf {R}) = \frac {\varphi}{2 \sin \varphi} \left(\mathbf {R} - \mathbf {R} ^ {T}\right) ^ {\vee}, \tag {90}
$$

$$
\mathcal {J} _ {\phi} = \mathbf {I} _ {3} + \frac {1 - \cos \phi}{\phi^ {2}} \phi_ {\times} + \frac {\phi - \sin \phi}{\phi^ {3}} \phi_ {\times} ^ {2}, \tag {91}
$$

$$
\mathcal {J} _ {\phi} ^ {- 1} = \mathbf {I} _ {3} - \frac {1}{2} \phi_ {\times} + \left(\phi^ {- 2} + \frac {1 + \cos \phi}{2 \phi \sin \phi}\right) \phi_ {\times} ^ {2}, \tag {92}
$$

where  $\vee$  is the linear inverse operator of  $\wedge$ ,  $\phi = \| \phi \|$  and  $\varphi = \cos^{-1}(\frac{\operatorname{trace}(\mathbf{R}) - 1}{2})$ . Let  $\pmb{\xi} \in \mathbb{R}^{9}$  and  $\mathbf{T} \in SE_2(3)$ .

The exponential and logarithm of  $SE_2(3)$  are computed in overloaded operators as

$$
\exp (\boldsymbol {\xi}) = \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} \exp (\phi) & \mathcal {J} _ {\phi} \boldsymbol {\nu} & \mathcal {J} _ {\phi} \boldsymbol {\rho} \\ \mathbf {0} _ {3} ^ {T} & 1 & 0 \\ \mathbf {0} _ {3} ^ {T} & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right], \log (\mathbf {T}) = \left[ \begin{array}{c} \log (\mathbf {R}) \\ \mathcal {J} _ {\log (\mathbf {R})} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {\nu} \\ \mathcal {J} _ {\log (\mathbf {R})} ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {\rho} \end{array} \right]. \tag {93}
$$

The Jacobian for  $SE_2(3)$  and its inverse are derived from those for  $SE(3)$  introduced in [5] as

$$
\mathcal {J} _ {\boldsymbol {\xi}} := \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} \mathcal {J} _ {\phi} & \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} & \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} \\ \mathcal {Q} _ {\phi , \nu} & \mathcal {J} _ {\phi} & \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} \\ \mathcal {Q} _ {\phi , \rho} & \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} & \mathcal {J} _ {\phi} \end{array} \right], \tag {94}
$$

$$
\mathcal {J} _ {\boldsymbol {\xi}} ^ {- 1} := \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} \mathcal {I} _ {\phi} ^ {- 1} & \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} & \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} \\ - \mathcal {J} _ {\phi} ^ {- 1} \mathcal {Q} _ {\phi , \nu} \mathcal {J} _ {\phi} ^ {- 1} & \mathcal {J} _ {\phi} ^ {- 1} & \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} \\ - \mathcal {J} _ {\phi} ^ {- 1} \mathcal {Q} _ {\phi , \rho} \mathcal {J} _ {\phi} ^ {- 1} & \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} & \mathcal {I} _ {\phi} ^ {- 1} \end{array} \right], \tag {95}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathcal {Q} _ {\phi , \nu} := \frac {1}{2} \boldsymbol {\nu} _ {\times} + \frac {\phi - \sin \phi}{\phi^ {3}} \left(\boldsymbol {\phi} _ {\times} \boldsymbol {\nu} _ {\times} + \boldsymbol {\nu} _ {\times} \phi_ {\times} + \boldsymbol {\phi} _ {\times} \boldsymbol {\nu} _ {\times} \phi_ {\times}\right) \\ + \frac {\phi^ {2} + 2 \cos \phi - 2}{\phi^ {4}} \left(\phi_ {\times} \phi_ {\times} \nu_ {\times} + \nu_ {\times} \phi_ {\times} \phi_ {\times} - 3 \phi_ {\times} \nu_ {\times} \phi_ {\times}\right) \\ + \frac {2 \phi - 3 \sin \phi + \phi \cos \phi}{2 \phi^ {5}} \left(\phi_ {\times} \nu_ {\times} \phi_ {\times} \phi_ {\times} + \phi_ {\times} \phi_ {\times} \nu_ {\times} \phi_ {\times}\right), \tag {96} \\ \end{array}
$$

and  $\mathcal{Q}_{\phi,\rho}$  is defined similarly as (96), replacing  $\pmb{\nu}$  by  $\pmb{\rho}$ .
