# Understanding Self-supervised Learning with Dual Deep Networks

Yuandong Tian $^{1}$  Lantao Yu $^{2}$  Xinlei Chen $^{1}$  Surya Ganguli $^{1,2}$

# Abstract

We propose a novel theoretical framework to understand contrastive self-supervised learning (SSL) methods that employ dual pairs of deep ReLU networks (e.g., SimCLR). First, we prove that in each SGD update of SimCLR with various loss functions, including simple contrastive loss, soft Triplet loss and InfoNCE loss, the weights at each layer are updated by a covariance operator that specifically amplifies initial random selectivities that vary across data samples but survive averages over data augmentations. To further study what role the covariance operator plays and which features are learned in such a process, we model data generation and augmentation processes through a hierarchical latent tree model (HLTM) and prove that the hidden neurons of deep ReLU networks can learn the latent variables in the HLTM, despite the fact that the network receives no direct supervision from these unobserved latent variables. This leads to a provable emergence of hierarchical features through the amplification of initially random selectivities through contrastive SSL. Extensive numerical studies justify our theoretical findings. Code is released in https://github.com/facebookresearch/luckmatters/tree/master_SSL.

# 1. Introduction

While self-supervised learning (SSL) has achieved great empirical success across multiple domains, including computer vision (He et al., 2020; Goyal et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2020a; Grill et al., 2020; Misra & Maaten, 2020; Caron et al., 2020), natural language processing (Devlin et al., 2018), and speech recognition (Wu et al., 2020; Baevski & Mohamed, 2020; Baevski et al., 2019), its theoretical understanding remains elusive, especially when multi-layer nonlinear deep networks are involved (Bahri et al., 2020). Unlike supervised learning (SL) that deals with labeled data, SSL learns meaningful structures from randomly initialized networks without human-provided labels.

In this paper, we propose a systematic theoretical analysis of SSL with deep ReLU networks. Our analysis imposes no parametric assumptions on the input data distribution and is applicable to state-of-the-art SSL methods that typically involve two parallel (or dual) deep ReLU networks during training (e.g., SimCLR (Chen et al., 2020a), BYOL (Grill et al., 2020), etc). We do so by developing an analogy between SSL and a theoretical framework for analyzing supervised learning, namely the student-teacher setting (Tian, 2020; Allen-Zhu & Li, 2020; Lampinen & Ganguli, 2018; Saad & Solla, 1996), which also employs a pair of dual networks. Our results indicate that SimCLR weight updates at every layer are amplified by a fundamental positive semi definite (PSD) covariance operator that only captures feature variability across data points that survive averages over data augmentation procedures designed in practice to scramble semantically unimportant features (e.g. random image crops, blurring or color distortions (Falcon & Cho, 2020; Kolesnikov et al., 2019; Misra & Maaten, 2020; Purushwalkam & Gupta, 2020)). This covariance operator provides a principled framework to study how SimCLR amplifies initial random selectivity to obtain distinctive features that vary across samples after surviving averages over data-augmentations.

While the covariance operator is a mathematical object that is valid for any data distribution and augmentations, we further study its properties under specific data distributions and augmentations. We first start with a simple one-layer case where two 1D objects undergo 1D translation, then study a fairly general case when the data are generated by a hierarchical latent tree model (HLTM), which can be regarded as an abstract conceptual model for object compositionality () in computer vision. In this case, training deep ReLU networks on the data generated by the HLTM leads to the emergence of learned representations of the latent variables in its intermediate layers, even if these intermediate nodes have never been directly supervised by the unobserved and inaccessible latent variables. This shows that in theory, useful hidden features can automatically emerge by contrastive self-supervised learning.

To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to provide a systematic theoretical analysis of modern SSL methods with deep ReLU networks that elucidates how both data and data augmentation, drive the learning of internal representations across multiple layers.

# 2. Related Works

In addition to SimCLR and BYOL, many concurrent SSL frameworks exist to learn good representations for computer vision tasks. MoCo (He et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2020b) keeps a large bank of past representations in a queue as the slow-progressing target to train from. DeepCluster (Caron et al., 2018) and SwAV (Caron et al., 2020) learn the representations by iteratively or implicitly clustering on the current representations and improving representations using the cluster label. (Alwassel et al., 2019) applies similar ideas to multi-modality tasks. Contrastive Predictive Coding (Oord et al., 2018) learns the representation by predicting the future of a sequence in the latent space with autoregressive models and InfoNCE loss. Contrastive MultiView Coding (Tian et al., 2019) uses multiple sensory channels (called "views") of the same scene as the positive pairs and independently sampled views as the negative pairs to train the model. Recently, (Li et al., 2020) moves beyond instance-wise pairs and proposes to use prototypes to construct training pairs that are more semantically meaningful.

In contrast, the literature on the (theoretical) analysis of SSL is sparse. (Wang & Isola, 2020) shows directly optimizing the alignment/uniformity of the positive/negative pairs leads to comparable performance against contrastive loss. (Arora et al., 2019b) proposes an interesting analysis of how contrastive learning aids downstream classification tasks, given assumptions about data generation. (Lee et al., 2020) analyzes how learning pretext tasks could reduce the sample complexity of the downstream task and (Tosh et al., 2020) analyzes contrastive loss with multi-view data in the semi-supervised setting, with different generative models. However, they either work on linear models or treat deep models as a black-box function approximators with sufficient capacity. In comparison, we incorporate self-supervision, deep models, contrastive loss, data augmentation and generative models together into the same theoretical framework, and make an attempt to understand how and what intermediate features emerge in modern SSL architectures with deep models that achieve SoTA.

# 3. Overall framework

Notation. Consider an  $L$ -layer ReLU network obeying  $\pmb{f}_l = \psi (\tilde{\pmb{f}}_l)$  and  $\tilde{\pmb{f}}_l = W_l\pmb{f}_{l - 1}$  for  $l = 1,\dots L$ . Here  $\tilde{\pmb{f}}_l$  and  $\pmb{f}_l$  are  $n_l$  dimensional pre-activation and activation vectors in layer  $l$ , with  $\pmb{f}_0 = \pmb{x}$  being the input and  $\pmb{f}_L = \tilde{\pmb{f}}_L$  the output (no ReLU at the top layer).  $W_{l}\in \mathbb{R}^{n_{l}\times n_{l - 1}}$  are the weight matrices, and  $\psi (\pmb {u})\coloneqq \max (\pmb {u},0)$  is the elementwise ReLU nonlinearity. We let  $\mathcal{W}\coloneqq \{W_l\}_{l = 1}^L$  be all network weights. We also denote the gradient of any loss function with respect to  $\pmb{f}_l$  by  $\pmb{g}_l\in \mathbb{R}^{n_l}$ , and the derivative of the output  $\pmb{f}_L$  with respect to an earlier pre-activation  $\tilde{\pmb{f}}_l$  by the Jacobian matrix  $J_{l}(\pmb {x};\mathcal{W})\in \mathbb{R}^{n_{L}\times n_{l}}$ , as both play key roles in backpropagation (Fig. 1(b)).

An analogy between self-supervised and supervised learning: the dual network scenario. Many recent successful approaches to self-supervised learning (SSL), including SimCLR (Chen et al., 2020a), BYOL (Grill et al., 2020) and MoCo (He et al., 2020), employ dual "Siamese" pairs (Koch et al., 2015) of such networks (Fig. 1(b)). Each network has its own set of weights  $\mathcal{W}_1$  and  $\mathcal{W}_2$ , receives respective inputs  $x_1$  and  $x_2$  and generates outputs  $f_{1,L}(x_1; \mathcal{W}_1)$  and  $f_{2,L}(x_2; \mathcal{W}_2)$ . The pair of inputs  $\{x_1, x_2\}$  can be either positive or negative, depending on how they are sampled. For a positive pair, a single data point  $x$  is drawn from the data distribution  $p(\cdot)$ , and then two augmented views  $x_1$  and  $x_2$  are drawn from a conditional augmentation distribution  $p_{\mathrm{aug}}(\cdot | x)$ . Possible image augmentations include random crops, blurs or color distortions, that ideally preserve semantic content useful for downstream tasks. In contrast, for a negative pair, two different data points  $x, x' \sim p(\cdot)$  are sampled, and then each are augmented independently to generate  $x_1 \sim p_{\mathrm{aug}}(\cdot | x)$  and  $x_2 \sim p_{\mathrm{aug}}(\cdot | x')$ . For SimCLR, the dual networks have tied weights with  $\mathcal{W}_1 = \mathcal{W}_2$ , and a loss function is chosen to encourage the representation of positive (negative) pairs to become similar (dissimilar).

Our fundamental goal is to analyze the mechanisms governing how contrastive SSL methods like SimCLR lead to the emergence of meaningful intermediate features, starting from random initializations, and how these features depend on the data distribution  $p(\pmb{x})$  and augmentation procedure  $p_{\mathrm{aug}}(\cdot|\pmb{x})$ . Interestingly, the analysis of supervised learning (SL) often employs a similar dual network scenario, called the teacher-student setting (Tian, 2020; Allen-Zhu & Li, 2020; Lampinen & Ganguli, 2018; Saad & Solla, 1996), where  $\mathcal{W}_2$  are the ground truth weights of a fixed teacher network, which generates outputs in response to random inputs. These input-output pairs constitute training data for the first network, which is a student network. Only the student network's weights  $\mathcal{W}_1$  are trained to match the target outputs provided by the teacher. This yields an interesting mathematical parallel between SL, in which the teacher is fixed and only the student evolves, and SSL, in which both the teacher and student evolve with potentially different dynamics. This mathematical parallel opens the door to using techniques from SL (e.g., (Tian, 2020)) to analyze SSL.

Gradient of  $\ell_2$  loss for dual deep ReLU networks. As seen above, the (dis)similarity of representations between a pair of dual networks plays a key role in both SSL and SL. We thus consider minimizing a simple measure of dissimilarity, the squared  $\ell_2$  distance  $r\coloneqq \frac{1}{2}\| f_{1,L} - f_{2,L}\| ^2$  between the final outputs  $f_{1,L}$  and  $f_{2,L}$  of two multi-layer ReLU networks with weights  $\mathcal{W}_1$  and  $\mathcal{W}_2$  and inputs  $\pmb{x}_{1}$  and  $\pmb{x}_2$ . This gradient formula will be used to analyze multiple contrastive loss functions in Sec. 4. Without loss of generality, we only analyze the gradient w.r.t  $\mathcal{W}_1$ . # 4. The Covariance Operator

As discussed above, SimCLR (Chen et al., 2020a) employs both positive and negative input pairs, and a symmetric network structure with  $\mathcal{W}_1 = \mathcal{W}_2 = \mathcal{W}$ . Let  $\{\pmb{x}_1,\pmb{x}_+\}$  be a positive input pair from  $\pmb{x}$ , and let  $\{\pmb{x}_1,\pmb{x}_{k - }\}$  for  $k = 1,\dots ,H$  be  $H$  negative pairs. These input pairs induce corresponding squared  $\ell_2$  distances in output space,  $r_{+}:= \frac{1}{2}\| f_{1,L} - f_{+,L}\| _2^2$ , and  $r_{k - }:= \frac{1}{2}\| f_{1,L} - f_{k - ,L}\| _2^2$ .

In this paper, we consider three different contrastive losses:

(1) the simple contrastive loss  $L_{\mathrm{simp}} \coloneqq r_+ - r_-$ ,  
(2) (Soft) Triplet loss  $L_{\mathrm{tri}}^{\tau} \coloneqq \tau \log (1 + e^{(r_{+} - r_{-} + r_{0}) / \tau})$  (here  $r_0 \geq 0$  is the margin). Note that  $\lim_{\tau \to 0} L_{\mathrm{tri}}^{\tau} = \max (r_{+} - r_{-} + r_{0}, 0)$  (Schroff et al., 2015)  
(3) InfoNCE loss  $L_{\mathrm{nce}}^{\tau}$  (Oord et al., 2018):

$$
L _ {\mathrm {n c e}} ^ {\tau} \left(r _ {+}, \left\{r _ {k -} \right\} _ {k = 1} ^ {H}\right) := - \log \frac {e ^ {- r _ {+} / \tau}}{e ^ {- r _ {+} / \tau} + \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {H} e ^ {- r _ {k -} / \tau}} \tag {2}
$$

Note that when  $\| \pmb{u}\| _2 = \| \pmb {v}\| _2 = 1$ , we have  $-\frac{1}{2}\| \pmb {u} - \pmb {v}\| _2^2 =$ $\mathrm{sim}(\pmb {u},\pmb {v}) - 1$  where  $\mathrm{sim}(\pmb {u},\pmb {v}) = \frac{\pmb{u}^{\mathrm{T}}\pmb{v}}{\|\pmb{u}\|_2\|\pmb{v}\|_2}$ , and Eqn. 2 reduces to what the original SimCLR uses (the term  $e^{-1 / \tau}$  cancels out).

For simplicity, we move the analysis of the final layer  $\ell_2$  normalization to Appendix. When there is no  $\ell_2$  normalization, the goal of our analysis is to show that useful weight components grow exponentially in the gradient updates.

We first note one interesting common property:

Theorem 2 (Common Property of Contrastive Losses). For loss functions  $L \in \{L_{\mathrm{simp}}, L_{\mathrm{tri}}^{\tau}, L_{\mathrm{nce}}^{\tau}\}$ , we have  $\frac{\partial L}{\partial r_+} > 0$ ,  $\frac{\partial L}{\partial r_{k-}} < 0$  for  $1 \leq k \leq H$  and  $\frac{\partial L}{\partial r_+} + \sum_{k=1}^{H} \frac{\partial L}{\partial r_{k-}} = 0$ .

With Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, we now present our first main contribution of this paper: the gradient in SimCLR is governed by a positive semi-definite (PSD) covariance operator at any layer  $l$ :

Theorem 3 (Covariance Operator for  $L_{\mathrm{simp}}$ ). With large batch limit,  $W_{l}$ 's update under  $L_{\mathrm{simp}}$  is  $W_{l}(t + 1) = W_{l}(t) + \alpha \Delta W_{l}(t)$  ( $\alpha$  is the learning rate), where

$$
\operatorname {v e c} \left(\Delta W _ {l} (t)\right) = \mathrm {O P} _ {l} ^ {\text {s i m p}} (\mathcal {W}) \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {l} (t)\right). \tag {3}
$$

Here  $\mathrm{OP}_l^{\mathrm{simp}}(\mathcal{W}):= \mathbb{V}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim p(\cdot)}[\bar{K}_l(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{W})]\in \mathbb{R}^{n_l n_{l - 1}\times n_l n_{l - 1}}$  is the covariance operator for  $L_{\mathrm{simp}}$ ,  $\bar{K}_l(\boldsymbol {x};\mathcal{W})\coloneqq \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}'\sim p_{\mathrm{aug}}(\cdot |\boldsymbol {x})}[K_l(\boldsymbol{x'};\mathcal{W})]$  is the expected connection under the augmentation distribution, conditioned on datapoint  $\boldsymbol{x}$ .

Intuitively, the covariance operator  $\mathrm{OP}_l(\mathcal{W})$  is a time-varying PSD matrix over the entire training procedure. Therefore, all its eigenvalues are non-negative and at any time  $t$ ,  $W_{l}$  is most amplified along its largest eigenmodes. Intuitively,  $\mathrm{OP}_l(\mathcal{W})$  ignores different views of the same sample  $\pmb{x}$  by averaging over the augmentation distribution to compute  $\bar{K}_l(\pmb {x})$ , and then computes the expected covariance of this augmentation averaged connection with respect to the data distribution  $p(\pmb {x})$ . Thus, at all layers, any variability in the connection across different data points, that survives augmentation averages, leads to weight amplification. This amplification of weights by the PSD data covariance of an augmentation averaged connection constitutes a fundamental description of SimCLR learning dynamics for arbitrary data and augmentation distributions and holds at every layer of arbitrarily deep ReLU networks.

Given this result for  $L_{\mathrm{simp}}$ , one might ask whether the same property holds for more realistic loss functions like  $L_{\mathrm{tri}}$  and  $L_{\mathrm{nce}}$  that are extensively used in prior works. The answer is yes. Define weighted covariance for matrices  $X$  and  $Y$ :

$$
\operatorname {C o v} ^ {\xi} [ X, Y ] := \mathbb {E} [ \xi (X, Y) (X - \mathbb {E} [ X ]) (Y - \mathbb {E} [ Y ]) ^ {\intercal} ] \tag {4}
$$

and  $\mathbb{V}^{\xi}[X] := \operatorname{Cov}^{\xi}[X, X]$ . Note that  $\operatorname{Cov}[X, Y]$  means  $\xi(\cdot) \equiv 1$ . Then we have the following theorem:

Theorem 4 (Covariance Operator for  $L_{\mathrm{tri}}^{\tau}$  and  $L_{\mathrm{nce}}^{\tau}$ $(H = 1$  , single negative pair)). Let  $r\coloneqq \frac{1}{2}\| f_L(\pmb {x}) -$ $\pmb {f}_L(\pmb {x}^\prime)\| _2^2$  . The covariance operator  $\mathrm{OP}_l(\mathcal{W})$  has the following form:

$$
\mathrm {O P} _ {l} (\mathcal {W}) = \frac {1}{2} \mathbb {V} _ {\boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime} \sim p (\cdot)} ^ {\xi} \left[ \bar {K} _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}) - \bar {K} _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right) \right] + \theta \tag {5}
$$

where the pairwise weight  $\xi (r)$  takes the following form for different loss functions:

$$
\xi (r) = \left\{ \begin{array}{c c} 1 & L = L _ {\text {s i m p}} \\ \frac {e ^ {- (r - r _ {0}) / \tau}}{1 + e ^ {- (r - r _ {0}) / \tau}} & L = L _ {\text {t r i}} ^ {\tau} \\ \frac {1}{\tau} \frac {e ^ {- r / \tau}}{1 + e ^ {- r / \tau}} & L = L _ {\text {n c e}} ^ {\tau} \end{array} \right. \tag {6}
$$

and  $\theta := \mathcal{O}(\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}'}\left[\sqrt{r(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}')}\sigma_{\mathrm{aug}}(\boldsymbol{x})\right] + \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left[\sigma_{\mathrm{aug}}^2 (\boldsymbol {x})\right])$

is the residue term.  $\sigma_{\mathrm{aug}}^2 (\pmb {x})\coloneqq \mathrm{tr}\mathbb{V}_{\pmb{x}^{\prime \prime}\sim p_{\mathrm{aug}}(\cdot |\pmb {x})}[\pmb {f}_L(\pmb{x}^{\prime \prime})]$  and tr is the trace of a matrix. For  $L_{\mathrm{simp}},\theta \equiv 0$

Note that Theorem 4 is a strict extension of Theorem 3: with constant pairwise weight  $\xi(r(\pmb{x},\pmb{x}')) \equiv 1$ ,  $\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{V}_{\pmb{x},\pmb{x}'}[\bar{K}_l(\pmb{x}) - \bar{K}_l(\pmb{x}')] = \mathbb{V}_\pmb{x}[\bar{K}_l(\pmb{x})]$ .

Intuitively, the difference between  $L_{\mathrm{simp}}$  and  $L_{\mathrm{tri}}$ ,  $L_{\mathrm{nce}}$  is that the last two put an emphasis on distinctive samples  $\pmb{x}$  and  $\pmb{x}'$  whose representations  $f_{1,L}$  and  $f_{2,L}$  are close (i.e., small  $r(\pmb{x},\pmb{x}')$ ). This makes sense since the goal of the contrastive learning is to learn a representation that separates distinct samples.

# 4.1. Discussion and Remarks

The residue term  $\theta$ . The residue term in Theorem 4 is related to the variance of the output representation within data augmentation,  $\mathrm{tr}\mathbb{V}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime \prime}\sim p_{\mathrm{aug}}(\cdot |\boldsymbol{x})}[f_L(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime \prime})]$ . For  $L_{\mathrm{simp}}$ , the expression for covariance operator is exact and  $\theta = 0$ . While for  $L_{\mathrm{tri}}^{\tau}$  and  $L_{\mathrm{nce}}^{\tau}$ , this term is nonzero, we expect it to shrink during the training so that these two losses drive learning more and more through a PSD operator at each layer  $l$ . Alternatively, we could construct a specific loss function whose covariance operator follows Eqn. 57 exactly but with  $\theta \equiv 0$ :

$$
L ^ {\tau , \text {e x a c t}} = \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {H} \operatorname {S t o p G r a d} \left(\xi^ {\tau} \left(r _ {k}\right)\right) \left(r _ {+} - r _ {k -}\right) \tag {7}
$$

where  $r_k = r(\pmb{x}, \pmb{x}_k)$  is the distance of two unaugmented distinct data points  $\pmb{x}$  and  $\pmb{x}_k$  whose augmentation gives  $\pmb{x}_+$  and  $\pmb{x}_k$ . It is easy to check that  $L^{\tau, \text{exact}}$  satisfies the

condition  $\frac{\partial L}{\partial r_{+}} + \sum_{k=1}^{H} \frac{\partial L}{\partial r_{k-}} = 0$ . In Sec. 7, we show that using  $L_{\mathrm{nce}}^{\tau,\mathrm{exact}}$ , the downstream performance in STL-10 is comparable with using regular  $L_{\mathrm{nce}}^{\tau}$  loss. This justifies that Theorem 4 indeed separates the most important part of gradient update from less important ones in the residue.

Difference from Neural Tangent Kernels (NTK). Note our covariance operator is a completely different mathematical object than the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) (Jacot et al., 2018; Arora et al., 2019a). The NTK is defined in the sample space and is full-rank if samples are distinct. For very wide networks (compared to sample size), the NTK seldom changes during training and leads to a convex optimization landscape. On the other hand, the covariance operator  $\mathbb{V}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left[\bar{K}_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})\right]$  is defined per layer on any data distribution and network of finite width, and does not grow with sample size.  $\mathbb{V}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left[\bar{K}_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})\right]$  may change over the entire training procedure but always remains PSD, leading to many interesting behaviors beyond learning with a fixed kernel. Furthermore, while NTK has nothing to do with data augmentation,  $\mathbb{V}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left[\bar{K}_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})\right]$  is tied to data augmentation and SSL architectures.

# 4.2. More general loss functions

For more general loss functions in which  $\frac{\partial L}{\partial r_{+}} + \sum_{k=1}^{H} \frac{\partial L}{\partial r_{k-}} = \beta \neq 0$ , we have a corollary:

Corollary 1. SimCLR under  $L_{\mathrm{simp}}^{\beta} \coloneqq (1 + \beta)r_{+} - r_{-}$  has the following gradient update rule at layer  $l$ :

$$
\operatorname {v e c} \left(\Delta W _ {l}\right) = \mathrm {O P} _ {l} \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {l}\right) = \left(- \beta \mathrm {E V} _ {l} + \mathrm {V E} _ {l}\right) \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {l}\right) \tag {8}
$$

where  $\mathrm{EV}_l$  and  $\mathrm{VE}_l$  are intra-augmentation and inter-augmentation covariance operators at layer  $l$ :

$$
\operatorname {E V} _ {l} := \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {x} \sim p (\cdot)} \left[ \mathbb {V} _ {\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime} \sim p _ {\text {a u g}} (\cdot | \boldsymbol {x})} \left[ K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right) \right] \right] \tag {9}
$$

$$
\mathrm {V E} _ {l} := \mathbb {V} _ {\boldsymbol {x} \sim p (\cdot)} \left[ \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime} \sim p _ {\text {a u g}} (\cdot | \boldsymbol {x})} \left[ K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right) \right] \right] \tag {10}
$$

In our experiments, we found that  $\beta < 0$  accelerates the training a bit relative to plain SimCLR, possibly due to the fact that  $\mathrm{OP}_l$  remains a PSD matrix.

# 5. Feature Emergence through Covariance Operator Based Amplification

The covariance operator in Theorem 3-4 applies to arbitrary data distributions and augmentations. While this conclusion is general, it is also abstract. To understand what feature representations emerge, we study learning under more specific assumptions on the generative process underlying the data.

Assumption 1. We make two assumptions under the generative paradigm of (Fig. 2):

(1) The input  $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}(z_0, z')$  is generated by two groups of latent variables, class/sample-specific latents  $z_0$  and nuisance latents  $z'$ .  
(2) Data augmentation changes  $z'$  while preserving  $z_0$ .

For brevity of analysis, we use simple loss  $L_{\mathrm{simp}}$  and Theorem 3. Since  $\pmb{x} = \pmb{x}(z_0,z')$ , the covariance operator can be represented using expectations over  $z_0$  and  $z'$ :

\begin{array}{l} \mathrm {O P} _ {l} = \mathbb {V} _ {\boldsymbol {x} \sim p (\cdot)} [ \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime} \sim p _ {\text {a u g}} (\cdot | \boldsymbol {x})} [ K _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}) ] ] \\ = \mathbb {V} _ {z _ {0}} \left[ \mathbb {E} _ {z ^ {\prime} | z _ {0}} \left[ K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} \left(z _ {0}, z ^ {\prime}\right)\right) \right] \right] = \mathbb {V} _ {z _ {0}} \left[ \bar {K} _ {l} \left(z _ {0}\right) \right] \\ \end{array}
$$

We leave the analysis of  $L_{\mathrm{nce}}$  and  $L_{\mathrm{tri}}$  as a future work. At a high-level, they work under similar principles.

In this setting, we first show that a linear neuron performs dimensionality reduction within an augmentation preserved subspace. We then consider how nonlinear neurons with local receptive fields (RFs) can learn to detect simple objects. Finally, we extend our analysis to deep ReLU networks exposed to data generated by a hierarchical latent tree model (HLTM), proving that, with sufficient over-parameterization, there exist lucky nodes at initialization whose activation is correlated with latent variables underlying the data, and that SimCLR amplifies these initial lucky representations during learning.

# 5.1. SSL and the single neuron: illustrative examples

# A single linear neuron performs dimensionality reduction in a subspace preserved by data augmentation.

For a single linear neuron  $(L = 1, n_{L} = 1)$ , the connection in Def. 1 is simply  $K_{1}(\pmb{x}) = \pmb{x}$ . Now imagine that the input space  $\pmb{x}$  can be decomposed into the direct sum of a semantically relevant subspace, and its orthogonal complement, which corresponds to a subspace of nuisance features. Suppose the augmentation distribution  $p_{\mathrm{aug}}(\cdot|\pmb{x})$  is obtained by multiplying  $\pmb{x}$  by a random Gaussian matrix

that acts only in the nuisance subspace, thereby identically preserving the semantic subspace. Then the augmentation averaged connection  $\bar{K}_1(\pmb{x}) = Q^s\pmb{x}$  where  $Q^s$  is a projection operator onto the semantic subspace. In essence, only the projection of data onto the semantic subspace survives augmentation averaging, as the nuisance subspace is scrambled. Then  $\mathrm{OP} = \mathbb{V}_{\pmb{x}}[\bar{K}_1(\pmb{x})] = Q^s\mathbb{V}_{\pmb{x}}[\pmb{x}]Q^{s\top}$ . Thus the covariance of the data distribution, projected onto the semantic subspace, governs the growth of the weight vector  $W_{1}$ , demonstrating SimCLR on a single linear neuron performs dimensionality reduction within a semantic subspace preserved by data augmentation.

A single linear neuron cannot detect localized objects. We now consider a generative model in which data vectors can be thought of as images of objects of the form  $\pmb{x}(z_0, z')$  where  $z_0$  is an important latent semantic variable denoting object identity and  $z'$  is a nuisance latent representing its spatial location. The augmentation procedure scrambles position while preserving object identity (Fig. 3):

$$
\boldsymbol {x} \left(z _ {0}, z ^ {\prime}\right) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \boldsymbol {e} _ {z ^ {\prime}} + \boldsymbol {e} _ {\left(z ^ {\prime} + 1\right) \bmod d} & z _ {0} = 1 \\ \boldsymbol {e} _ {z ^ {\prime}} + \boldsymbol {e} _ {\left(z ^ {\prime} + 2\right) \bmod d} & z _ {0} = 2, \end{array} \right. \tag {11}
$$

Specifically,  $0 \leq z' \leq d - 1$  denotes  $d$  discrete translational object positions on a periodic ring and  $z_0 \in \{1,2\}$  denotes two possible objects 11 and 101. The distribution is uniform both over objects and positions:  $p(z_0,z') = \frac{1}{2d}$ . Augmentation shifts the object to a uniformly random position via  $p_{\mathrm{aug}}(z'|z_0) = 1 / d$ . For a single linear neuron  $K_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \boldsymbol{x}$ , and the augmentation-averaged connection is  $\bar{K}_1(z_0) = \frac{2}{d}\mathbf{1}$ , and is actually independent of object identity  $z_0$  (both objects activate two pixels at any location). Thus  $\mathrm{OP}_1 = \mathbb{V}_{z_0}\left[\bar{K}_1(z_0)\right] = 0$  and no learning happens.

We next show both a local RF and nonlinearity can rescue this unfortunate situation.

A local RF alone does not help. With the same generative model, now consider a linear neuron with a local RF of width 2. Within the RF only four patterns can arise: 00, 01, 10, 11. Taking the expectation over  $z'$  given  $z_0$  yields  $\bar{K}_1(z_0 = 1) = \frac{1}{d} [\pmb{x}_{11} + \pmb{x}_{01} + \pmb{x}_{10} + (d - 3)\pmb{x}_{00}]$  and  $\bar{K}_1(z_0 = 2) = \frac{1}{d} [2\pmb{x}_{01} + 2\pmb{x}_{10} + (d - 4)\pmb{x}_{00}]$ . Here,  $\pmb{x}_{11} \in \mathbb{R}^2$  denotes pattern 11. This yields (here  $\pmb{u} \coloneqq$ )

$$
\boldsymbol {x} _ {1 1} + \boldsymbol {x} _ {0 0} - \boldsymbol {x} _ {0 1} - \boldsymbol {x} _ {1 0}):
$$

$$
\mathrm {O P} _ {1} = \mathbb {V} _ {z _ {0}} \left[ \bar {K} _ {1} \left(z _ {0}\right) \right] = \frac {1}{4 d ^ {2}} \boldsymbol {u} \boldsymbol {u} ^ {\intercal} \tag {12}
$$

and  $\mathrm{OP}_1\in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}$  since the RF has width 2. Note that the signed sum of the four pattern vectors in  $\pmb{u}$  actually cancel, so that  $\pmb {u} = \pmb{0}$ , implying  $\mathrm{OP}_1 = 0$  and no learning happens.

A nonlinear neuron with local RF can learn to detect object selective features. With a ReLU neuron with weight vector  $\pmb{w}$ , from Def. 1, the connection is now  $K_{1}(\pmb{x},\pmb{w}) = \psi^{\prime}(\pmb{w}^{\top}\pmb{x})\pmb{x}$ . Suppose at initialization,  $\pmb{w}(t)$  happens to be selective for a single pattern  $\pmb{x}_{p}$  (where  $p \in \{00,01,10,11\}$ ), i.e.,  $\pmb{w}(t)^{\top}\pmb{x}_{p} > 0$  and  $\pmb{w}(t)^{\top}\pmb{x}_{p'} < 0$  for  $p' \neq p$ . The augmentation averaged connection is then  $\bar{K}_{1}(z_{0}) \propto \pmb{x}_{p}$  where the proportionality constant depends on object identity  $z_{0}$ . Since this averaged connection varies with object identity  $z_{0}$  for all  $p$ , the covariance operator  $\mathrm{OP}_1$  is nonzero and is given by  $\mathbb{V}_{z_0}\left[\bar{K}_1(z_0)\right] = c_p\pmb{x}_p\pmb{x}_p^{\top}$  where  $c_{p} > 0$  is some constant. By Theorem 3, the dot product  $\pmb{x}_p^{\top}\pmb{w}(t)$  grows over time:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {x} _ {p} ^ {\intercal} \boldsymbol {w} (t + 1) = \boldsymbol {x} _ {p} ^ {\intercal} \left(I _ {2 \times 2} + \alpha c _ {p} \boldsymbol {x} _ {p} \boldsymbol {x} _ {p} ^ {\intercal}\right) \boldsymbol {w} (t) \tag {13} \\ = \left(1 + \alpha c _ {p} \| \boldsymbol {x} _ {p} \| ^ {2}\right) \boldsymbol {x} _ {p} ^ {\intercal} \boldsymbol {w} _ {j} (t) > \boldsymbol {x} _ {p} ^ {\intercal} \boldsymbol {w} _ {j} (t) > 0 \\ \end{array}
$$

Thus the learning dynamics amplifies the initial selectivity to the object selective feature vector  $\boldsymbol{x}_p$  in a way that cannot be done with a linear neuron. Note this argument also holds with bias terms and initial selectivity for more than one pattern. Moreover, with a local RF, the probability of weak initial selectivity to some local object sensitive features is high, and we may expect amplification of such weak selectivity in real neural network training, as observed in other settings (Williams et al., 2018).

# 6. Deep ReLU SSL training with Hierarchical Latent Tree Models (HLTM)

Here we describe a general Hierarchical Latent Tree Model (HLTM) of data, and the structure of a multilayer neural network that learns from this data.

Motivation. The HLTM is motivated by the hierarchical structure of our world in which objects may consist of parts, which in turn may consist of subparts. Moreover the parts and subparts may be in different configurations in relation to each other in any given instantiation of the object, or any given subpart may be occluded in some views of an object.

The Generative Model. The HLTM is a very simple toy model that represents a highly abstract mathematical version of such a hierarchical structure. It consists of a tree structured generative model of data (see Fig. 4). At the top of the tree (i.e. level  $L$ ), a single categorical latent variable  $z_0$  takes one of  $m_0$  possible integer values in  $\{0, \dots, m_0 - 1\}$ , with a prior distribution  $\mathbb{P}(z_0)$ . One can roughly think of the value of  $z_0$  as denoting the identity of one of  $m_0$  possible objects. At level  $L - 1$  there is a set of

This set is indexed by  $\mu$  and each latent variable  $z_{\mu}$  is itself a categorical variable that takes one of  $m_{\mu}$  values in  $\{0, \dots, m_{\mu} - 1\}$ . Roughly we can think of each latent variable  $z_{\mu}$  as corresponding to a part, and the different values of  $z_{\mu}$  reflect different configurations or occlusion states of that part. The transition matrix (conditional probability)  $\mathbb{P}(z_{\mu}|z_0) \in \mathbb{R}^{m_0 \times m_{\mu}}$  can roughly be thought of as collectively reflecting the distribution over the presence or absence, as well as configurational and occlusional states of each part  $\mu$ , conditioned on object identity  $z_0$ . This process can continue onwards to describe subparts of parts, until layer  $l = 0$  (the "pixel" level). All the latent variables at  $l = 0$  are now visible and form a signal  $\boldsymbol{x}(z_0, z')$  received by the deep network for SSL training.

Data Augmentation. Given a sample  $\pmb{x} = \pmb{x}(z_0, z')$ , data augmentation involves resampling all  $z_{\mu}$  (which are  $z'$  in Fig. 4), while fixing the root  $z_0$ . This models augmentations as changing part configurations while keeping object identity  $z_0$  fixed.

The neural network. We now consider the multi-layer ReLU network that learns from data generated from HLTM (right hand side of Fig. 4). For simplicity let  $L = 2$ . The neural network has a set of input neurons that are in one to one correspondence with the pixels or visible variables  $z_{\nu}$  that arise at the leaves of the HLTM, where  $l = 0$ . For any given object  $z_0$  at layer  $l = 2$ , and its associated parts states  $z_{\mu}$  at layer  $l = 1$ , and visible feature values  $z_{\nu}$  at layer  $l = 0$ , the input neurons of the neural network receive only the visible feature values  $z_{\nu}$  as real analog inputs. Thus the neural network does not have direct access to the latent variables  $z_0$  and  $z_{\mu}$  that generate these visible variables.

The objective. Under this setting, one key question is: what do the hidden units of the neural network learn? In particular, can the network learn some hidden unit  $j$  whose activation  $f_{j}$  correlates well with the value of a latent variable  $z_{\mu}$ ? Or more precisely, does  $\mathbb{E}[f_j|z_\mu ]$  correlate strongly with  $z_{\mu}$  even if  $j$  never receives any direct supervision from  $z_{\mu}$  during SSL training? In this paper, we make a first attempt to address this question in a simplified setting.

# 6.1. Symmetric Binary HLTM (SB-HLTM)

To ease the analysis, we consider a simpler version of HLTM: symmetric binary HLTM. At layer  $l$ , we have latent binary variables  $\{z_{\mu}\}$ , where  $\mu \in \mathcal{Z}_l$  indexes different latent variables and each  $z_{\mu} \in \{0,1\}$ . The top-most latent variable is  $z_0$ . Following the tree structure, for  $\mu \in \mathcal{Z}_l$  and  $\nu_1, \nu_2 \in \mathcal{Z}_{l-1}$ , conditional independence holds:  $\mathbb{P}(z_{\nu_1}, z_{\nu_2} | z_{\mu}) = \mathbb{P}(z_{\nu_1} | z_{\mu}) \mathbb{P}(z_{\nu_2} | z_{\mu})$ . For  $\mathbb{P}(z_{\nu} | z_{\mu})$ , we assume it is symmetric: for  $\mu \in \mathcal{Z}_l$  and  $\nu \in \mathcal{Z}_{l-1}$ :

$$
\mathbb {P} \left(z _ {\nu} = 1 \mid z _ {\mu} = 1\right) = \mathbb {P} \left(z _ {\nu} = 0 \mid z _ {\mu} = 0\right) = (1 + \rho_ {\mu \nu}) / 2 \tag {14}
$$

where the polarity  $\rho_{\mu \nu} \in [-1, 1]$  measures how informative  $z_{\mu}$  is. If  $\rho_{\mu \nu} = \pm 1$  then there is no stochasticity in the top-down generation process. If  $\rho_{\mu \nu} = 0$ , then there is no information in the downstream latents and the posterior of  $z_{0}$  given the observation  $\mathbf{x}$  can only be uniform. See Appendix for more general cases.

The final sample  $\pmb{x}$  is a collection of all visible leaf variables (Fig. 4), and thus depends on all latent variables. Corresponding to the hierarchical tree model, each neural network node  $j \in \mathcal{N}_l$  maps to a unique  $\mu = \mu(j) \in \mathcal{Z}_l$ . Let  $\mathcal{N}_{\mu}$  be all nodes that map to  $\mu$ . While in the pixel level, there is a 1-1 correspondence between the children  $\nu$  of a subpart  $\mu$  and the pixel, in the hidden layer, more than one neuron could correspond to  $z_{\mu}$  and thus  $|N_{\mu}| > 1$ , which is a form of over-parameterization. We further let  $\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ch}}$  denote the subset of nodes that provide input to nodes in  $\mathcal{N}_{\mu}$ . For  $j \in \mathcal{N}_{\mu}$ , its activation  $f_j$  only depends on the value of  $z_{\mu}$  and its descendant latent variables, through input  $\pmb{x}$ . Define  $v_j(z_{\mu}) := \mathbb{E}_z [f_j | z_{\mu}]$  as the expected activation conditioned on  $z_{\mu}$ . See Tbl. 1 for a summary of symbols.

# 6.1.1. LUCKY NODES AT INITIALIZATION

We mainly study the following question: for a latent variable  $z_{\mu}$  at some intermediate layer, is there a node  $j$  in the deep ReLU network at the corresponding layer that correlates strongly with  $z_{\mu}$ ? In binary HLTM, this is means asking whether the selectivity  $s_j \coloneqq (v_j(1) - v_j(0)) / 2$  is high after training. If  $|s_j|$  is large (or highly selective), then the node  $j$  changes its behavior drastically for  $z_{\mu} = 0$  or 1, and thus  $j$  is highly correlated with the latent variable  $z_{\mu}$ . We show this arises over training in two steps.

Second, we show training strengthens this weak initial selectivity. We compute covariance operator  $\mathrm{OP}_{\mu} = \mathbb{V}_{z_0}[\bar{K}_{\mu}(z_0)]$  at different fully-connected part (Fig. 5(b)), indexed by latent variable  $z_{\mu}$ . Here  $\bar{K}_{\mu}(z_0) = \mathbb{E}_{z'}\left[f_{\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ch}}}\otimes J_{\mu}^{\intercal}|z_0\right]$ . Here we assume  $J_{\mu}$  is a constant matrix and mainly check the term  $\mathbb{E}_{z'}\left[f_{\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ch}}}|z_0\right]$ , which turns out to have a nice close form.

Theorem 6 (Activation covariance in SB-HLTM).  $\mathbb{V}_{z_0}[\mathbb{E}_{z'}\left[f_{\mathcal{N}_\mu^{\mathrm{ch}}}|z_0\right]] = o_\mu \pmb {a}_\mu \pmb {a}_\mu^\intercal$  Here  $o_{\mu}\coloneqq \rho_{0\mu}^{2}(1 - \rho_{0}^{2})$

Theorem 6 suggests when  $\rho_{0\mu}$  and  $\| \pmb{a}_{\mu}\|$  are large, the covariance  $\mathrm{OP}_{\mu} = o_{\mu}\pmb{a}_{\mu}\pmb{a}_{\mu}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes J_{\mu}^{\mathsf{T}}J_{\mu}$  has large magnitude and training is faster. Note that  $\| \pmb{a}_{\mu}\|$  is large when the children are highly selective (large  $|s_k|$ ) and/or the magnitude of the polarity  $|\rho_{\mu \nu}|$  is large (i.e., the top-down generation process is more deterministic).

In this case,  $P_{0\mu}$  becomes uniform, making  $\mathrm{OP}_{\mu}$  small. Thus training in SSL is faster at the top layers where the covariance operators have larger magnitude.

# 8. Conclusion and Future Works

In this paper, we propose a novel theoretical framework to study self-supervised learning (SSL) paradigms that consist of dual deep ReLU networks. We analytically show that the weight update at each intermediate layer is governed by a covariance operator, a PSD matrix that amplifies weight directions that align with variations across data points which

survive averages over augmentations. We show how the operator interacts with multiple generative models that generate the input data distribution, including a simple 1D model with circular translation and hierarchical latent tree models. Experiments support our theoretical findings.

To our best knowledge, our work is the first to open the blackbox of deep ReLU neural networks to bridge contrastive learning, (hierarchical) generative models, augmentation procedures, and the emergence of features and representations. We hope this work opens new opportunities and perspectives for the research community.

# A. Background and Basic Setting (Section 3)

# A.1. Lemmas

Definition 2 (reversibility). A layer  $l$  is reversible if there is a  $G_{l}(\pmb{x};\mathcal{W}) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{l} \times n_{l-1}}$  so that the pre-activation at layer  $l$  satisfies  $\tilde{\pmb{f}}_{l}(\pmb{x};\mathcal{W}) = G_{l}(\pmb{x};\mathcal{W})\tilde{\pmb{f}}_{l-1}(\pmb{x};\mathcal{W})$  and backpropagated gradient after nonlinearity  $\tilde{\pmb{g}}_{l-1} = G_{l}^{\top}(\pmb{x};\mathcal{W})Q_{l}^{\top}(\pmb{x};\mathcal{W})\tilde{\pmb{g}}_{l}$  for some matrix  $Q_{l}(\pmb{x};\mathcal{W}) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{l} \times n_{l}}$ . A network is reversible if all layers are.

Note that many different kinds of layers have this reversible property, including linear layers (MLP and Conv) and (leaky) ReLU nonlinearity. For linear layers, at layer  $l$ , we have:

$$
G _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}; \mathcal {W}) = W _ {l}, \quad Q _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}; \mathcal {W}) \equiv I _ {n _ {l} \times n _ {l}} \tag {16}
$$

For multi-layer ReLU network, for each layer  $l$ , we have:

$$
G _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}; \mathcal {W}) = W _ {l} D _ {l - 1} (\boldsymbol {x}; \mathcal {W}), \quad Q _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}; \mathcal {W}) \equiv I _ {n _ {l} \times n _ {l}} \tag {17}
$$

where  $D_{l - 1}\in \mathbb{R}^{n_{l - 1}\times n_{l - 1}}$  is a binary diagonal matrix that encodes the gating of each neuron at layer  $l - 1$ . The gating  $D_{l - 1}(\pmb {x};\mathcal{W})$  depends on the current input  $\pmb{x}$  and current weight  $\mathcal{W}$ .

In addition to ReLU, other activation functions also satisfies this condition, including linear, LeakyReLU and monomial activations. For example, for power activation  $\psi(x) = x^p$  where  $p > 1$ , we have:

$$
G _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}; \mathcal {W}) = W _ {l} \operatorname {d i a g} ^ {p - 1} \left(\tilde {\boldsymbol {f}} _ {l - 1}\right), \quad Q _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}; \mathcal {W}) \equiv p I _ {n _ {l} \times n _ {l}} \tag {18}
$$

Remark. Note that the reversibility is not the same as invertible. Specifically, reversibility only requires the transfer function of a backpropagation gradient is a transpose of the forward function.

Lemma 1 (Recursive Gradient Update (Extension to Lemma 1 in (Tian, 2020)). Let forward and backward transition matrix  $V_{L}^{\mathrm{f}}(\boldsymbol{x}) = V_{L}^{\mathrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{x}) = I_{n_{L} \times n_{L}}$ , and define recursively:

$$
V _ {l - 1} ^ {\mathrm {f}} (\boldsymbol {x}) := V _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {f}} (\boldsymbol {x}) G _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}) \in \mathbb {R} ^ {n _ {l} \times n _ {l - 1}} \tag {19}
$$

$$
V _ {l - 1} ^ {\mathrm {b}} (\boldsymbol {x}) := V _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {b}} (\boldsymbol {x}) Q _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}) G _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}) \in \mathbb {R} ^ {n _ {l} \times n _ {l - 1}} \tag {20}
$$

If the network is reversible (Def. 2), then minimizing the  $\ell_2$  objective:

$$
r \left(\mathcal {W} _ {1}\right) := \frac {1}{2} \left\| \boldsymbol {f} _ {L} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}; \mathcal {W} _ {1}\right) - \boldsymbol {f} _ {L} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {2}; \mathcal {W} _ {2}\right) \right\| _ {2} ^ {2} \tag {21}
$$

with respect to weight matrix  $W_{l}$  at layer  $l$  yields the following gradient at layer  $l$ :

$$
\tilde {\boldsymbol {g}} _ {l} = V _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {b T}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}; \mathcal {W} _ {1}\right) \left[ V _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {f}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}; \mathcal {W} _ {1}\right) \tilde {\boldsymbol {f}} _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}; \mathcal {W} _ {1}\right) - V _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {f}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {2}; \mathcal {W} _ {2}\right) \tilde {\boldsymbol {f}} _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {2}; \mathcal {W} _ {2}\right) \right] \tag {22}
$$

Proof. We prove by induction. Note that our definition of  $W_{l}$  is the transpose of  $W_{l}$  defined in (Tian, 2020). Also our  $g_{l}(\pmb{x})$  is the gradient before nonlinearity, while (Tian, 2020) uses the same symbol for the gradient after nonlinearity.

For notation brievity, we let  $\tilde{\pmb{f}}_l(\pmb{x}_1) \coloneqq \tilde{\pmb{f}}_l(\pmb{x}_1; \mathcal{W}_l)$  and  $G_l(\pmb{x}_1) \coloneqq G_l(\pmb{x}_1; \mathcal{W}_l)$ . Similar for  $\pmb{x}_2$  and  $W_2$ .

When  $l = L$ , by the property of  $\ell_2$ -loss and the fact that  $\pmb{f}_L = \tilde{\pmb{f}}_L$  (no nonlinearity in the top-most layer), we know that  $\pmb{g}_L = \tilde{\pmb{f}}_L(\pmb{x}_1; \mathcal{W}_1) - \tilde{\pmb{f}}_L(\pmb{x}_2; \mathcal{W}_2)$ , by setting  $V_L^{\mathrm{f}}(\pmb{x}_1) = V_L^{\mathrm{f}}(\pmb{x}_2) = V_L^{\mathrm{b}}(\pmb{x}_1) = V_L^{\mathrm{b}}(\pmb{x}_2) = I$ , the condition holds. Now suppose for layer  $l$ , we have:

$$
\tilde {\boldsymbol {g}} _ {l} = V _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {b T}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) \left[ V _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {f}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) \tilde {\boldsymbol {f}} _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) - V _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {f}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {2}\right) \tilde {\boldsymbol {f}} _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {2}\right) \right] \tag {23}
$$

Then:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \tilde {\boldsymbol {g}} _ {l - 1} = G _ {l} ^ {\mathsf {T}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) Q _ {l} ^ {\mathsf {T}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) \tilde {\boldsymbol {g}} _ {l} (24) \\ = \underbrace {G _ {l} ^ {\mathsf {T}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) Q _ {l} ^ {\mathsf {T}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) V _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {b T}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right)} _ {V _ {l - 1} ^ {\mathrm {b T}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right)} \cdot \left[ V _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {f}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) \tilde {\boldsymbol {f}} _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) - V _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {f}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {2}\right) \tilde {\boldsymbol {f}} _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {2}\right) \right] (25) \\ = V _ {l - 1} ^ {\mathrm {b T}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) \left[ \underbrace {V _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {f}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) G _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right)} _ {V _ {l - 1} ^ {\mathrm {f}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right)} \tilde {\boldsymbol {f}} _ {l - 1} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) - \underbrace {V _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {f}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {2}\right) G _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {2}\right)} _ {V _ {l - 1} ^ {\mathrm {f}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {2}\right)} \tilde {\boldsymbol {f}} _ {l - 1} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {2}\right) \right] (26) \\ = V _ {l - 1} ^ {\mathrm {b T}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) \left[ V _ {l - 1} ^ {\mathrm {f}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) \tilde {\boldsymbol {f}} _ {l - 1} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) - V _ {l - 1} ^ {\mathrm {f}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {2}\right) \tilde {\boldsymbol {f}} _ {l - 1} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {2}\right) \right] (27) \\ \end{array}
$$

Remark on Deep ReLU networks. Note that for multi-layered ReLU network,  $G_{l}(\pmb{x}) = D_{l}(\pmb{x})W_{l}$ ,  $Q_{l}(\pmb{x}) \equiv I$  for each ReLU+Linear layer. Therefore, for each layer  $l$ , we have  $V_{l}^{\mathrm{f}}(\pmb{x}) = V_{l}^{\mathrm{b}}(\pmb{x})$  and we can just use  $V_{l}(\pmb{x}) \coloneqq V_{l}^{\mathrm{f}}(\pmb{x}) = V_{l}^{\mathrm{b}}(\pmb{x})$  to represent both. If we set  $x_{1} = x_{2} = x$ ,  $\mathcal{W}_{1} = \mathcal{W}$ ,  $\mathcal{W}_{2} = \mathcal{W}^{*}$  (teacher weights), then we go back to the original Lemma 1 in (Tian, 2020).

Remark on  $\ell_2$ -normalization in the topmost layer. For  $\ell_2$ -normalized objective function on DeepReLU networks:

$$
r _ {\mathrm {n}} \left(\mathcal {W} _ {1}\right) := \frac {1}{2} \left\| \frac {\boldsymbol {f} _ {L} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1} ; \mathcal {W} _ {1}\right)}{\left\| \boldsymbol {f} _ {L} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1} ; \mathcal {W} _ {1}\right) \right\| _ {2}} - \frac {\boldsymbol {f} _ {L} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {2} ; \mathcal {W} _ {2}\right)}{\left\| \boldsymbol {f} _ {L} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {2} ; \mathcal {W} _ {2}\right) \right\| _ {2}} \right\| _ {2} ^ {2}, \tag {28}
$$

it is equivalent to add a top-most  $\ell_2$ -normalization layer  $f_{L + 1} := \frac{f_L}{\|\pmb{f}_L\|_2}$ , we have  $G_{L + 1} := \frac{1}{\|\pmb{f}_L\|_2} I_{n_L\times n_L}$  and due to the following identity (here  $\hat{\pmb{y}} := \pmb {y} / \| \pmb {y}\| _2$ ):

$$
\frac {\partial \hat {\boldsymbol {y}}}{\partial \boldsymbol {y}} = \frac {1}{\| \boldsymbol {y} \| _ {2}} (I - \hat {\boldsymbol {y}} \hat {\boldsymbol {y}} ^ {\intercal}) \tag {29}
$$

Therefore we have  $\partial \pmb{f}_{L + 1} / \partial \pmb{f}_L = (I_{n_L\times n_L} - \pmb{f}_{L + 1}\pmb{f}_{L + 1}^{\intercal})G_{L + 1}$  and we could set  $Q_{L + 1}\coloneqq I - \pmb{f}_{L + 1}\pmb{f}_{L + 1}^{\intercal}$ , which is a projection matrix (and is also a positive semi-definite matrix). Let  $\ell_2$ -normalized transition matrix  $V_{l}^{\mathrm{n}}(\boldsymbol {x})\coloneqq \frac{1}{\|\pmb{f}_{L}(\boldsymbol{x})\|_{2}} V_{l}(\boldsymbol {x})$ . Applying Lemma 1 and we have for  $1\leq l\leq L$

$$
\tilde {\boldsymbol {g}} _ {l} = V _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {n T}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}; \mathcal {W} _ {1}\right) Q _ {L + 1} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}; \mathcal {W} _ {1}\right) \left[ V _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {n}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}; \mathcal {W} _ {1}\right) \tilde {\boldsymbol {f}} _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}; \mathcal {W} _ {1}\right) - V _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {n}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {2}; \mathcal {W} _ {2}\right) \tilde {\boldsymbol {f}} _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {2}; \mathcal {W} _ {2}\right) \right] \tag {30}
$$

Remark on ResNet. Note that the same structure holds for blocks of ResNet with ReLU activation.

# A.2. Theorem 1

Now we prove Theorem 1. Note that for deep ReLU networks,  $Q_{l}$  is a simple identity matrix and thus:

$$
J _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}) := \frac {\partial \boldsymbol {f} _ {L}}{\partial \tilde {\boldsymbol {f}} _ {l}} = V _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}) := V _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {f}} (\boldsymbol {x}) = V _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {b}} (\boldsymbol {x}) \tag {31}
$$

Theorem 1 (Squared  $\ell_2$  Gradient for dual deep reversible networks). The gradient  $g_{W_l}$  of the squared loss  $r$  with respect to  $W_{l}\in \mathbb{R}^{n_{l}\times n_{l - 1}}$  for a single input pair  $\{\pmb {x}_1,\pmb {x}_2\}$  is:

$$
g _ {W _ {l}} = \operatorname {v e c} \left(\partial r / \partial W _ {1, l}\right) = K _ {1, l} \left[ K _ {1, l} ^ {\top} \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {1, l}\right) - K _ {2, l} ^ {\top} \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {2, l}\right) \right]. \tag {32}
$$

Here  $K_{l}(\pmb {x};\mathcal{W}):= \pmb{f}_{l - 1}(\pmb {x};\mathcal{W})\otimes J_{l}^{\top}(\pmb {x};\mathcal{W}),K_{1,l}:= K_{l}(\pmb{x}_{1};\mathcal{W}_{1})$  and  $K_{2,l}\coloneqq K_l(\pmb {x}_2;\mathcal{W}_2)$

Proof. We consider a more general case where the two towers have different parameters, namely  $\mathcal{W}_1$  and  $\mathcal{W}_2$ . Applying Lemma 1 for the branch with input  $x_1$  at the linear layer  $l$ , and using Eqn. 31 we have:

$$
\tilde {\boldsymbol {g}} _ {1, l} = J _ {1, l} ^ {\mathsf {T}} \left[ J _ {1, l} W _ {1, l} \boldsymbol {f} _ {1, l - 1} - J _ {2, l} W _ {2, l} \boldsymbol {f} _ {2, l - 1} \right] \tag {33}
$$

where  $\pmb{f}_{1,l-1} := \pmb{f}_{l-1}(\pmb{x}_1; \mathcal{W}_1)$  is the activation of layer  $l-1$  just below the linear layer at tower 1 (similar for other symbols), and  $\tilde{\pmb{g}}_{1,l}$  is the back-propagated gradient after the nonlinearity.

In this case, the gradient (and the weight update, according to gradient descent) of the weight  $W_{l}$  between layer  $l$  and layer  $l - 1$  is:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {\partial r}{\partial W _ {1 , l}} = \tilde {\boldsymbol {g}} _ {1, l} \boldsymbol {f} _ {1, l - 1} ^ {\intercal} (34) \\ = J _ {1, l} ^ {\mathsf {T}} J _ {1, l} W _ {1, l} \boldsymbol {f} _ {1, l - 1} \boldsymbol {f} _ {1, l - 1} ^ {\mathsf {T}} - J _ {1, l} ^ {\mathsf {T}} J _ {2, l} W _ {2, l} \boldsymbol {f} _ {2, l - 1} \boldsymbol {f} _ {1, l - 1} ^ {\mathsf {T}} (35) \\ \end{array}
$$

Using  $\operatorname{vec}(AXB) = (B^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes A)\operatorname{vec}(X)$  (where  $\otimes$  is the Kronecker product), we have:

$$
\operatorname {v e c} \left(\frac {\partial r}{\partial W _ {1 , l}}\right) = \left(\boldsymbol {f} _ {1, l - 1} \boldsymbol {f} _ {1, l - 1} ^ {\intercal} \otimes J _ {1, l} ^ {\intercal} J _ {1, l}\right) \operatorname {v e c} (W _ {1, l}) - \left(\boldsymbol {f} _ {1, l - 1} \boldsymbol {f} _ {2, l - 1} ^ {\intercal} \otimes J _ {1, l} ^ {\intercal} J _ {2, l}\right) \operatorname {v e c} (W _ {2, l}) \tag {36}
$$

Let

$$
K _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}; W) := \boldsymbol {f} _ {l - 1} (\boldsymbol {x}; \mathcal {W}) \otimes J _ {l} ^ {\intercal} (\boldsymbol {x}; \mathcal {W}) \in \mathbb {R} ^ {n _ {l} n _ {l - 1} \times n _ {L}} \tag {37}
$$

Note that  $K_{l}(\pmb{x};\mathcal{W})$  is a function of the current weight  $W$ , which includes weights at all layers. By the mixed-product property of Kronecker product  $(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D) = AC\otimes BD$ , we have:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \operatorname {v e c} \left(\frac {\partial r}{\partial W _ {1 , l}}\right) = K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) ^ {\intercal} \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {1, l}\right) - K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {2}\right) ^ {\intercal} \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {2, l}\right) (38) \\ = K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) \left[ K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) ^ {\intercal} \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {1, l}\right) - K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {2}\right) ^ {\intercal} \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {2, l}\right) \right] (39) \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $K_{l}(\pmb{x}_{1}) = K_{l}(\pmb{x}_{1};\mathcal{W}_{1})$  and  $K_{l}(\pmb{x}_{2}) = K_{l}(\pmb{x}_{2};\mathcal{W}_{2})$ .

In SimCLR case, we have  $\mathcal{W}_1 = \mathcal{W}_2 = \mathcal{W}$  so

$$
\operatorname {v e c} \left(\frac {\partial r}{\partial W _ {l}}\right) = K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) \left[ K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) - K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {2}\right) \right] ^ {\intercal} \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {l}\right) \tag {40}
$$

Remark for  $\ell_2$  normalization. Note that in the presence of  $\ell_2$  normalization (Eqn. 28), with similar derivation, we get:

$$
\operatorname {v e c} \left(\frac {\partial r _ {\mathrm {n}}}{\partial W _ {l}}\right) = K _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {n}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) P _ {\boldsymbol {f} _ {L} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right)} ^ {\perp} \left[ K _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {n}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) - K _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {n}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {2}\right) \right] ^ {\intercal} \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {l}\right) \tag {41}
$$

where  $K_{l}^{\mathrm{n}}(\pmb{x}) \coloneqq K_{l}(\pmb{x}) / \| \pmb{f}_{L}(\pmb{x})\|_{2}$  and  $P_{\pmb{f}_{L}(\pmb{x}_{1})}^{\perp}$  is a projection matrix that project the gradient to the orthogonal complementary subspace of  $\pmb{f}_{L}(\pmb{x}_{1})$ :

$$
P _ {\boldsymbol {v}} ^ {\perp} := I _ {n _ {L} \times n _ {L}} - \frac {\boldsymbol {v}}{\| \boldsymbol {v} \| _ {2}} \frac {\boldsymbol {v} ^ {\intercal}}{\| \boldsymbol {v} \| _ {2}} \tag {42}
$$

# B. Analysis of SimCLR using Teacher-Student Setting (Section 4)

# B.1. Theorem 2

Theorem 2 (Common Property of Contrastive Losses). For loss functions  $L \in \{L_{\mathrm{simp}}, L_{\mathrm{tri}}^{\tau}, L_{\mathrm{nce}}^{\tau}\}$ , we have  $\frac{\partial L}{\partial r_+} > 0$ ,  $\frac{\partial L}{\partial r_{k-}} < 0$  for  $1 \leq k \leq H$  and  $\frac{\partial L}{\partial r_+} + \sum_{k=1}^{H} \frac{\partial L}{\partial r_{k-}} = 0$ .

Proof. For  $L_{\mathrm{simp}}$  and  $L_{\mathrm{tri}}$  the derivation is obvious. For  $L_{\mathrm{nce}}$ , we have:

$$
\frac {\partial L}{\partial r _ {+}} = \frac {1}{\tau} \left(1 - \frac {e ^ {- r _ {+} / \tau}}{e ^ {- r _ {+} / \tau} + \sum_ {k ^ {\prime} = 1} ^ {H} e ^ {- r _ {k ^ {\prime} -} / \tau}}\right) > 0 \tag {43}
$$

$$
\frac {\partial L}{\partial r _ {k -}} = - \frac {1}{\tau} \left(\frac {e ^ {- r _ {k -} / \tau}}{e ^ {- r _ {+} / \tau} + \sum_ {k ^ {\prime} = 1} ^ {H} e ^ {- r _ {k ^ {\prime} -} / \tau}}\right) <   0, \quad k = 1, \dots , H \tag {44}
$$

and obviously we have:

$$
\frac {\partial L}{\partial r _ {+}} + \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {H} \frac {\partial L}{\partial r _ {k -}} = 0 \tag {45}
$$

# B.2. The Covariance Operator under different loss functions

Lemma 2. For a loss function  $L$  that satisfies Theorem 2, with a batch of size one with samples  $\mathcal{X} := \{\pmb{x}_1, \pmb{x}_+,\pmb{x}_{1-},\pmb{x}_{2-},\dots,\pmb{x}_{H-}\}$ , where  $\pmb{x}_1,\pmb{x}_+ \sim p_{\mathrm{aug}}(\cdot|\pmb{x})$  are augmentation from the same sample  $\pmb{x}$ , and  $\pmb{x}_{k-} \sim p_{\mathrm{aug}}(\cdot|\pmb{x}_k')$  are augmentations from independent samples  $\pmb{x}_k' \sim p(\cdot)$ . We have:

$$
\operatorname {v e c} \left(g _ {W _ {l}}\right) = K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {H} \left[ \frac {\partial L}{\partial r _ {k -}} \Bigg | _ {\mathcal {X}} \cdot \left(K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {+}\right) - K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {k -}\right)\right) ^ {\intercal} \right] \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {l}\right) \tag {46}
$$

Proof. First we have:

$$
\operatorname {v e c} \left(g _ {W _ {l}}\right) = \frac {\partial L}{\partial W _ {l}} = \frac {\partial L}{\partial r _ {+}} \frac {\partial r _ {+}}{\partial W _ {l}} + \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {H} \frac {\partial L}{\partial r _ {k -}} \frac {\partial r _ {k -}}{\partial W _ {l}} \tag {47}
$$

Then we compute each terms. Using Theorem 1, we know that:

$$
\frac {\partial r _ {+}}{\partial W _ {l}} = K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) \left(K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) - K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {+}\right)\right) ^ {\intercal} \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {l}\right) \tag {48}
$$

$$
\frac {\partial r _ {k -}}{\partial W _ {l}} = K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) \left(K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) - K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {k -}\right)\right) ^ {\intercal} \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {l}\right), \quad k = 1, \dots , n \tag {49}
$$

Since Eqn. 45 holds,  $K_{l}(\pmb{x}_{1})K_{l}^{\mathsf{T}}(\pmb{x}_{1})$  will be cancelled out and we have:

$$
\operatorname {v e c} \left(g _ {W _ {l}}\right) = K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {H} \left[ \frac {\partial L}{\partial r _ {k -}} \left(K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {+}\right) - K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {k -}\right)\right) ^ {\intercal} \right] \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {l}\right) \tag {50}
$$

Remark. In  $\ell_2$ -normalized loss function, similarly we have:

$$
\operatorname {v e c} \left(g _ {W _ {l}}\right) = K _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {n}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) P _ {\boldsymbol {f} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right)} ^ {\perp} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {H} \left[ \frac {\partial L}{\partial r _ {k -}} \left(K _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {n}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {+}\right) - K _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {n}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {k -}\right)\right) ^ {\intercal} \right] \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {l}\right) \tag {51}
$$

# B.3. Theorem 3

Theorem 3 (Covariance Operator for  $L_{\mathrm{simp}}$ ). With large batch limit,  $W_{l}$ 's update under  $L_{\mathrm{simp}}$  is  $W_{l}(t + 1) = W_{l}(t) + \alpha \Delta W_{l}(t)$  ( $\alpha$  is the learning rate), where

$$
\operatorname {v e c} \left(\Delta W _ {l} (t)\right) = \mathrm {O P} _ {l} ^ {\text {s i m p}} (\mathcal {W}) \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {l} (t)\right). \tag {52}
$$

Here  $\mathrm{OP}_l^{\mathrm{simp}}(\mathcal{W})\coloneqq \mathbb{V}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim p(\cdot)}[\bar{K}_l(\boldsymbol {x};\mathcal{W})]\in \mathbb{R}^{n_l n_{l - 1}\times n_l n_{l - 1}}$  is the covariance operator for  $L_{\mathrm{simp}}$ ,  $\bar{K}_l(\pmb {x};\mathcal{W})\coloneqq \mathbb{E}_{\pmb{x}'\sim p_{\mathrm{aug}}(\cdot |\pmb {x})}[K_l(\pmb{x}';\mathcal{W})]$  is the expected connection under the augmentation distribution, conditioned on datapoint  $\pmb{x}$ .

Proof. For  $L_{\mathrm{simp}} \coloneqq r_+ - r_-$ , we have  $H = 1$  and  $\frac{\partial L}{\partial r_-} \equiv -1$ . Therefore using Lemma 2, we have:

$$
\operatorname {v e c} \left(g _ {W _ {l}}\right) = - K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) \left[ K _ {l} ^ {\intercal} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {+}\right) - K _ {l} ^ {\intercal} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {-}\right) \right] \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {l}\right) \tag {53}
$$

Taking large batch limits, we know that  $\mathbb{E}\left[K_l(\pmb{x}_1)K_l^\top(\pmb{x}_+)\right] = \mathbb{E}_{\pmb{x}}\left[\bar{K}_l(\pmb{x})\bar{K}_l^\top(\pmb{x})\right]$  since  $\pmb{x}_1, \pmb{x}_+ \sim p_{\mathrm{aug}}(\cdot|\pmb{x})$  are all augmented data points from a common sample  $\pmb{x}$ . On the other hand,  $\mathbb{E}\left[K_l(\pmb{x}_1)K_l^\top(\pmb{x}_{k-})\right] = \mathbb{E}_{\pmb{x}}\left[\bar{K}_l(\pmb{x})\right]\mathbb{E}_{\pmb{x}}\left[\bar{K}_l^\top(\pmb{x})\right]$  since  $\pmb{x}_1 \sim p_{\mathrm{aug}}(\cdot|\pmb{x})$  and  $\pmb{x}_{k-} \sim p_{\mathrm{aug}}(\cdot|\pmb{x}_k')$  are generated from independent samples  $\pmb{x}$  and  $\pmb{x}_k'$  and independent data augmentation. Therefore,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \operatorname {v e c} \left(g _ {W _ {l}}\right) = - \left\{\mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {x}} \left[ \bar {K} _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}) \bar {K} _ {l} ^ {\top} (\boldsymbol {x}) \right] - \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {x}} \left[ \bar {K} _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}) \right] \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {x}} \left[ \bar {K} _ {l} ^ {\top} (\boldsymbol {x}) \right] \right\} \operatorname {v e c} (W _ {l}) (54) \\ = - \mathbb {V} _ {\boldsymbol {x}} \left[ \bar {K} _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}) \right] \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {l}\right) (55) \\ \end{array}
$$

The conclusion follows since gradient descent is used and  $\Delta W_{l} = -g_{W_{l}}$ .

Remark. In  $\ell_2$ -normalized loss function, similarly we have:

$$
\operatorname {v e c} \left(g _ {W _ {l}}\right) = - \operatorname {C o v} _ {\boldsymbol {x}} \left[ \bar {K _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {n}} (\boldsymbol {x}) P _ {\boldsymbol {f} (\boldsymbol {x})} ^ {\perp}}, \bar {K _ {l} ^ {\mathrm {n}}} (\boldsymbol {x}) \right] \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {l}\right) \tag {56}
$$

Note that it is no longer symmetric. We leave detailed discussion to the future work.

# B.4. Theorem 4

Theorem 4 (Covariance Operator for  $L_{\mathrm{tri}}^{\tau}$  and  $L_{\mathrm{nce}}^{\tau}$  ( $H = 1$ , single negative pair)). Let  $r \coloneqq \frac{1}{2} \| \pmb{f}_L(\pmb{x}) - \pmb{f}_L(\pmb{x}')\|_2^2$ . The covariance operator  $\mathrm{OP}_l(\mathcal{W})$  has the following form:

$$
\mathrm {O P} _ {l} (\mathcal {W}) = \frac {1}{2} \mathbb {V} _ {\boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime} \sim p (\cdot)} ^ {\xi} \left[ \bar {K} _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}) - \bar {K} _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right) \right] + \theta \tag {57}
$$

where the pairwise weight  $\xi (r)$  takes the following form for different loss functions:

$$
\xi (r) = \left\{ \begin{array}{c c} 1 & L = L _ {\text {s i m p}} \\ \frac {e ^ {- (r - r _ {0}) / \tau}}{1 + e ^ {- (r - r _ {0}) / \tau}} & L = L _ {\text {t r i}} ^ {\tau} \\ \frac {1}{\tau} \frac {e ^ {- r / \tau}}{1 + e ^ {- r / \tau}} & L = L _ {\text {n c e}} ^ {\tau} \end{array} \right. \tag {58}
$$

and  $\theta := \mathcal{O}(\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}'}\left[\sqrt{r(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}')}\sigma_{\mathrm{aug}}(\boldsymbol{x})\right] + \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left[\sigma_{\mathrm{aug}}^2 (\boldsymbol {x})\right])$  is the residue term.  $\sigma_{\mathrm{aug}}^2 (\pmb {x})\coloneqq \mathrm{tr}\mathbb{V}_{\pmb{x}''\sim p_{\mathrm{aug}}(\cdot |\pmb {x})}[\pmb {f}_L(\pmb{x}'')]$  and tr is the trace of a matrix. For  $L_{\mathrm{simp}},\theta \equiv 0$

Proof. When  $\partial L / \partial r_{k - }$  is no longer constant, we consider its expansion with respect to un-augmented data point  $\mathcal{X}_0 = \{\pmb {x},\pmb{x}_1^{\prime},\dots ,\pmb{x}_k^{\prime}\}$ . Here  $\mathcal{X} = \{\pmb {x}_1,\pmb {x}_+,\pmb {x}_{1 - },\dots ,\pmb{x}_{H - }\}$  is one data sample that includes both positive and negative pairs. Note that  $\pmb{x}_1,\pmb{x}_+\sim p_{\mathrm{aug}}(\cdot |\pmb {x})$  and  $\pmb{x}_{k - }\sim p_{\mathrm{aug}}(\cdot |\pmb{x}_k^{\prime})$  for  $1\leq k\leq H$ .

$$
\left. \frac {\partial L}{\partial r _ {k -}} \right| _ {\mathcal {X}} = \left. \frac {\partial L}{\partial r _ {k -}} \right| _ {\mathcal {X} _ {0}} + \epsilon \tag {59}
$$

where  $\epsilon$  is a bounded quantity for  $L_{\mathrm{tri}}(|\epsilon |\leq 1)$  and  $L_{\mathrm{nce}}(|\epsilon |\leq 2 / \tau)$ .

We consider  $H = 1$  where there is only a single negative pair (and  $r_{-}$ ). In this case  $\mathcal{X}_0 = \{\pmb{x},\pmb{x}'\}$ . Let

$$
r := \frac {1}{2} \| \boldsymbol {f} _ {L} (\boldsymbol {x}) - \boldsymbol {f} _ {L} \left(\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \tag {60}
$$

and

$$
\xi \left(\boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right) := - \frac {\partial L}{\partial r _ {-}} \mid_ {\mathcal {X} _ {0}} \tag {61}
$$

Note that for  $L_{\mathrm{tri}}$ , it is not differentiable, so we could use its soft version:  $L_{\mathrm{tri}}^{\tau}(r_{+}, r_{-}) = \tau \log (1 + e^{(r_{+} - r_{-} + r_{0}) / \tau})$ . It is easy to see that  $\lim_{\tau \to 0} L_{\mathrm{tri}}^{\tau}(r_{+}, r_{-}) \to \max (r_{+} - r_{-} + r_{0}, 0)$ .

For the two losses:

For  $L_{\mathrm{tri}}^{\tau}$  we have

$$
\xi \left(\boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right) = \xi (r) = \frac {e ^ {- r / \tau}}{e ^ {- r _ {0} / \tau} + e ^ {- r / \tau}}. \tag {62}
$$

- For  $L_{\mathrm{nce}}^{\tau}$ , we have

$$
\xi \left(\boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right) = \xi (r) = \frac {1}{\tau} \frac {e ^ {- r / \tau}}{1 + e ^ {- r / \tau}}. \tag {63}
$$

Note that for  $L_{\mathrm{tri}}^{\tau}$  we have  $\lim_{\tau \to 0} \xi(r) = \mathbb{I}(r \leq r_0)$ . For  $L_{\mathrm{nce}}$ , since it is differentiable, by Taylor expansion we have  $\epsilon = O(\|x_1 - x\|_2, \|x_+ - x\|_2, \|x_- - x'\|_2)$ , which will be used later.

The constant term  $\xi$  with respect to data augmentation. In the following, we first consider the term  $\xi$ , which only depends on un-augmented data points  $\mathcal{X}_0$ . From Lemma 2, we now have a term in the gradient:

$$
\boldsymbol {g} _ {l} (\mathcal {X}) := - K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) \left[ K _ {l} ^ {\intercal} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {+}\right) - K _ {l} ^ {\intercal} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {-}\right) \right] \xi \left(\boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right) \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {l}\right) \tag {64}
$$

Under the large batch limit, taking expectation with respect to data augmentation  $p_{\mathrm{aug}}$  and notice that all augmentations are done independently, given un-augmented data  $\pmb{x}$  and  $\pmb{x}'$ , we have:

$$
\boldsymbol {g} _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right) := \mathbb {E} _ {p _ {\text {a u g}}} \left[ \boldsymbol {g} _ {l} (\mathcal {X}) \right] = - \bar {K} _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}) \left[ \bar {K} _ {l} ^ {\top} (\boldsymbol {x}) - \bar {K} _ {l} ^ {\top} \left(\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right) \right] \xi \left(\boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right) \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {l}\right) \tag {65}
$$

Symmetrically, if we swap  $\pmb{x}$  and  $x'$  since both are sampled from the same distribution  $p(\cdot)$ , we have:

$$
\boldsymbol {g} _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}, \boldsymbol {x}\right) = - \bar {K} _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right) \left[ \bar {K} _ {l} ^ {\intercal} \left(\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right) - \bar {K} _ {l} ^ {\intercal} (\boldsymbol {x}) \right] \xi \left(\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}, \boldsymbol {x}\right) \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {l}\right) \tag {66}
$$

since  $\xi (\pmb {x}^{\prime},\pmb {x})$  only depends on the squared  $\ell_2$  distance  $r$  (Eqn. 62 and Eqn. 63), we have  $\xi (\pmb {x}^{\prime},\pmb {x}) = \xi (\pmb {x},\pmb{x}^{\prime}) = \xi (r)$  and thus:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {g} _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}) + \boldsymbol {g} _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}, \boldsymbol {x}) = - \left[ \bar {K} _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}) \bar {K} _ {l} ^ {\intercal} (\boldsymbol {x}) - \bar {K} _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}) \bar {K} _ {l} ^ {\intercal} (\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}) + \bar {K} _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}) \bar {K} _ {l} ^ {\intercal} (\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}) - \bar {K} _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}) \bar {K} _ {l} ^ {\intercal} (\boldsymbol {x}) \right] \xi (r) \operatorname {v e c} (W _ {l}) \\ = - \xi (r) \left(\bar {K} _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}) - \bar {K} _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right)\right) \left(\bar {K} _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}) - \bar {K} _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right)\right) ^ {\intercal} \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {l}\right) \tag {67} \\ \end{array}
$$

Therefore, we have:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime} \sim p} \left[ \boldsymbol {g} _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right) \right] = - \frac {1}{2} \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime} \sim p} \left[ \xi (r) \left(\bar {K} _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}) - \bar {K} _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right)\right) \left(\bar {K} _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}) - \bar {K} _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right)\right) ^ {\intercal} \right] \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {l}\right) (68) \\ = - \frac {1}{2} \mathbb {V} _ {\boldsymbol {x}, \boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime} \sim p} ^ {\xi} \left[ \bar {K} _ {l} (\boldsymbol {x}) - \bar {K} _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right) \right] \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {l}\right) (69) \\ \end{array}
$$

Bound the error. For  $L_{\mathrm{nce}}$ , let

$$
F := - \frac {\partial L}{\partial r _ {-}} = \frac {1}{\tau} \left(\frac {e ^ {- r _ {-} / \tau}}{e ^ {- r _ {+} / \tau} + e ^ {- r _ {-} / \tau}}\right) > 0 \tag {70}
$$

then it is clear that  $0 < F < 1 / \tau$ . We can compute its partial derivatives:

$$
\frac {\partial F}{\partial r _ {+}} = - F (1 / \tau - F), \quad \frac {\partial F}{\partial r _ {-}} = F (1 / \tau - F) \tag {71}
$$

Note that  $F$  is a differentiable function with respect to  $r_+$  and  $r_-$ . We do a Taylor expansion of  $F$  on the two variables  $(r_+, r_-)$ , using intermediate value theorem, we have:

for derivatives evaluated at some point  $\{\tilde{r}_{+},\tilde{r}_{-}\}$  at the line connecting  $(\pmb {x},\pmb {x},\pmb{x}^{\prime})$  and  $(\pmb {x}_1,\pmb {x}_+,\pmb {x}_-$ $r_+^0$  and  $r_{-}^{0}$  are squared  $\ell^2$  distances evaluated at  $(\pmb {x},\pmb {x},\pmb{x}^{\prime})$  , therefore,  $r_+^0\equiv 0$  and  $r_{-}^{0} = \frac{1}{2}\| \pmb {f}(\pmb {x}) - \pmb {f}(\pmb{x}^{\prime})\|_{2}^{2}$  (note that here we just use  $\pmb {f}\coloneqq \pmb{f}_{L}$  for brevity).

\begin{array}{l} \mathbb {E} _ {p _ {\mathrm {a u g}}} \left[ \left| \frac {\partial F}{\partial r _ {+}} \right| _ {\{\tilde {r} _ {+}, \tilde {r} _ {-} \}} (r _ {+} - r _ {+} ^ {0}) \right] \leq \frac {1}{\tau^ {2}} \cdot \frac {1}{2} \int \| \boldsymbol {f} (\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}) - \boldsymbol {f} (\boldsymbol {x} _ {+}) \| _ {2} ^ {2} p _ {\mathrm {a u g}} (\boldsymbol {x} _ {1} | \boldsymbol {x}) p _ {\mathrm {a u g}} (\boldsymbol {x} _ {+} | \boldsymbol {x}) \mathrm {d} \boldsymbol {x} _ {1} \mathrm {d} \boldsymbol {x} _ {+} \\ = \frac {1}{\tau^ {2}} \left\{\mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime} \sim p _ {\text {a u g}} (\cdot | \boldsymbol {x})} \left[ \| \boldsymbol {f} \left(\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right) \| ^ {2} \right] - \| \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime} \sim p _ {\text {a u g}} (\cdot | \boldsymbol {x})} \left[ \boldsymbol {f} \left(\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right) \right] \| ^ {2} \right\} (73) \\ = \frac {1}{\tau^ {2}} \operatorname {t r} \mathbb {V} _ {\text {a u g}} [ \boldsymbol {f} | \boldsymbol {x} ] (74) \\ \end{array}

$$

we have (here  $\pmb{a} \coloneqq \pmb{f}(\pmb{x})$ ,  $\pmb{b} \coloneqq \pmb{f}(\pmb{x}')$ ,  $\Delta \pmb{a} \coloneqq \pmb{f}(\pmb{x}_1) - \pmb{f}(\pmb{x})$  and  $\Delta \pmb{b} \coloneqq \pmb{f}(\pmb{x}_-) - \pmb{f}(\pmb{x}')$ ):

$$
\begin{array}{l} r _ {-} - r _ {-} ^ {0} = \frac {1}{2} \left[ \| \boldsymbol {f} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) - \boldsymbol {f} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {-}\right) \| _ {2} ^ {2} - \| \boldsymbol {f} (\boldsymbol {x}) - \boldsymbol {f} \left(\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right) \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right] (76) \\ = \left[ \boldsymbol {f} (\boldsymbol {x}) - \boldsymbol {f} \left(\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right) \right] ^ {\top} \left[ \left(\boldsymbol {f} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) - \boldsymbol {f} (\boldsymbol {x})\right) - \left(\boldsymbol {f} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {-}\right) - \boldsymbol {f} \left(\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime}\right)\right) \right] \\ + \frac {1}{2} \| (\boldsymbol {f} (\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}) - \boldsymbol {f} (\boldsymbol {x})) - (\boldsymbol {f} (\boldsymbol {x} _ {-}) - \boldsymbol {f} (\boldsymbol {x} ^ {\prime})) \| ^ {2} (77) \\ \end{array}

Using  $\frac{1}{2}\| \pmb {a} - \pmb {b}\| _2^2\leq \| \pmb {a}\| _2^2 +\| \pmb {b}\| _2^2$  and Eqn. 94, we have the following (Here  $c_{0}(\pmb {x})\coloneqq \| \pmb {f}(\pmb {x}) - \mathbb{E}_{\pmb{x}^{\prime}\sim p_{\mathrm{aug}}(\cdot |\pmb {x})}\left[\pmb {f}(\pmb{x}^{\prime})\right]\|$

Remarks for  $H > 1$ . Note that for  $H > 1$ ,  $L_{\mathrm{nce}}$  has multiple negative pairs and  $\partial L / \partial r_{k - } = e^{-r_{k - } / \tau} / Z(\mathcal{X})$  where  $Z(\mathcal{X})\coloneqq e^{-r_+ / \tau} + \sum_{k = 1}^{H}e^{-r_{k - } / \tau}$ . While the nominator  $e^{-r_{k - } / \tau}$  still only depends on the distance between  $\pmb{x}_1$  and  $\pmb{x}_{k - }$  (which is good), the normalization constant  $Z(\mathcal{X})$  depends on  $H + 1$  distance pairs simultaneously. This leads to

$$
\xi_ {k} = \left. \frac {\partial L}{\partial r _ {k -}} \right| _ {\mathcal {X} _ {0}} = \frac {e ^ {- \| \boldsymbol {x} - \boldsymbol {x} _ {k} ^ {\prime} \| _ {2} ^ {2} / \tau}}{1 + \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {H} e ^ {- \| \boldsymbol {x} - \boldsymbol {x} _ {k} ^ {\prime} \| _ {2} ^ {2} / \tau}} \tag {80}
$$

which causes issues with the symmetry trick (Eqn. 67), because the denominator involves many negative pairs at the same time.

However, if we think given one pair of distinct data point  $(\pmb{x},\pmb{x}^{\prime})$ , the normalized constant  $Z$  averaged over data augmentation is approximately constant due to homogeneity of the dataset and data augmentation, then Eqn. 67 can still be applied and similar conclusion follows.

Proof. Note that the second part  $\mathbb{V}_{\boldsymbol{x} \sim p(\cdot)}\left[\bar{K}_l(\boldsymbol{x})\right]$  (which corresponds to  $r_+ - r_-)$  has already proven in Theorem 3 of the main paper. By gradient descent, we have:

and by Lemma 2:

\frac {\partial r _ {+}}{\partial W _ {l}} = K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) \left(K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {1}\right) - K _ {l} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {+}\right)\right) ^ {\intercal} \operatorname {v e c} \left(W _ {l}\right) \tag {85}

Definition 3 (Transition-Regularized HLTM (abbr. TR-HLTM)). For  $\mu \in \mathcal{Z}_l$  and  $\nu \in \mathcal{Z}_{l - 1}$ , the transitional probability matrix  $P_{\mu \nu} \coloneqq [\mathbb{P}(z_\nu | z_\mu)]$  has decomposition  $P_{\mu \nu} = \frac{1}{m_\nu} \mathbf{1}_\mu \mathbf{1}_\nu^\intercal + C_{\mu \nu}$  where  $C_{\mu \nu} \mathbf{1}_\nu = \mathbf{0}_\mu$  and  $\mathbf{1}_\mu^\intercal C_{\mu \nu} = \mathbf{0}_\nu$ .

Note that  $C_{\mu \nu} \mathbf{1} = \mathbf{0}$  is obvious due to the property of conditional probability. The real condition is  $\mathbf{1}_{\mu}^{\top} C_{\mu \nu} = \mathbf{0}_{\nu}$ . If  $m_{\mu} = m_{\nu}$ , then  $P_{\mu \nu}$  is a square matrix and Def. 3 is equivalent to  $P_{\mu \nu}$  is double-stochastic. The Def. 3 makes computation of  $P_{\mu \nu}$  easy for any  $z_{\mu}$  and  $z_{\nu}$ .

As we will see in the remark of Lemma 6, symmetric binary HLTM (SB-HLTM) naturally satisfies Def. 3 and is a TR-HLTM. Conversely, TR-HLTM allows categorical latent variables and is a super set of SB-HLTM. See Fig. 9.

Lemma 6 (Property of TR-HLTM). For TR-HLTM(Def. 3), for  $\mu \in \mathcal{Z}_l$ ,  $\nu \in \mathcal{Z}_{1 - 1}$  and  $\alpha \in \mathcal{Z}_{l - 2}$ , we have:

$$
P _ {\mu \alpha} = P _ {\mu \nu} P _ {\nu \alpha} = \frac {1}{m _ {\alpha}} \mathbf {1} _ {\mu} \mathbf {1} _ {\alpha} ^ {\intercal} + C _ {\mu \nu} C _ {\nu \alpha} \tag {98}
$$

In general, for any  $\mu \in \mathcal{N}_{l_1}$  and  $\alpha \in \mathcal{N}_{l_2}$  with  $l_{1} > l_{2}$ , we have:

$$
P _ {\mu \alpha} = \frac {1}{m _ {\alpha}} \mathbf {1} _ {\mu} \mathbf {1} _ {\alpha} ^ {\intercal} + \prod_ {\mu , \dots , \xi , \zeta , \dots , \alpha} C _ {\xi \zeta} \tag {99}
$$

Proof. By Def. 3, we have

$$
\begin{array}{l} P _ {\mu \alpha} = P _ {\mu \nu} P _ {\nu \alpha} (100) \\ = \left(\frac {1}{m _ {\nu}} \mathbf {1} _ {\mu} \mathbf {1} _ {\nu} ^ {\intercal} + C _ {\mu \nu}\right) \left(\frac {1}{m _ {\alpha}} \mathbf {1} _ {\nu} \mathbf {1} _ {\alpha} ^ {\intercal} + C _ {\nu \alpha}\right) (101) \\ \end{array}
$$

since  $\mathbf{1}_{\nu}^{\intercal}\mathbf{1}_{\nu} = m_{\nu}$ ,  $C_{\mu \nu}\mathbf{1}_{\nu} = \mathbf{0}_{\nu}$  and  $\mathbf{1}_{\nu}^{\intercal}C_{\nu \alpha} = \mathbf{0}_{\alpha}$ , the conclusion follows.

Lemma 7. TR-HLTM with all latent variables being binary (or binary TR-HLTM) is equivalent to SB-HLTM.

Proof. SB-HLTM is TR-HLTM. For symmetric binary HLTM (SB-HLTM) defined in Sec. 6.1, we could define  $C_{\mu \nu}$  as the following (here  $q := [-1,1]^{\top}$ ):

$$
C _ {\mu \nu} = C _ {\mu \nu} \left(\rho_ {\mu \nu}\right) = \frac {1}{2} \left[ \begin{array}{c c} \rho_ {\mu \nu} & - \rho_ {\mu \nu} \\ - \rho_ {\mu \nu} & \rho_ {\mu \nu} \end{array} \right] = \frac {1}{2} \rho_ {\mu \nu} \boldsymbol {q} \boldsymbol {q} ^ {\intercal} \tag {102}
$$

And it is easy to verify this satisfies the definition of TR-HLTM (Def. 3).

Binary TR-HLTM is SB-HLTM. To see this, note that  $\mathbf{1}_2^{\mathsf{T}}C_{\mu \nu} = \mathbf{0}_2$  and  $C_{\mu \nu}\mathbf{1}_2 = \mathbf{0}_2$  provides 4 linear constraints (1 redundant), leaving 1 free parameter, which is the polarity  $\rho_{\mu \nu}$ . It is easy to verify that  $\rho_{\mu \nu}\in [-1,1]$  in order to keep  $0\leq P_{\mu \nu}\leq 1$  a probabilistic measure. Moreover, since  $\pmb {q}^{\mathrm{T}}\pmb {q} = 2$ , the parameterization is close under multiplication:

$$
C \left(\rho_ {\mu \nu}\right) C \left(\rho_ {\nu \alpha}\right) = \frac {1}{4} \boldsymbol {q} \boldsymbol {q} ^ {\intercal} \boldsymbol {q} \boldsymbol {q} ^ {\intercal} \rho_ {\mu \nu} \rho_ {\nu \alpha} = \frac {1}{2} \boldsymbol {q} \boldsymbol {q} ^ {\intercal} \rho_ {\mu \nu} \rho_ {\nu \alpha} = C \left(\rho_ {\mu \nu} \rho_ {\nu \alpha}\right) \tag {103}
$$

s _ {k} := \frac {1}{2} \left(v _ {k} ^ {+} - v _ {k} ^ {-}\right) = \frac {1}{2} \left(\mathbb {E} _ {z} [ f _ {k} | z _ {\nu} = 1 ] - \mathbb {E} _ {z} [ f _ {k} | z _ {\nu} = 0 ]\right) \tag {108}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol {s} _ {\mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} := \left[ s _ {k} \right] _ {k \in \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} \in \mathbb {R} ^ {\left| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} \right|} \tag {109}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol {a} _ {\mu} := \left[ \rho_ {\mu \nu (k)} s _ {k} \right] _ {k \in \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} \tag {110}
$$

Lemma 8 (Lower-bound of tail distribution of 2D Gaussian). For standardized Gaussian variable  $y_{+}$  and  $y_{-}$  with (here  $\gamma \geq 0$ ):

$$
\boldsymbol {y} := \left[ \begin{array}{l} y _ {+} \\ y _ {-} \end{array} \right] \sim \mathcal {N} \left(\boldsymbol {0}, \Sigma_ {\boldsymbol {y}}\right), \quad \text {w h e r e} \Sigma_ {\boldsymbol {y}} := \left[ \begin{array}{c c} 1 & - \gamma \\ - \gamma & 1 \end{array} \right] \tag {111}
$$

Then we have lower-bound for the probability:

$$
\mathbb {P} \left(\boldsymbol {y} \geq \boldsymbol {y} _ {0} := \left[ \begin{array}{c} \sqrt {c} \\ 0 \end{array} \right]\right) \geq \frac {1}{2 \pi} \exp \left(- \frac {c}{2}\right) (1 - \gamma^ {2}) ^ {1 / 2} \hat {R} (c, \gamma) \tag {112}
$$

where  $\hat{R} (c,\gamma)$  is defined in Eqn. 121.

Proof. First we compute the precision matrix  $M_{\pmb{y}} \coloneqq \Sigma_{\pmb{y}}^{-1}$ :

$$
M _ {\boldsymbol {y}} = \frac {1}{1 - \gamma^ {2}} \left[ \begin{array}{l l} 1 & \gamma \\ \gamma & 1 \end{array} \right] \tag {113}
$$

![](images/1a8e598638cede482d5fe896f774ed5e438d0860c148244df07cca07f66ed29c.jpg)  
Figure 10. Joint distribution of  $\mathbf{y} = [y_{+}, y_{-}]^{\mathrm{T}}$  and the region we want to lower-bound.

and we can apply the lower-bound of the Mills ratio (The equation below Eqn. 5 in (Steck, 1979)) for standardized 2D Gaussian distribution (note that “ $\rho$ ” in (Steck, 1979) is  $-\gamma$  here):

$$
\begin{array}{l} R \left(\boldsymbol {y} _ {0}, M _ {\boldsymbol {y}}\right) := 2 \pi \left| \Sigma_ {\boldsymbol {y}} \right| ^ {1 / 2} \mathbb {P} (\boldsymbol {y} \geq \boldsymbol {y} _ {0}) \exp \left(\frac {\boldsymbol {y} _ {0} ^ {\mathrm {T}} M _ {\boldsymbol {y}} \boldsymbol {y} _ {0}}{2}\right) (114) \\ \geq \left(1 - \gamma^ {2}\right) \max  \left[ r \left(\kappa_ {1}\right) r \left(\kappa_ {2} + \gamma e \left(\kappa_ {1}\right)\right), r \left(\kappa_ {2}\right) r \left(\kappa_ {1} + \gamma e \left(\kappa_ {2}\right)\right) \right] (115) \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $r(y) > 0$  is the 1D Mills ratio and other terms defined as follows:

$$
r (y) := \int_ {0} ^ {+ \infty} e ^ {- y t - \frac {t ^ {2}}{2}} \mathrm {d} t \tag {116}
$$

$$
e (y) := \frac {1}{r (y)} - y \tag {117}
$$

$$
\kappa_ {1} := (1 - \gamma^ {2}) ^ {- 1 / 2} \left(\boldsymbol {y} _ {0} [ 1 ] + \gamma \boldsymbol {y} _ {0} [ 2 ]\right) = (1 - \gamma^ {2}) ^ {- 1 / 2} \sqrt {c} \tag {118}
$$

$$
\kappa_ {2} := (1 - \gamma^ {2}) ^ {- 1 / 2} \left(\boldsymbol {y} _ {0} [ 2 ] + \gamma \boldsymbol {y} _ {0} [ 1 ]\right) = (1 - \gamma^ {2}) ^ {- 1 / 2} \gamma \sqrt {c} = \gamma \kappa_ {1} \tag {119}
$$

Note that for  $r(y)$ , we have:

$$
r (y) = \int_ {0} ^ {+ \infty} e ^ {- y t - \frac {t ^ {2}}{2}} \mathrm {d} t = e ^ {\frac {y ^ {2}}{2}} \int_ {y} ^ {+ \infty} e ^ {- \frac {t ^ {2}}{2}} \mathrm {d} t = \sqrt {2 \pi} e ^ {\frac {y ^ {2}}{2}} (1 - r (y)) \tag {120}
$$

where  $r(y)$  is the Cumulative Probability Distribution (CDF) for standard Gaussian distribution.

For simplicity, we can define:

$$
\hat {R} (c, \gamma) := \max  \left[ r \left(\kappa_ {1}\right) r \left(\kappa_ {2} + \gamma e \left(\kappa_ {1}\right)\right), r \left(\kappa_ {2}\right) r \left(\kappa_ {1} + \gamma e \left(\kappa_ {2}\right)\right) \right] \tag {121}
$$

Plug them in, we get:

$$
\mathbb {P} \left(\boldsymbol {y} \geq \boldsymbol {y} _ {0} := \left[ \begin{array}{c} \sqrt {c} \\ 0 \end{array} \right]\right) \geq \frac {1}{2 \pi} \det ^ {1 / 2} \left(M _ {\boldsymbol {y}}\right) \exp \left(- \frac {1}{2} \boldsymbol {y} _ {0} ^ {\intercal} M _ {\boldsymbol {y}} \boldsymbol {y} _ {0}\right) (1 - \gamma^ {2}) \hat {R} (c, \gamma) \tag {122}
$$

Note that  $\pmb{y}_0^\top M_{\pmb{y}}\pmb{y}_0 = c$  and  $\operatorname*{det}^{1 / 2}(M_{\pmb{y}}) = (1 - \gamma^2)^{-1 / 2}$ . Therefore, we have:

$$
\mathbb {P} \left(\boldsymbol {y} \geq \boldsymbol {y} _ {0} := \left[ \begin{array}{c} \sqrt {c} \\ 0 \end{array} \right]\right) \geq \frac {1}{2 \pi} \exp \left(- \frac {c}{2}\right) (1 - \gamma^ {2}) ^ {1 / 2} \hat {R} (c, \gamma) \tag {123}
$$

and the conclusion follows.

Remarks. Obviously,  $r(y)$  is a decreasing function. Following Theorem 2 (Soranzo & Epure, 2014), we have for  $y > 0$ :

$$
\frac {2}{\sqrt {y ^ {2} + 4} + y} <   r (y) <   \frac {4}{\sqrt {y ^ {2} + 8} + 3 y} \tag {124}
$$

and we could estimate the bound of  $\hat{R}(c, \gamma)$  accordingly. Using Eqn. 120 to estimate the bound would be very difficult numerically since  $1 - r(y)$  becomes very close to zero once  $y$  becomes slightly large (e.g., when  $y \geq 10$ ).

Note that from Theorem 1 in (Soranzo & Epure, 2014), we have a coarser bound for  $r(y)$  and  $e(y)$  (the bound will blow-up when  $y \to 0$ ):

$$
\frac {y}{1 + y ^ {2}} <   r (y) <   \frac {1}{y}, \quad 0 <   e (y) <   \frac {1}{y} \tag {125}
$$

So when  $y$  is large, we have  $r(y) \sim \frac{1}{y}$ . Therefore, when  $\gamma$  is close to 1 and  $c$  is large (Fig. 11):

$$
\hat {R} (c, \gamma) \sim \kappa_ {1} ^ {- 1} \kappa_ {2} ^ {- 1} \sim \frac {1 - \gamma^ {2}}{\gamma c} \tag {126}
$$

Therefore,  $\hat{R} (c,\gamma)$  has the following properties:

$$
\lim  _ {\gamma \rightarrow 1} \hat {R} (c, \gamma) = 0, \quad \lim  _ {c \rightarrow + \infty} \hat {R} (c, \gamma) = 0 \tag {127}
$$

Intuitively, the first one is due to the fact that when  $\gamma = 1$ ,  $\tilde{v}_j^+$  and  $\tilde{v}_j^-$  become perfectly correlated (i.e.,  $y_+$  and  $y_-$  are perfectly negatively correlated) with each other. In that case,  $\mathbb{P}(\pmb{y} \geq \pmb{y}_0)$  becomes zero. The second one is due to the fact that  $\pmb{y}$  as a random variable cannot deviate too far away from its mean value.

Note that when  $\gamma \to 0$ , Eqn. 126 is not accurate and there is no singularity in  $\hat{R}(c, \gamma)$  near  $\gamma = 0$ , as shown in Fig. 11.

# C.4. Lucky node at initialization (Sec. 6.1.1)

Theorem 5 (Lucky nodes at initialization in deep ReLU networks regarding to SB-HLTM). Suppose each element of the weights  $W_{l}$  between layer  $l + 1$  and  $l$  are initialized with Uniform  $\left[-\sigma_w\sqrt{\frac{3}{|\mathcal{N}_\mu^{\mathrm{ch}}|}},\sigma_w\sqrt{\frac{3}{|\mathcal{N}_\mu^{\mathrm{ch}}|}}\right]$ . Then for any  $\mu \in \mathcal{Z}_{l + 1}$ , if

$$
\left| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} \right| \geq \frac {2 \pi e ^ {c / 2}}{\sqrt {1 - \gamma^ {2}} \hat {R} (c , \gamma)} \ln 1 / \eta \tag {128}
$$

where

$$
\gamma := \frac {\| \bar {\boldsymbol {v}} _ {\mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} \| _ {2} ^ {2} - \| \boldsymbol {a} _ {\mu} \| _ {2} ^ {2}}{\| \bar {\boldsymbol {v}} _ {\mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} - \boldsymbol {a} _ {\mu} \| _ {2} \| \bar {\boldsymbol {v}} _ {\mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} + \boldsymbol {a} _ {\mu} \| _ {2}} \tag {129}
$$

then with probability at least  $1 - \eta$ , there exists at least one node  $j \in \mathcal{N}_{\mu}$  so that their pre-activation gap  $\tilde{v}_j(1) - \tilde{v}_j(0) = 2\pmb{w}_j^\top \pmb{a}_{\mu} > 0$  and the activations satisfy (here  $\bar{v}_j \coloneqq \frac{1}{2}(v_j(0) + v_j(1))$ ):

$$
\left| s _ {j} \right| \geq \frac {3 \sigma_ {w} ^ {2}}{4 \bar {v} _ {j}} \left[ \frac {1}{\left| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} \right|} \sum_ {k \in \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} s _ {k} ^ {2} \left(\frac {c + 6}{6} \rho_ {\mu \nu} ^ {2} - 1\right) - \sigma_ {l} ^ {2} \right]. \tag {130}
$$

Here  $\sigma_l^2 \coloneqq \max_{k \in \mathcal{N}_\mu^{\mathrm{ch}}} \mathbb{V}[f_k | z_\nu]$  and  $\pmb{a}_{\mu} \coloneqq [\rho_{\mu \nu(k)} s_k]_{k \in \mathcal{N}_\mu^{\mathrm{ch}}}$  is a vector which contains selectivity of all child nodes of  $z_\mu$ , multiplied by the corresponding polarity  $\rho_{\mu \nu(k)}$  for each child  $k$  whose latent variable is  $\nu = \nu(k)$  (See Fig. 8).

Proof. According to our setting, for each node  $k \in \mathcal{N}_{\mu}$ , there exists a unique latent variable  $z_{\nu}$  with  $\nu = \nu(k)$  that corresponds to it. In the following we omit its dependency on  $k$  for brevity.

We first consider pre-activation  $\tilde{f}_j\coloneqq \sum_kw_{jk}f_k$  and its expectation with respect to latent variable  $z$ :

$$
\tilde {v} _ {j} ^ {+} := \mathbb {E} _ {z} \left[ \tilde {f} _ {j} \mid z _ {\mu} = 1 \right], \quad \tilde {v} _ {j} ^ {-} := \mathbb {E} _ {z} \left[ \tilde {f} _ {j} \mid z _ {\mu} = 0 \right] \tag {131}
$$

Note that for each node  $k \in \mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ch}}$  we compute the conditional expectation of the child node  $k$ , conditioned on the parent level latent variable  $z_{\mu}$ :

$$
\begin{array}{l} v _ {\mu k} ^ {+} := \mathbb {E} _ {z} [ f _ {k} | z _ {\mu} = 1 ] (132) \\ = \sum_ {z _ {\nu}} \mathbb {E} _ {z} \left[ f _ {k} \mid z _ {\nu}, z _ {\mu} = 1 \right] \mathbb {P} \left(z _ {\nu} \mid z _ {\mu} = 1\right) (133) \\ \stackrel {1} {=} \sum_ {z _ {\nu}} \mathbb {E} _ {z} \left[ f _ {k} \mid z _ {\nu} \right] \mathbb {P} \left(z _ {\nu} \mid z _ {\mu} = 1\right) (134) \\ = \frac {1}{2} \left(1 + \rho_ {\mu \nu}\right) v _ {k} (1) + \frac {1}{2} \left(1 - \rho_ {\mu \nu}\right) v _ {k} (0) (135) \\ = \bar {v} _ {k} + \rho_ {\mu \nu} s _ {k} (136) \\ \end{array}
$$

Note that 1 is due to the fact that the activation  $f_{k}$  only depends on  $z_{\nu}$  (and its descendants), and is independent of  $z_{\mu}$  as long as  $z_{\nu}$  is known. Similarly we have  $v_{\mu k}^{-} = \mathbb{E}_{z}[f_{k}|z_{\mu} = 0] = \bar{v}_{k} - \rho_{\mu \nu}s_{k}$ . For convenience, we vectorize it. Let  $\pmb{a}_{\mu} := [a_k]_{k\in \mathcal{N}_\mu^{\mathrm{ch}}} := [\rho_{\mu \nu}s_k]_{k\in \mathcal{N}_\mu^{\mathrm{ch}}}$  and

$$
\boldsymbol {u} _ {\mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} ^ {+} := V _ {\mu , \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} ^ {+} := \left[ \mathbb {E} \left[ f _ {k} \mid z _ {\mu} = 1 \right] \right] = \bar {\boldsymbol {v}} _ {\mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} + \boldsymbol {a} _ {\mu} \tag {137}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol {u} _ {\mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} ^ {-} := V _ {\mu , \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} ^ {-} := \left[ \mathbb {E} \left[ f _ {k} \mid z _ {\mu} = 0 \right] \right] = \bar {\boldsymbol {v}} _ {\mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} - \boldsymbol {a} _ {\mu} \tag {138}
$$

Then we have  $\tilde{v}_j^+ = \pmb {w}_j^\top \pmb{u}_{\mathcal{N}_\mu^{\mathrm{ch}}}^+$  and  $\tilde{v}_j^{-} = \pmb {w}_j^\top \pmb{u}_{\mathcal{N}_\mu^{\mathrm{ch}}}^{-}$  and the pre-activation gap  $\tilde{v}_j^+ -\tilde{v}_j^- = 2\pmb {w}_j^\top \pmb {a}_\mu$

Note that  $\mathbf{w}_j$  is a random variable with each entry  $w_{jk} \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\left[-\sigma_w\sqrt{\frac{3}{|\mathcal{N}_\mu^{\mathrm{ch}}|}}, \sigma_w\sqrt{\frac{3}{|\mathcal{N}_\mu^{\mathrm{ch}}|}}\right]$ . It is easy to verify that  $\mathbb{E}[w_{jk}] = 0$  and  $\mathbb{V}[w_{jk}] = \sigma_w^2 / |\mathcal{N}_\mu^{\mathrm{ch}}|$ . Therefore, for two-dimensional vector

$$
\tilde {\boldsymbol {v}} _ {j} = \left[ \begin{array}{l} \tilde {v} _ {j} ^ {+} \\ \tilde {v} _ {j} ^ {-} \end{array} \right] \tag {139}
$$

we can compute its first and second order moments:  $\mathbb{E}_w[\tilde{\pmb{v}}_j] = \mathbf{0}$  and its variance (here we set  $\pmb {h}:= \bar{\pmb{v}}_{\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ch}}}$  and  $\pmb {a}\coloneqq \pmb{a}_{\mu}$  for notation brevity, in this case we have  $\pmb{u}_{\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ch}}}^{+} = \pmb {h} + \pmb{a}$  and  $\pmb{u}_{\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ch}}}^{-} = \pmb {h} - \pmb {a})$

$$
\mathbb {V} _ {w} [ \tilde {\boldsymbol {v}} _ {j} ] = \frac {\sigma_ {w} ^ {2}}{| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} |} V _ {\mu , \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} V _ {\mu , \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} ^ {\intercal} = \frac {\sigma_ {w} ^ {2}}{| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} |} \left[ \begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {u} _ {\mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} ^ {+} \\ \boldsymbol {u} _ {\mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} ^ {-} \end{array} \right] \left[ \boldsymbol {u} _ {\mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} ^ {+}, \boldsymbol {u} _ {\mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} ^ {-} \right] = \frac {\sigma_ {w} ^ {2}}{| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} |} \left[ \begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {h} + \boldsymbol {a} \\ \boldsymbol {h} - \boldsymbol {a} \end{array} \right] [ \boldsymbol {h} + \boldsymbol {a}, \boldsymbol {h} - \boldsymbol {a} ] \tag {140}
$$

Define the positive and negative set (note that  $a_{k}\coloneqq \rho_{\mu \nu}s_{k}$ ):

$$
A _ {+} = \{k: a _ {k} \geq 0 \}, \quad A _ {-} = \{k: a _ {k} <   0 \} \tag {141}
$$

Without loss of generality, assume that  $\sum_{k\in A_+}a_k^2\geq \sum_{k\in A_-}a_k^2$ . In the following, we show there exists  $j$  with  $(v_j^+)^2 - (v_j^-)^2$  is greater than some positive threshold. Otherwise the proof is symmetric and we can show  $(v_j^+)^2 - (v_j^-)^2$  is lower than some negative threshold.

When  $|\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ch}}|$  is large, by Central Limit Theorem,  $\tilde{\pmb{v}}_j$  can be regarded as zero-mean 2D Gaussian distribution with the following covariance matrix:

$$
\tilde {\boldsymbol {v}} _ {j} \sim \mathcal {N} \left(\mathbf {0}, \frac {\sigma_ {w} ^ {2}}{| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} |} \left[ \begin{array}{l l} \| \boldsymbol {h} + \boldsymbol {a} \| _ {2} ^ {2} & \| \boldsymbol {h} \| _ {2} ^ {2} - \| \boldsymbol {a} \| _ {2} ^ {2} \\ \| \boldsymbol {h} \| _ {2} ^ {2} - \| \boldsymbol {a} \| _ {2} ^ {2} & \| \boldsymbol {h} - \boldsymbol {a} \| _ {2} ^ {2} \end{array} \right]\right) \tag {142}
$$

Now we want to lower-bound the following probability. For some  $c > 0$ :

$$
\mathbb {P} \left(\tilde {v} _ {j} ^ {+} \geq \frac {\sqrt {c} \sigma_ {w}}{\sqrt {| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} |}} \| \boldsymbol {u} _ {\mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} ^ {+} \| \quad \text {a n d} \quad \tilde {v} _ {j} ^ {-} <   0\right) \tag {143}
$$

For this, we consider the following transformation to standardize each component of  $\tilde{\pmb{v}}_j$ :

$$
y _ {+} = \tilde {v} _ {j} ^ {+} \frac {\sqrt {\left| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} \right|}}{\sqrt {c} \sigma_ {w}} \frac {1}{\left\| \boldsymbol {h} + \boldsymbol {a} \right\| _ {2}}, \quad y _ {-} = - \tilde {v} _ {j} ^ {-} \frac {\sqrt {\left| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} \right|}}{\sqrt {c} \sigma_ {w}} \frac {1}{\left\| \boldsymbol {h} - \boldsymbol {a} \right\| _ {2}} \tag {144}
$$

And the probability we want to lower-bound becomes:

$$
\mathbb {P} \left(\boldsymbol {y} \geq \boldsymbol {y} _ {0} := \left[ \begin{array}{c} \sqrt {c} \\ 0 \end{array} \right]\right) \tag {145}
$$

Then  $\pmb{y} = [y_{+}, y_{-}]^{\mathrm{T}}$  satisfies the condition of Lemma 8 with  $\gamma$ :

$$
\gamma := \frac {\| \boldsymbol {h} \| _ {2} ^ {2} - \| \boldsymbol {a} \| _ {2} ^ {2}}{\| \boldsymbol {h} + \boldsymbol {a} \| _ {2} \| \boldsymbol {h} - \boldsymbol {a} \| _ {2}} \tag {146}
$$

Note that  $\gamma \geq 0$ . To see this, we know  $\pmb{u}_{\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ch}}}^{+} = \pmb{h} + \pmb{a} \geq \pmb{0}$  and  $\pmb{u}_{\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ch}}}^{-} = \pmb{h} - \pmb{a} \geq \pmb{0}$  by the definition of  $\pmb{u}_{\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ch}}}^{+}$  and  $\pmb{u}_{\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ch}}}^{-}$  since they are both expectation of ReLU outputs (here  $\geq$  is in the sense of element-wise). Therefore, we have:

$$
\left\| \boldsymbol {h} \right\| _ {2} ^ {2} - \left\| \boldsymbol {a} \right\| _ {2} ^ {2} = (\boldsymbol {h} + \boldsymbol {a}) ^ {\intercal} (\boldsymbol {h} - \boldsymbol {a}) \geq 0 \tag {147}
$$

and thus  $\gamma \geq 0$

Therefore, with (note that the residue term  $\hat{R}(c,\gamma)$  is defined in Lemma 8):

$$
\left| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} \right| \geq \frac {2 \pi e ^ {c / 2}}{\sqrt {1 - \gamma^ {2}} \hat {R} (c , \gamma)} \ln 1 / \eta \tag {148}
$$

we have:

$$
\left| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} \right| \geq \frac {\ln 1 / \eta}{\mathbb {P} (\boldsymbol {y} \geq \boldsymbol {y} _ {0})} \geq \frac {\ln 1 / \eta}{- \ln [ 1 - \mathbb {P} (\boldsymbol {y} \geq \boldsymbol {y} _ {0}) ]} \tag {149}
$$

Or

$$
1 - \left(1 - \mathbb {P} \left(\boldsymbol {y} \geq \boldsymbol {y} _ {0}\right)\right) ^ {\left| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} \right|} \geq 1 - \eta \tag {150}
$$

which means that with at least  $1 - \eta$  probability, there exists at least one node  $j\in \mathcal{N}_{\mu}$  and  $\pmb {w}_j$  , so that Eqn. 143 holds.

In this case, we have:

$$
\tilde {v} _ {j} ^ {+} = \boldsymbol {w} _ {j} ^ {\intercal} \boldsymbol {u} _ {\mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} ^ {+} \geq \frac {\sqrt {c} \sigma_ {w}}{\sqrt {| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} |}} \| \boldsymbol {u} _ {\mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} ^ {+} \|, \quad \tilde {v} _ {j} ^ {-} = \boldsymbol {w} _ {j} ^ {\intercal} \boldsymbol {u} _ {\mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} ^ {-} <   0 \tag {151}
$$

Since  $\bar{v}_{\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ch}}}\geq 0$  (all  $f_{k}$  are after ReLU and non-negative), this leads to:

$$
\tilde {v} _ {j} ^ {+} \geq \frac {\sqrt {c} \sigma_ {w}}{\sqrt {\left| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} \right|}} \| \boldsymbol {u} _ {\mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} ^ {+} \| \geq \frac {\sqrt {c} \sigma_ {w}}{\sqrt {\left| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} \right|}} \sqrt {\sum_ {k \in A _ {+}} a _ {k} ^ {2}} \geq \sigma_ {w} \sqrt {\frac {c}{2 \left| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} \right|} \sum_ {k \in \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} \rho_ {\mu \nu} ^ {2} s _ {k} ^ {2}} \tag {152}
$$

By Jensen's inequality, we have (note that  $\psi(x) \coloneqq \max(x, 0)$  is the ReLU activation):

$$
v _ {j} ^ {+} = \mathbb {E} _ {z} [ f _ {j} | z _ {\mu} = 1 ] = \mathbb {E} _ {z} \left[ \psi (\tilde {f} _ {j}) | z _ {\mu} = 1 \right] \tag {153}
$$

$$
\geq \psi \left(\mathbb {E} _ {z} \left[ \tilde {f} _ {j} \mid z _ {\mu} = 1 \right]\right) = \psi \left(\tilde {v} _ {j} ^ {+}\right) \geq \sigma_ {w} \sqrt {\frac {c}{2 \left| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} \right|} \sum_ {k \in \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} \rho_ {\mu \nu} ^ {2} s _ {k} ^ {2}} \tag {154}
$$

On the other hand, we also want to compute  $v_{j}^{-} \coloneqq \mathbb{E}_{z}[f_{j}|z_{\mu} = 0]$  using sharpened Jensen's inequality (Lemma 5). For this we need to compute the conditional covariance  $\mathbb{V}_z[\tilde{f}_j|z_\mu ]$ :

$$
\mathbb {V} _ {z} \left[ \tilde {f} _ {j} \mid z _ {\mu} \right] \stackrel {(2)} {=} \sum_ {k} w _ {j k} ^ {2} \mathbb {V} _ {z} \left[ f _ {k} \mid z _ {\mu} \right] \stackrel {(3)} {\leq} \frac {3 \sigma_ {w} ^ {2}}{\left| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} \right|} \sum_ {k} \mathbb {V} _ {z} \left[ f _ {k} \mid z _ {\mu} \right] \tag {155}
$$

$$
= \frac {3 \sigma_ {w} ^ {2}}{\left| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} \right|} \sum_ {k} \left(\mathbb {E} _ {z _ {\nu} | z _ {\mu}} \left[ \mathbb {V} \left[ f _ {k} \mid z _ {\nu} \right] \right] + \mathbb {V} _ {z _ {\nu} | z _ {\mu}} \left[ \mathbb {E} _ {z} \left[ f _ {k} \mid z _ {\nu} \right] \right]\right) \tag {156}
$$

$$
\leq 3 \sigma_ {w} ^ {2} \left(\sigma_ {l} ^ {2} + \frac {1}{| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} |} \sum_ {k} \mathbb {V} _ {z _ {\nu} | z _ {\mu}} [ \mathbb {E} _ {z} [ f _ {k} | z _ {\nu} ] ]\right) \tag {157}
$$

Note that (2) is due to conditional independence:  $f_{k}$  as the computed activation, only depends on latent variable  $z_{\nu}$  and its descendants. Given  $z_{\mu}$ , all  $z_{\nu}$  and their respective descendants are independent of each other and so does  $f_{k}$ . (3) is due to the fact that each  $w_{jk}$  are sampled from uniform distribution and  $|w_{jk}| \leq \sigma_w \sqrt{\frac{3}{|\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ch}}|}}$ .

Here  $\mathbb{V}_{z_{\nu}|z_{\mu}}[\mathbb{E}_z[f_k|z_{\nu}]] = s_k^2 (1 - \rho_{\mu \nu}^2)$  can be computed analytically. It is the variance of a binomial distribution: with probability  $\frac{1}{2} (1 + \rho_{\mu \nu})$  we get  $v_{k}^{+}$  otherwise get  $v_{k}^{-}$ . Therefore, we finally have:

$$
\mathbb {V} _ {z} \left[ \tilde {f} _ {j} \mid z _ {\mu} \right] \leq 3 \sigma_ {w} ^ {2} \left(\sigma_ {l} ^ {2} + \frac {1}{\left| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} \right|} \sum_ {k} s _ {k} ^ {2} \left(1 - \rho_ {\mu \nu} ^ {2}\right)\right) \tag {158}
$$

As a side note, using Lemma 3, since ReLU function  $\psi$  has Lipschitz constant  $\leq 1$  (empirically it is smaller), we know that:

$$
\mathbb {V} _ {z} \left[ f _ {j} \mid z _ {\mu} \right] \leq 3 \sigma_ {w} ^ {2} \left(\sigma_ {l} ^ {2} + \frac {1}{\left| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} \right|} \sum_ {k} s _ {k} ^ {2} \left(1 - \rho_ {\mu \nu} ^ {2}\right)\right) \tag {159}
$$

Finally using Lemma 5 and  $\tilde{v}_j^{-} := \mathbb{E}_z\left[\tilde{f}_j|z_\mu = 0\right] < 0$ , we have:

$$
v _ {j} ^ {-} = \mathbb {E} _ {z} \left[ f _ {j} \mid z _ {\mu} = 0 \right] = \mathbb {E} _ {z} \left[ \psi (\tilde {f} _ {j}) \mid z _ {\mu} = 0 \right] \tag {160}
$$

$$
\leq \psi \left(\mathbb {E} _ {z} \left[ \tilde {f} _ {j} \mid z _ {\mu} = 0 \right]\right) + \sqrt {\mathbb {V} _ {z} [ \tilde {f} _ {j} \mid z _ {\mu} = 0 ]} \tag {161}
$$

$$
= \sqrt {\mathbb {V} _ {z} [ \tilde {f} _ {j} | z _ {\mu} = 0 ]} \tag {162}
$$

$$
\leq \sigma_ {w} \sqrt {3 \sigma_ {l} ^ {2} + \frac {3}{| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} |} \sum_ {k} s _ {k} ^ {2} \left(1 - \rho_ {\mu \nu} ^ {2}\right)} \tag {163}
$$

Combining Eqn. 154 and Eqn. 163, we have a bound for  $\lambda_{j}$ :

$$
(v _ {j} ^ {+}) ^ {2} - (v _ {j} ^ {-}) ^ {2} \geq 3 \sigma_ {w} ^ {2} \left[ \frac {1}{| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} |} \sum_ {k} s _ {k} ^ {2} \left(\frac {c + 6}{6} \rho_ {\mu \nu} ^ {2} - 1\right) - \sigma_ {l} ^ {2} \right] \tag {164}
$$

Note that  $|(v_j^+)^2 - (v_j^-)^2| = |(v_j^+ + v_j^-)(v_j^+ - v_j^-)| = 4|\bar{v}_j s_j|$  and the conclusion follows.

Remark. Intuitively, this means that with large polarity  $\rho_{\mu \nu}$  (strong top-down signals), randomly initialized overparameterized ReLU networks yield selective neurons, if the lower layer also contains selective ones. For example, when  $\sigma_l = 0$ ,  $c = 9$ , if  $\rho_{\mu \nu} \geq 63.3\%$  then there is a gap between expected activations  $v_j(1)$  and  $v_j(0)$ , and the gap is larger when the selectivity in the lower layer  $l$  is higher. With the same setting, if  $\gamma = 0.2$ , we could actually compute the range of  $|\mathcal{N}_{\mu}|$  specified by Theorem 5, using Eqn. 124:

$$
1 0 9 9 \leq | \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} | \leq 1 4 6 7 \tag {165}
$$

while in practice, we don't need such a strong over-parameterization.

For  $l = 0$  (the lowest layer),  $\{v_k\}$  are themselves observable leaf latent variables and are selective by definition ( $|s_k| \equiv 1$ ) and the conditional variance  $\sigma_l^2 \equiv 0$ . In the higher level,  $|s_j|$  often goes down, since only a few child nodes will be selective (with large  $s_k^2$ ), and their contribution are divided by  $|\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ch}}|$ . These insights are confirmed in Tbl. C.5 and more experiments in Sec. D.1.

$$

For a categorical distribution

$$

Note that each column of  $V_{0,\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ch}}}$  is  $\pmb{v}_{0k}$ . Setting  $\pmb{u} = \pmb{v}_{0k}$  and we have:

Proof. We simply apply Lemma 9 to SB-HLTM. Note here we define  $\rho_0 \coloneqq \mathbb{P}(z_0 = 1) - \mathbb{P}(z_0 = 0)$ , and  $\pmb{q} \coloneqq [-1,1]^{\mathrm{T}}$  so we have:

P _ {0} - P _ {0} \mathbf {1 1} ^ {\intercal} P _ {0} = \frac {1}{4} \left(1 - \rho_ {0} ^ {2}\right) \boldsymbol {q} \boldsymbol {q} ^ {\intercal} \tag {172}
$$

Therefore, we have:

$$
\mathbb {V} _ {z _ {0}} \left[ \mathbb {E} _ {z} \left[ \boldsymbol {f} _ {\mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} | z _ {0} \right] \right] = \frac {1}{4} \left(1 - \rho_ {0} ^ {2}\right) V _ {0, \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} ^ {\intercal} \boldsymbol {q} \boldsymbol {q} ^ {\intercal} V _ {0, \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} \quad \in \mathbb {R} ^ {\left| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} \right| \times \left| \mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}} \right|} \tag {173}
$$

This is a square matrix of size  $|\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ch}}|-$  by-  $|\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ch}}|$ . Now let's check each entry. For entry  $(k,k^{\prime})$  (where  $k,k^{\prime}\in \mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ch}}$ ), it is

$$
\frac {1}{4} \left(1 - \rho_ {0} ^ {2}\right) \left(\boldsymbol {v} _ {0 k} ^ {\intercal} \boldsymbol {q}\right) \left(\boldsymbol {v} _ {0 k ^ {\prime}} ^ {\intercal} \boldsymbol {q}\right) ^ {\intercal} \tag {174}
$$

So it reduces to computing  $\pmb{v}_{0k}^{\mathsf{T}}\pmb{q}$ . Note that following Eqn. 169:

$$
\boldsymbol {q} ^ {\intercal} \boldsymbol {v} _ {0 k} = \frac {1}{m _ {\nu}} \boldsymbol {q} ^ {\intercal} \boldsymbol {1} _ {0} \boldsymbol {1} _ {\nu} ^ {\intercal} \boldsymbol {v} _ {k} + \boldsymbol {q} ^ {\intercal} C _ {0 \nu} \boldsymbol {v} _ {k} = \boldsymbol {q} ^ {\intercal} C _ {0 \nu} \boldsymbol {v} _ {k} \tag {175}
$$

since  $\pmb{q}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{1}_0 = 0$ . In SB-HLTM, according to Lemma 7, all  $C_{\mu \nu} = \frac{1}{2}\rho_{\mu \nu}\pmb{q}\pmb{q}^{\mathrm{T}}$  and we could compute  $C_{0\nu}\pmb{v}_k$ :

$$
C _ {0 \nu} \boldsymbol {v} _ {k} = \frac {1}{2} \rho_ {0 \nu} \boldsymbol {q} \boldsymbol {q} ^ {\intercal} \boldsymbol {v} _ {k} = \rho_ {0 \nu} \frac {1}{2} (v _ {k} (1) - v _ {k} (0)) \boldsymbol {q} = \rho_ {0 \nu} s _ {k} \boldsymbol {q} \tag {176}
$$

Therefore, we have  $\pmb{q}^{\intercal}\pmb{v}_{0k} = \pmb{q}^{\intercal}C_{0\nu}\pmb{v}_{k} = 2\rho_{0\nu}s_{k}$  since  $\pmb{q}^{\intercal}\pmb{q} = 2$

On the other hand, according to Eqn. 103, we have:

$$
\rho_ {0 \nu} := \prod_ {0, \dots , \alpha , \beta , \dots , \nu} \rho_ {\alpha \beta} = \rho_ {0 \mu} \rho_ {\mu \nu} \tag {177}
$$

This is due to the fact that due to tree structure, the path from  $z_0$  to all child nodes in  $\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ch}}$  must pass  $z_{\mu}$ . Therefore, we have  $\pmb{q}^{\top}\pmb{v}_{0k} = 2\rho_{0\mu}\rho_{\mu \nu}s_k = 2\rho_{0\mu}\pmb{a}_{\mu}[k]$ , which gives for entry  $(k,k')$ :

$$
\frac {1}{4} \left(1 - \rho_ {0} ^ {2}\right) \left(\boldsymbol {v} _ {0 k} ^ {\intercal} \boldsymbol {q}\right) \left(\boldsymbol {v} _ {0 k ^ {\prime}} ^ {\intercal} \boldsymbol {q}\right) ^ {\intercal} = \rho_ {0 \mu} ^ {2} \left(1 - \rho_ {0} ^ {2}\right) \boldsymbol {a} _ {\mu} [ k ] \boldsymbol {a} _ {\mu} [ k ^ {\prime} ] \tag {178}
$$

$$
\mathbb {V} _ {z _ {0}} \left[ \mathbb {E} _ {z} \left[ \boldsymbol {f} _ {\mathcal {N} _ {\mu} ^ {\mathrm {c h}}} \mid z _ {0} \right] \right] = \rho_ {0 \mu} ^ {2} \left(1 - \rho_ {0} ^ {2}\right) \boldsymbol {a} _ {\mu} \boldsymbol {a} _ {\mu} ^ {\intercal} \tag {179}
$$

When  $L\to +\infty$  , from Lemma 6 and its remark, we have:

$$
\rho_ {0 \mu} := \prod_ {0, \dots , \alpha , \beta , \dots , \mu} \rho_ {\alpha \beta} \rightarrow 0 \tag {180}
$$

and thus the covariance becomes zero as well.

# D.1. HLTM

Experiment Setup. The construction of our Symmetric Binary Hierarchical Latent Tree Models (SB-HLTM) is shown in Fig. 12(a). We construct a complete binary tree of depth  $L$ . The class/sample-specific latent  $z_0$  is at the root, while other nuisance latent variables are labeled with a binary encoding (e.g.,  $\mu =$  "s010" for a  $z_{\mu}$  that is the left-right-left child of the root  $z_0$ ). The polarity  $\rho_{\mu \nu}$  is random sampled from a uniform distribution:  $\rho_{\mu \nu} \sim \text{Uniform[delta_lower, 1]}$  at each layer, where delta_lower is a hyperparameter.

On the neural network side, we use deep ReLU networks. We train with NCE loss  $(L_{\mathrm{nce}}^{\tau})$  with  $\tau = 0.1$  and  $H = 1$ .  $\ell_2$  normalization is used in the output layer. Simple SGD is used for the training with learning rate 0.1. We generate a dataset using HLM with  $N = 64000$  samples. The training batchsize is 128. The model usually is well converged after 50 epochs.

In particular, we can clearly see the following several trends:

(1) Top-layer latent is less correlated with the top-layer nodes in deep networks. This shows that top-layer latents are harder to learn (and bottom-layer latents are easier to learn since they are closer to the corresponding network layers).  
(2) There is already a non-trivial amount of NC at initialization. Furthermore, NC is higher in the bottom-layer, since they are closer to the input. Nodes in the lowest layer (leaves) would have perfect NC (1.0), since they are identical to the leaves of the latent tree models.
