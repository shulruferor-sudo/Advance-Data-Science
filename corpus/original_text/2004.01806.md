# On the convergence of physics informed neural networks for linear second-order elliptic and parabolic type PDEs

Yeonjong Shin<sup>1</sup>, Jérôme Darbon<sup>1</sup>, and George Em Karniadakis<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup> Division of Applied Mathematics, Brown University, Providence, RI 02912, USA

Abstract. Physics informed neural networks (PINNs) are deep learning based techniques for solving partial differential equations (PDEs) encountered in computational science and engineering. Guided by data and physical laws, PINNs find a neural network that approximates the solution to a system of PDEs. Such a neural network is obtained by minimizing a loss function in which any prior knowledge of PDEs and data are encoded. Despite its remarkable empirical success in one, two or three dimensional problems, there is little theoretical justification for PINNs.

As the number of data grows, PINNs generate a sequence of minimizers which correspond to a sequence of neural networks. We want to answer the question: Does the sequence of minimizers converge to the solution to the PDE? We consider two classes of PDEs: linear second-order elliptic and parabolic. By adapting the Schauder approach and the maximum principle, we show that the sequence of minimizers strongly converges to the PDE solution in  $C^0$ . Furthermore, we show that if each minimizer satisfies the initial/boundary conditions, the convergence mode becomes  $H^1$ . Computational examples are provided to illustrate our theoretical findings. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first theoretical work that shows the consistency of PINNs.

Key words: Physics Informed Neural Networks, Convergence, Hölder Regularization, Elliptic and Parabolic PDEs, Schauder approach

# 1 Introduction

Machine learning techniques using deep neural networks have been successfully applied in various fields [24] such as computer vision and natural language processing. A notable advantage of using neural networks is its efficient implementation using a dedicated hardware (see [8, 22]). Such techniques have also been applied in solving partial differential equations (PDEs) [4, 10, 22, 23, 32, 35], and it has become a new sub-field under the name of Scientific Machine Learning (SciML) [2, 26]. The term Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) was introduced in [32] and it has become one of the most popular deep learning methods in SciML. PINNs employ a neural network as a solution surrogate and seek to find the best neural network guided by data and physical laws expressed as PDEs.

A series of works have shown the effectiveness of PINNs in one, two or three dimensional problems: fractional PDEs [30,36], stochastic differential equations [18,39], biomedical problems [33], and fluid mechanics [27]. Despite such remarkable success in these and related areas, PINNs lack theoretical justification. In this paper, we provide a mathematical justification of PINNs.

One of the main goals of PINNs is to approximate the solution to the PDE. For readers' convenience, we provide a concrete example to explain the PINNs method. Let us consider a 1D Poisson equation on  $U = (0,1)$  with the Dirichlet boundary conditions:

$$
\mathcal {L} [ u ] (x) = f (x), \quad \forall x \in U, \qquad \mathcal {B} [ u ] (x) = g (x), \quad \forall x \in \partial U,
$$

where the differential operator  $\mathcal{L}$  is the Laplace  $\Delta$  operator and the boundary operator  $\mathcal{B}$  is the identity operator. Suppose that the differential operator  $\mathcal{L}$  and the boundary operator  $\mathcal{B}$  are known to us, however, the PDE data (i.e.,  $f$  and  $g$ ) are only known at some sample points. In other words, although we do not know what  $f$  and  $g$  are exactly, we can access them through their pointwise evaluations. Here and throughout the paper, we assume the high regularity setting for PDEs where point-wise evaluations are defined. We refer to a set of available pointwise values of  $f$  and  $g$  as the training data set. The loss functionals are then designed to penalize functions that fail to satisfy both governing equations (PDEs) and boundary conditions on the training data. For example, if  $(j/5, f(j/5))$  for  $j = 1, \dots, 4$ , and  $(0, g(0)$  and  $(1, g(1))$  are given to us as the training data, a prototype PINN loss functional [32] is defined by

$$
\operatorname {L o s s} (u) = \frac {1}{4} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {4} (\Delta u (j / 4) - f (j / 4)) ^ {2} + \frac {1}{2} \left[ (u (0) - g (0)) ^ {2} + (u (1) - g (1)) ^ {2} \right].
$$

PINNs seek to find a neural network that minimizes the loss in a class of neural networks. A minimizer serves as an approximation to the solution to the PDE. Since neural networks are parameterized with finitely many variables, the loss functional restricted to it becomes a function of network parameters, which is called the loss function. With a slight abuse of terminology, we shall not distinguish the loss functional and the loss function and both will be simply called the loss function. In Figure 1, we provide a schematic of PINNs.

PINNs are different approaches to the traditional variational principle that minimizes an energy functional [1,6,11]. The most distinctive difference between them is that not all PDEs satisfy a variational principle, however, the formulation of PINNs does not require the considered PDE to have a variational principle and can be applied to broad classes of PDEs as shown in aforementioned works.

As explained, given a set of  $m$ -training data, PINNs require one to choose a class of networks  $\mathcal{H}_n$  and a loss function.  $n$  represents a complexity of the class, e.g., the number of parameters.  $\mathcal{H}_n$  may depend on the number of training data and also the data itself. The goal is then to find a minimizer  $h_m \in \mathcal{H}_n$  of the loss. The optimization process is referred to as training. However, even in an extreme case where  $\mathcal{H}_n$  contains the exact solution  $u^*$  to PDEs and a global minimizer is found, since there could be multiple (often infinitely many) global minimizers, there is no guarantee that a minimizer one found and the solution  $u^*$  coincide. By assuming an idealized setup of a global minimizer always being found, PINNs generate a sequence of neural networks with respect to the

The total errors of neural networks-based supervised learning can be decomposed into three components [5, 29]: (a) approximation error, (b) optimization errors, and (c) estimation error. We illustrate the decomposition of the total errors in Figure 2. The approximation error (a) is relatively well understood. [31] showed that a single layer neural network with a sufficiently large width can uniformly approximate a function and its partial derivative. It also has been shown that neural networks are capable of approximating the solutions for some classes of PDEs: Quasilinear parabolic PDEs [35], the Black-Scholes PDEs [17], and the Hamilton-Jacobi PDEs [8, 9]. The optimization error (b) is, however, poorly understood as the objective function is highly nonconvex. Optimization often involves many engineering tricks and tedious trial and error type fine-tuning of parameters. Gradient-based optimization methods are commonly used for the training. Numerous variants of the stochastic gradient descent method have been proposed [34]. In particular, Adam [21] and L-BFGS [25] are popularly employed in the context of PINNs [26]. Empirically, the solutions found by gradient-based optimization are shown to perform well in various challenging tasks. However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no guarantee that gradient-based optimization will find a global minimum for general machine learning problems including PINNs. This is one of widely open problems. The works of [20, 37] studied a couple of ways to improve the training of PINNs. The estimation error (c) comes from the use of finite data, and is one of two components (together with approximation error) that constitute generalization error [29]. In machine learning, generalization error refers to a measure of the accuracy of the prediction on unseen data [28]. In PDE problems, the generalization error is the distance between a global minimizer of the loss and the solution to the PDE, where the distance has to be defined appropriately to reflect its regularity. From this perspective, the present paper is concerned with the convergence of generalization error.

In addition, we show that if minimizers satisfy the initial/boundary conditions, the mode of convergence becomes  $H^1$ . # 2 Mathematical Setup and Preliminaries

Let  $U$  be a bounded domain (open and connected) in  $\mathbb{R}^d$ . We consider partial differential equations (PDEs) of the form

$$
\mathcal {L} [ u ] (\mathbf {x}) = f (\mathbf {x}) \quad \forall \mathbf {x} \in U, \quad \mathcal {B} [ u ] (\mathbf {x}) = g (\mathbf {x}) \quad \forall \mathbf {x} \in \Gamma \subseteq \partial U, \tag {2.1}
$$

where  $\mathcal{L}[\cdot ]$  is a differential operator and  $\mathcal{B}[\cdot ]$  could be Dirichlet, Neumann, Robin, or periodic boundary conditions. This paper considers PDEs that admit a unique classical solution  $u(\mathbf{x})$  with  $\mathbf{x} = (x_{1},\dots ,x_{d})$ . The classical solution should satisfy the governing equation everywhere on  $U$  and  $\Gamma$ . We remark that for time-dependent problems, we consider time  $t$  as a special component of  $\mathbf{x}$ , and  $U$  contains the temporal domain. The initial condition can be simply treated as a special type of Dirichlet boundary condition on the spatio-temporal domain.

The goal is to approximate the solution to the PDE (2.1) from a set of training data. The training data consist of two types of data sets: residual and initial/boundary data. A residual datum is a pair of input and output  $(\mathbf{x}_r, f(\mathbf{x}_r))$ , where  $\mathbf{x}_r \in U$  and an initial/boundary datum is a pair of input and output  $(\mathbf{x}_b, g(\mathbf{x}_b))$ , where  $\mathbf{x}_b \in \Gamma$ . The set of  $m_r$  residual input data points and the set of  $m_b$  initial/boundary input data points are denoted by  $\mathcal{T}_r^{m_r} = \{\mathbf{x}_r^i\}_{i=1}^{m_r}$  and  $\mathcal{T}_b^{m_b} = \{\mathbf{x}_b^i\}_{i=1}^{m_b}$ , respectively. Let us denote the vector of the number of training samples by  $\boldsymbol{m} = (m_r, m_b)$ . Note

that we slightly abuse notation as in [32]:  $\mathbf{x}_r$  refers to a point in  $U$ . Similarly,  $\mathbf{x}_b$  refers to a point in  $\Gamma$ .  $m_r$  and  $m_b$  represent the number of training data points in  $U$  and  $\Gamma$ , respectively.

Remark 2.1. The present paper only considers the high regularity setting for PDEs where pointwise evaluations are well-defined. For the sake of simplicity, we consider one boundary operator case. And  $u$  is a scalar real-valued function.

Given a class of neural networks  $\mathcal{H}_n$  that may depend on the number of training samples  $m$  (also may implicitly depend on the data sample itself), we seek to find a neural network  $h^*$  in  $\mathcal{H}_n$  that minimizes an objective (loss) function. Here  $n$  represents a complexity of the class, e.g., the number of parameters. To define an appropriate objective function, let us consider a loss integrand, which is the standard choice in physics informed neural networks (PINNs) [32]:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbf {L} \left(\mathbf {x} _ {r}, \mathbf {x} _ {b}; h, \lambda , \lambda^ {R}\right) = \left(\lambda_ {r} \| \mathcal {L} [ h ] \left(\mathbf {x} _ {r}\right) - f \left(\mathbf {x} _ {r}\right) \| ^ {2}\right) \mathbb {I} _ {U} \left(\mathbf {x} _ {r}\right) + \lambda_ {r} ^ {R} R _ {r} (h) \\ + \lambda_ {b} \| \mathcal {B} [ h ] (\mathbf {x} _ {b}) - g (\mathbf {x} _ {b}) \| ^ {2} \mathbb {I} _ {\Gamma} (\mathbf {x} _ {b}) + \lambda_ {b} ^ {R} R _ {b} (h), \tag {2.2} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\| \cdot \|$  is the Euclidean norm,  $\mathbb{I}_A(\mathbf{x})$  is the indicator function on the set  $A$ ,  $\lambda = (\lambda_r, \lambda_b)$ ,  $\lambda^R = (\lambda_r^R, \lambda_b^R)$ , and  $R_{r}(\cdot), R_{b}(\cdot)$  are regularization functionals. Here  $\lambda_r, \lambda_r^R, \lambda_b, \lambda_b^R \in \mathbb{R}_+ \cup \{0\}$ .

Suppose  $\mathcal{T}_r^{m_r}$  and  $\mathcal{T}_b^{m_b}$  are independently and identically distributed (iid) samples from probability distributions  $\mu_r$  and  $\mu_b$ , respectively. Let us define the empirical probability distribution on  $\mathcal{T}_r^{m_r}$  by  $\mu_r^{m_r} = \frac{1}{m_r}\sum_{i=1}^{m_r}\delta_{\mathbf{x}_r^i}$ . Similarly,  $\mu_b^{m_b}$  is defined. The empirical loss and the expected loss are obtained by taking the expectations on the loss integrand (2.2) with respect to  $\mu^m = \mu_r^{m_r} \times \mu_b^{m_b}$  and  $\mu = \mu_r \times \mu_b$ , respectively:

$$
\operatorname {L o s s} _ {m} (h; \lambda , \lambda^ {R}) = \mathbb {E} _ {\mu^ {m}} [ \mathbf {L} (\mathbf {x} _ {r}, \mathbf {x} _ {b}; h, \lambda , \lambda^ {R}) ], \quad \operatorname {L o s s} (h; \lambda , \lambda^ {R}) = \mathbb {E} _ {\mu} [ \mathbf {L} (\mathbf {x} _ {r}, \mathbf {x} _ {b}; h, \lambda , \lambda^ {R}) ]. \tag {2.3}
$$

In order for the expected loss to be well-defined, it is assumed that  $\mathcal{L}[h]$  and  $f$  are in  $L^2 (U;\mu_r)$ , and  $\mathcal{B}[h]$  and  $g$  are in  $L^2 (\Gamma ;\mu_b)$  for all  $h\in \mathcal{H}_n$ . If the expected loss function were available, its minimizer would be the solution to the PDE (2.1) or close to it. However, since it is unavailable in practice, the empirical loss function is employed. This leads to the following minimization problem:

$$
\min  _ {h \in \mathcal {H} _ {n}} \operatorname {L o s s} _ {m} \left(h; \lambda , \lambda^ {R}\right). \tag {2.4}
$$

We then hope a minimizer of the empirical loss to be close to the solution to the PDE (2.1).

We remark that in general, global minimizers to the problem of (2.4) need not exist. However, for  $\epsilon > 0$ , there always exists a  $\epsilon$ -suboptimal global solution  $h^{\epsilon} \in \mathcal{H}_n$  [19] satisfying  $\mathrm{Loss}_m(h^\epsilon) \leq \inf_{h \in \mathcal{H}_n} \mathrm{Loss}_m(h) + \epsilon$ . All the results of the present paper remain valid if one replaces global minimizers to  $\epsilon$ -suboptimal global minimizers for sufficiently small  $\epsilon$ . Henceforth, for the sake of readability, we assume the existence of at least one global minimizer of the minimization problem (2.4).

When  $\lambda^R = 0$ , we refer to the (either empirical or expected) loss as the (empirical or expected) PINN loss [32]. We denote the empirical and the expected PINN losses by  $\mathrm{Loss}_m^{\mathrm{PINN}}(h;\lambda)$  and

Loss $^{\mathrm{PINN}}(h;\lambda)$ , respectively. Specifically,

$$
\operatorname {L o s s} _ {m} ^ {\text {P I N N}} (h; \lambda) = \frac {\lambda_ {r}}{m _ {r}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {m _ {r}} \| \mathcal {L} [ h ] \left(\mathbf {x} _ {r} ^ {i}\right) - f \left(\mathbf {x} _ {r} ^ {i}\right) \| ^ {2} + \frac {\lambda_ {b}}{m _ {b}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {m _ {b}} \| \mathcal {B} [ h ] \left(\mathbf {x} _ {b} ^ {i}\right) - g \left(\mathbf {x} _ {b} ^ {i}\right) \| ^ {2}, \tag {2.5}
$$

$$
\mathrm {L o s s} ^ {\mathrm {P I N N}} (h; \boldsymbol {\lambda}) = \lambda_ {r} \| \mathcal {L} [ h ] - f \| _ {L ^ {2} (U; \mu)} ^ {2} + \lambda_ {b} \| \mathcal {B} [ h ] - g \| _ {L ^ {2} (\Gamma ; \mu_ {b})} ^ {2}.
$$

Also, note that for  $\lambda \leq \lambda'$  and  $\lambda^R \leq \lambda'^R$  (element-wise inequality), we have  $\mathrm{Loss}_m(h; \lambda, \lambda^R) \leq \mathrm{Loss}_m(h; \lambda', \lambda'^R)$ .

# 2.1 Function Spaces and Regular Boundary

Throughout this paper, we adopt the notation from [14, 15]. Let  $U$  be a bounded domain in  $\mathbb{R}^d$ . Let  $\mathbf{x} = (x_1,\dots ,x_d)$  be a point in  $\mathbb{R}^d$ . For a positive integer  $k$ , let  $C^k (U)$  be the set of functions having all derivatives of order  $\leq k$  continuous in  $U$ . Also, let  $C^k (\overline{U})$  be the set of functions in  $C^k (U)$  whose derivatives of order  $\leq k$  have continuous extensions to  $\overline{U}$  (the closure of  $U$ ).

We call a function  $u$  uniformly Hölder continuous with exponent  $\alpha$  in  $U$  if the quantity

$$
[ u ] _ {\alpha ; U} = \sup  _ {x, y \in U, x \neq y} \frac {| u (x) - u (y) |}{\| x - y \| ^ {\alpha}} <   \infty , \quad 0 <   \alpha \leq 1, \tag {2.6}
$$

is finite. Also, we call a function  $u$  locally Hölder continuous with exponent  $\alpha$  in  $U$  if  $u$  is uniformly Hölder continuous with exponent  $\alpha$  on compact subsets of  $U$ .  $[u]_{\alpha;U}$  is called the Hölder constant (coefficient) of  $u$  on  $U$ .

Given a multi-index  $\mathbf{k} = (k_1, \dots, k_d)$ , we define

$$
D ^ {\mathbf {k}} u = \frac {\partial^ {| \mathbf {k} |} u}{\partial x _ {1} ^ {k _ {1}} \cdots \partial x _ {d} ^ {k _ {d}}},
$$

where  $|\mathbf{k}| = \sum_{i=1}^{d} k_i$ . Let  $\mathbf{k} = (k_1, \dots, k_d)$  and  $\mathbf{k}' = (k_1', \dots, k_d')$ . If  $k_j' \leq k_j$  for all  $j$ , we write  $\mathbf{k}' \leq \mathbf{k}$ . Let us define

$$
[ u ] _ {j, 0; U} := \sup  _ {| \mathbf {k} | = j} \sup  _ {U} | D ^ {\mathbf {k}} u |, \qquad j = 0, 1, 2 \dots ,
$$

$$
[ u ] _ {j, \alpha ; U} := \sup  _ {| \mathbf {k} | = j} [ D ^ {\mathbf {k}} u ] _ {\alpha ; U} = \sup  _ {| \mathbf {k} | = j} \left[ \sup  _ {x, y \in U, x \neq y} \frac {\| D ^ {\mathbf {k}} u (x) - D ^ {\mathbf {k}} u (y) \|}{\| x - y \| ^ {\alpha}} \right].
$$

Definition 2.1. For a positive integer  $k$  and  $0 < \alpha \leq 1$ , the Hölder spaces  $C^{k,\alpha}(\overline{U})$  ( $C^{k,\alpha}(U)$ ) are the subspaces of  $C^k (\overline{U})$  ( $C^k (U)$ ) consisting of all functions  $u\in C^k (\overline{U})$  ( $C^k (U)$ ) satisfying  $\sum_{j = 0}^{k}[u]_{j,0;U} + [u]_{k,\alpha ;U} < \infty$ .

For simplicity, we often write  $C^{k,0} = C^k$  and  $C^{0,\alpha} = C^{\alpha}$  for  $0 < \alpha < 1$ . The related norms are defined on  $C^k (\overline{U})$  and  $C^{k,\alpha}(\overline{U})$ , respectively, by

$$
\| u \| _ {C ^ {k} (\overline {{U}})} = \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {k} [ u ] _ {j, 0; U}, \qquad \| u \| _ {C ^ {k, \alpha} (\overline {{U}})} = \| u \| _ {C ^ {k} (\overline {{U}})} + [ u ] _ {k, \alpha ; U}, \qquad 0 <   \alpha \leq 1.
$$

With these norms,  $C^k(\overline{U})$  and  $C^{k,\alpha}(\overline{U})$  are Banach spaces. Also, we denote  $\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_j}$  as  $D_ju$  and  $\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial x_i\partial x_j}$  as  $D_{ij}u$ .

Definition 2.2. A bounded domain  $U$  in  $\mathbb{R}^d$  and its boundary are said to be of class  $C^{k,\alpha}$  where  $0\leq \alpha \leq 1$ , if at each point  $x_0\in \partial U$  there is a ball  $B = B(x_0)$  and a one-to-one mapping  $\psi$  of  $B$  onto  $D\subset \mathbb{R}^d$  such that (i)  $\psi (B\cap U)\subset \mathbb{R}_+^d$ , (ii)  $\psi (B\cap \partial U)\subset \partial \mathbb{R}_+^d$ , (iii)  $\psi \in C^{k,\alpha}(B),\psi^{-1}\in C^{k,\alpha}(D)$ .

For the parabolic equations, we introduce function spaces for interior estimates following [14]. For  $T > 0$  and a bounded domain  $U$  in  $\mathbb{R}^d$ , let  $\Omega = U \times (0,T)$ ,  $B = U \times \{0\}$ ,  $S_{\tau} = \partial U \times [0,\tau]$ . For any two points  $P = (\mathbf{x},t)$  and  $Q = (\mathbf{x}',t')$  in  $\Omega$ , let  $d(P,Q) = (\| \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'\| ^2 +|t - t'|)^{1 / 2}$  where  $\| \cdot \|$  is the Euclidean norm. For  $Q = (\mathbf{x},\tau)$ , let  $d_{Q}\coloneqq \inf_{P\in B\cup S_{\tau}}d(P,Q)$  be the distance from  $Q$  to  $B\cup S_{\tau}$ . For any two points in  $P,Q\in \Omega$ , let  $d_{PQ} = \min (d_P,d_Q)$ . Let  $D_{t} = \partial /\partial t$ . Let us define

$$
|u|_{\alpha} = \sup_{P\in \Omega}|u(P)| + \sup_{P,Q\in \Omega}d_{PQ}^{\alpha}\frac{|u(P) - u(Q)|}{d(P,Q)^{\alpha}},
$$

$$
\left| \mathrm {d} ^ {m} u \right| _ {\alpha} = \sup  _ {P \in \Omega} d _ {P} ^ {m} | u (P) | + \sup  _ {P, Q \in \Omega} d _ {P Q} ^ {m + \alpha} \frac {| u (P) - u (Q) |}{d (P , Q) ^ {\alpha}}, \tag {2.7}
$$

$$
| u | _ {2 + \alpha} = | u | _ {\alpha} + \sum_ {i} | \mathrm {d} D _ {i} u | _ {\alpha} + \sum_ {i, j} | \mathrm {d} D _ {i j} u | _ {\alpha} + | \mathrm {d} ^ {2} D _ {t} u | _ {\alpha}.
$$

Let  $C_{\alpha}(\Omega)$  and  $C_{2 + \alpha}(\Omega)$  be the space of all functions  $u$  with finite norm  $|u|_{\alpha}$  and  $|u|_{2 + \alpha}$ , respectively. They are Banach spaces. We refer the readers to [14] for more details.

# 2.2 Neural Networks

Let  $h^L: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}}$  be a feed-forward neural network having  $L$  layers and  $n_\ell$  neurons in the  $\ell$ -th layer. The weights and biases in the  $l$ -th layer are represented by a weight matrix  $\mathbf{W}^l \in \mathbb{R}^{n_l \times n_{l-1}}$  and a bias vector  $\mathbf{b}^l \in \mathbb{R}^{n_l}$ , respectively. Let  $\pmb{\theta}_L := \{\pmb{W}^j, \pmb{b}^j\}_{1 \leq j \leq L}$ . For notational completeness, let  $n_0 = d$  and  $n_L = d_{\mathrm{out}}$ . For a fixed positive integer  $L$ , let  $\vec{n} = (n_0, n_1, \dots, n_L) \in \mathbb{N}^{L+1}$  where  $\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$ . Then,  $\vec{n}$  describes a network architecture. Given an activation function  $\sigma(\cdot)$  which is applied element-wise, the feed-forward neural network is defined by

$$
h ^ {\ell} (\mathbf {x}) = \boldsymbol {W} ^ {\ell} \sigma \left(h ^ {\ell - 1} (\mathbf {x})\right) + \boldsymbol {b} ^ {\ell} \in \mathbb {R} ^ {N _ {\ell}}, \quad \text {f o r} \quad 2 \leq \ell \leq L
$$

and  $h^1(\mathbf{x}) = W^1\mathbf{x} + b^1$ . The input is  $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_0}$ , and the output of the  $\ell$ -th layer is  $h^\ell(\mathbf{x}) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_\ell}$ . Popular choices of activation functions include the sigmoid  $(1/(1 + e^{-x}))$ , the hyperbolic tangent  $(\tanh(x))$ , and the rectified linear unit  $(\max\{x, 0\})$ . Note that  $h^L$  is called a  $(L-1)$ -hidden layer neural network or a  $L$ -layer neural network.

Since a network  $h^L(\mathbf{x})$  depends on the network parameters  $\pmb{\theta}_L$  and the architecture  $\vec{n}$ , we often denote  $h^L(\mathbf{x})$  by  $h^L(\mathbf{x}; \vec{n}, \pmb{\theta}_L)$ . If  $\vec{n}$  is clear in the context, we simple write  $h^L(\mathbf{x}; \pmb{\theta}_L)$ . Given a network architecture, we define a neural network function class

$$
\mathcal {H} _ {\vec {n}} ^ {\mathrm {N N}} = \left\{h ^ {L} (\cdot ; \vec {n}, \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {L}): \mathbb {R} ^ {d} \mapsto \mathbb {R} ^ {d _ {\text {o u t}}} \mid \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {L} = \left\{\left(\boldsymbol {W} ^ {j}, \boldsymbol {b} ^ {j}\right) \right\} _ {j = 1} ^ {L} \right\}. \tag {2.8}
$$

Since  $h^L(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}_L)$  is parameterized by  $\boldsymbol{\theta}_L$ , the problem of (2.4) with  $\mathcal{H}_{\vec{n}}^{\mathrm{NN}}$  is equivalent to

$$
\min  _ {\boldsymbol {\theta} _ {L}} \operatorname {L o s s} _ {\boldsymbol {m}} (\boldsymbol {\theta} _ {L}; \boldsymbol {\lambda}, \boldsymbol {\lambda} ^ {R}), \quad \text {w h e r e} \quad \operatorname {L o s s} _ {\boldsymbol {m}} (\boldsymbol {\theta} _ {L}; \boldsymbol {\lambda}, \boldsymbol {\lambda} ^ {R}) = \operatorname {L o s s} _ {\boldsymbol {m}} (h (\mathbf {x}; \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {L}); \boldsymbol {\lambda}, \boldsymbol {\lambda} ^ {R}).
$$

Throughout this paper, the activation function is assumed to be sufficiently smooth, which is common in practice.

Lemma 2.1. Let  $U$  be a bounded domain and  $\mathcal{H}_{\vec{n}}^{NN}$  be a class of neural networks whose architecture is  $\vec{n} = (n_0,\dots ,n_L)$  whose activation function  $\sigma (x)\in C^{k^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R})$  satisfies that for each  $s\in \{0,\dots ,k\}$  where  $k < k'$ ,  $\frac{d^s\sigma(x)}{dx^s}$  is bounded and Lipschitz continuous. For a function  $u\in C^k (\overline{U})$ , let  $\{h_j\}_{j = 1}^{\infty}$  be a sequence of networks in  $\mathcal{H}_{\vec{n}}^{NN}$  such that the associated weights and biases are uniformly bounded and  $h_j\rightarrow u$  in  $C^0 (\overline{U})$ . Then,  $h_j\rightarrow u$  in  $C^k (\overline{U})$ .

Proof. The proof can be found in Appendix A.

It can be checked that the tanh activation function satisfies the conditions of Lemma 2.1 for all  $k$  as follows: Note that  $\tanh(x)$  is bounded by 1 and is 1-Lipschitz continuous. The  $k$ -th derivative of  $\tanh(x)$  is expressed as a polynomial of  $\tanh(x)$  with a finite degree, which shows both the boundedness and Lipschitz continuity.

Remark 2.2. In what follows, we simply write  $\mathcal{H}_{\vec{n}}^{NN}$  as  $\mathcal{H}_m$ , assuming  $\vec{n}$  depends on  $m$  and possibly implicitly on the data samples itself. Since neural networks are universal approximators, the network architecture  $\vec{n}$  is expected to grow proportionally on  $m$ .

# 3 Convergence Analysis

If the expected loss function were available, a function that minimizes it would be sought. However, since the expected loss is not available in practice, the empirical loss function is employed. We first derive an upper bound of the expected PINN loss (2.5). The bound involves a specific regularized empirical loss.

The derivation is based on the probabilistic space filling arguments [7, 13]. In this regard, we make the following assumptions on the training data distributions.

Assumption 3.1. Let  $U$  be a bounded domain in  $\mathbb{R}^d$  that is at least of class  $C^{0,1}$  and  $\Gamma$  be a closed subset of  $\partial U$ . Let  $\mu_r$  and  $\mu_b$  be probability distributions defined on  $U$  and  $\Gamma$ , respectively. Let  $\rho_r$  be the probability density of  $\mu_r$  with respect to  $d$ -dimensional Lebesgue measure on  $U$ . Let  $\rho_b$  be the probability density of  $\mu_b$  with respect to  $(d - 1)$ -dimensional Hausdorff measure on  $\Gamma$ .

1.  $\rho_r$  and  $\rho_b$  are supported on  $\overline{U}$  and  $\Gamma$ , respectively. Also,  $\inf_{U} \rho_r > 0$  and  $\inf_{\Gamma} \rho_b > 0$ .  
2. For  $\epsilon > 0$ , there exists partitions of  $U$  and  $\Gamma$ ,  $\{U_j^\epsilon\}_{j=1}^{K_r}$  and  $\{\Gamma_j^\epsilon\}_{j=1}^{K_b}$  that depend on  $\epsilon$  such that for each  $j$ , there are cubes  $H_\epsilon(\mathbf{z}_{j,r})$  and  $H_\epsilon(\mathbf{z}_{j,b})$  of side length  $\epsilon$  centered at  $\mathbf{z}_{j,r} \in U_j^\epsilon$  and  $\mathbf{z}_{j,b} \in \Gamma_j^\epsilon$ , respectively, satisfying  $U_j^\epsilon \subset H_\epsilon(\mathbf{z}_{j,r})$  and  $\Gamma_j^\epsilon \subset H_\epsilon(\mathbf{z}_{j,b})$ .

3. There exists positive constants  $c_{r}, c_{b}$  such that  $\forall \epsilon > 0$ , the partitions from the above satisfy  $c_{r} \epsilon^{d} \leq \mu_{r}(U_{j}^{\epsilon})$  and  $c_{b} \epsilon^{d-1} \leq \mu_{b}(\Gamma_{j}^{\epsilon})$  for all  $j$ .

There exists positive constants  $C_r, C_b$  such that  $\forall \mathbf{x}_r \in U$  and  $\forall \mathbf{x}_b \in \Gamma$ ,  $\mu_r(B_\epsilon(\mathbf{x}_r) \cap U) \leq C_r \epsilon^d$  and  $\mu_b(B_\epsilon(\mathbf{x}_b) \cap \Gamma) \leq C_b \epsilon^{d-1}$  where  $B_\epsilon(\mathbf{x})$  is a closed ball of radius  $\epsilon$  centered at  $\mathbf{x}$ .

Here  $C_r, c_r$  depend only on  $(U, \mu_r)$  and  $C_b, c_b$  depend only on  $(\Gamma, \mu_b)$ .

4. When  $d = 1$ , we assume that all boundary points are available. Thus, no random sample is needed on the boundary.

We remark that Assumption 3.1 guarantees that random samples drawn from probability distributions can fill up both the interior of the domain  $U$  and the boundary  $\partial U$ . These are mild assumptions and can be satisfied in many practical cases. For example, let  $U = (0,1)^d$ . Then the uniform probability distributions on both  $U$  and  $\partial U$  satisfy Assumption 3.1.

We now state our result that bounds the expected PINN loss in terms of a regularized empirical loss. Let us recall that  $\pmb{m}$  is the vector of the number of training data points, i.e.,  $\pmb{m} = (m_r, m_b)$ . The constants  $c_r, C_r, c_b, C_b$  are introduced in Assumption 3.1. For a function  $u$ ,  $[u]_{\alpha;U}$  is the Hölder constant of  $u$  with exponent  $\alpha$  in  $U$  (2.6).

Theorem 3.1. Suppose Assumption 3.1 holds. Let  $m_r$  and  $m_b$  be the number of iid samples from  $\mu_r$  and  $\mu_b$ , respectively. For some  $0 < \alpha \leq 1$ , let  $h, f, g$ ,  $R_r(h)$ ,  $R_b(h)$  satisfy

$$
\left[ \mathcal {L} [ h ] \right] _ {\alpha ; U} ^ {2} \leq R _ {r} (h) <   \infty , \quad \left[ \mathcal {B} [ h ] \right] _ {\alpha ; \Gamma} ^ {2} \leq R _ {b} (h) <   \infty , \quad \left[ f \right] _ {\alpha ; U ^ {\prime}} \left[ g \right] _ {\alpha ; \Gamma} <   \infty .
$$

Let  $\lambda = (\lambda_{r},\lambda_{b})$  be a fixed vector. Let  $\hat{\lambda}_{m}^{R} = (\hat{\lambda}_{r,m}^{R},\hat{\lambda}_{b,m}^{R})$  be a vector whose elements to be defined. For  $d\geq 2$  , with probability at least,  $(1 - \sqrt{m_r}(1 - 1 / \sqrt{m_r})^{m_r})(1 - \sqrt{m_b}(1 - 1 / \sqrt{m_b})^{m_b})$  we have

$$
L o s s ^ {P I N N} (h; \lambda) \leq C _ {m} \cdot L o s s _ {m} (h; \lambda , \hat {\lambda} _ {m} ^ {R}) + C ^ {\prime} \left(m _ {r} ^ {- \frac {\alpha}{d}} + m _ {b} ^ {- \frac {\alpha}{d - 1}}\right),
$$

where  $\kappa_r = \frac{C_r}{c_r}$ ,  $\kappa_b = \frac{C_b}{c_b}$ ,  $C_m = 3\max \{\kappa_r\sqrt{d}^d m_r^{\frac{1}{2}}, \kappa_b\sqrt{d}^{d - 1}m_b^{\frac{1}{2}}\}$ ,  $C'$  is a universal constant that depends only on  $\lambda, d, c_r, c_b, \alpha, f, g$ , and

$$
\hat {\lambda} _ {r, m} ^ {R} = \frac {3 \lambda_ {r} \sqrt {d} ^ {2 \alpha} c _ {r} ^ {- \frac {2 \alpha}{d}}}{C _ {m}} \cdot m _ {r} ^ {- \frac {\alpha}{d}}, \quad \hat {\lambda} _ {b, m} ^ {R} = \frac {3 \lambda_ {b} \sqrt {d} ^ {2 \alpha} c _ {b} ^ {- \frac {2 \alpha}{d - 1}}}{C _ {m}} \cdot m _ {b} ^ {- \frac {\alpha}{d - 1}}. \tag {3.1}
$$

For  $d = 1$ , with probability at least,  $1 - \sqrt{m_r} \left(1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{m_r}}\right)^{m_r}$ , we have

$$
L o s s ^ {P I N N} (h; \lambda) \leq C _ {m} \cdot L o s s _ {m} (h; \lambda , \hat {\lambda} _ {m} ^ {R}) + C ^ {\prime} m _ {r} ^ {- \alpha},
$$

where  $C_m = 3\kappa_r m_r^{\frac{1}{2}}$ ,  $\hat{\lambda}_{r,m}^{R} = \frac{\lambda_{r}c_{r}^{-2\alpha}}{\kappa_{r}} \cdot m_{r}^{-\alpha -\frac{1}{2}}$ ,  $\hat{\lambda}_{b,m}^{R} = 0$  and  $C'$  is a universal constant that depends only on  $\lambda_r, c_r, \alpha, f$ .

Proof. The proof can be found in Appendix B.

□

Let  $\lambda$  be a vector independent of  $m$  and  $\lambda_{m}^{R} = (\lambda_{r,m}^{R},\lambda_{b,m}^{R})$  be a vector satisfying

$$
\lambda_ {m} ^ {R} \geq \hat {\lambda} _ {m} ^ {R}, \quad \| \lambda_ {m} ^ {R} \| _ {\infty} = \mathcal {O} \left(\| \hat {\lambda} _ {m} ^ {R} \| _ {\infty}\right), \tag {3.2}
$$

where  $\hat{\lambda}_m^R$  is defined in (3.1). For a vector  $v$ ,  $\| v \|_{\infty}$  is the maximum norm, i.e.,  $\| v \|_{\infty} = \max_i |v_i|$ . We note that since  $\hat{\lambda}_m^R \to 0$  as  $m \to \infty$ , the above condition implies  $\lambda_m^R \to 0$  as  $m \to \infty$ .

By letting  $R_{r}(h) = \left[\mathcal{L}[h]\right]_{\alpha;U}^{2}$  and  $R_{b}(h) = \left[\mathcal{B}[h]\right]_{\alpha;\Gamma}^{2}$ , let us define the Hölder regularized empirical loss:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathrm {L o s s} _ {m} (h; \lambda , \lambda_ {m} ^ {R}) \\ = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \operatorname {L o s s} _ {m} ^ {\text {P I N N}} (h; \lambda) + \lambda_ {r, m} ^ {R} [ \mathcal {L} [ h ] ] _ {\alpha ; U} ^ {2} + \lambda_ {b, m} ^ {R} [ \mathcal {B} [ h ] ] _ {\alpha ; \Gamma^ {\prime}} ^ {2}, & \text {i f} d \geq 2 \\ \operatorname {L o s s} _ {m} ^ {\text {P I N N}} (h; \lambda) + \lambda_ {r, m} ^ {R} [ \mathcal {L} [ h ] ] _ {\alpha ; U ^ {\prime}} ^ {2}, & \text {i f} d = 1 \end{array} \right. \tag {3.3} \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $\lambda_{m}^{R}$  are vectors satisfying (3.2). We note that the Hölder regularized loss (3.3) is greater than or equal to the empirical loss shown in Theorem 3.1 (assuming  $R_{r}(h) = \left[\mathcal{L}[h]\right]_{\alpha;U}^{2}$  and  $R_{b}(h) = \left[\mathcal{B}[h]\right]_{\alpha;\Gamma}^{2}$ ). Since  $\left[\mathcal{L}[h]\right]_{\alpha;U}^{2}$  and  $\left[\mathcal{B}[h]\right]_{\alpha;\Gamma}^{2}$  do not depend on the training data and  $\hat{\lambda}_{r,m}^{R}, \hat{\lambda}_{b,m}^{R} \to 0$  as  $m_{r}, m_{b} \to \infty$ , this suggests that the more data we have, the less regularization is needed.

Theorem 3.1 indicates that minimizing the Hölder regularized loss (3.3) over  $h$  results in minimizing an upper bound of the expected PINN loss (2.5). Thus the Hölder regularized loss could be used as a loss function in a way to have a small expected PINN loss. In general, however, the Hölder-regularization functionals (the Hölder constants) are impractical to be evaluated numerically. Also, minimizing an upper bound does not necessarily imply minimizing the expected PINN loss.

If the domain is convex and the Hölder exponent is  $\alpha = 1$  (i.e., Lipschitz constant), it follows from Rademacher's Theorem [12] that the Lipschitz constant of  $\mathcal{L}[h]$  is the supremum of the sup norm of  $\nabla \mathcal{L}[h]$  over  $U$  (assuming  $\nabla \mathcal{L}[h]$  exists). Thus, in practice, one can use the maximum of the sup norm of the derivative over the set of training data points to estimate the Lipschitz constant. The resulting Lipschitz regularized (LIPR) loss is given by

$$
\operatorname {L o s s} _ {m} ^ {\mathrm {L I P R}} (h; \lambda , \lambda^ {R}) = \operatorname {L o s s} _ {m} ^ {\mathrm {P I N N}} (h; \lambda) + \lambda_ {r} ^ {R} \max  _ {1 \leq i \leq m _ {r}} \| \nabla \mathcal {L} [ h ] \left(\mathbf {x} _ {r} ^ {i}\right) \| _ {\infty} ^ {2} + \lambda_ {b} ^ {R} \max  _ {1 \leq j \leq m _ {b}} \| \nabla \mathcal {B} [ h ] \left(\mathbf {x} _ {b} ^ {j}\right) \| _ {\infty} ^ {2}, \tag {3.4}
$$

where  $\mathrm{Loss}_m^{\mathrm{PINN}}(h;\lambda)$  is defined in (2.5) and  $\lambda^R = (\lambda_r^R,\lambda_b^R)$  are weights of the regularization penalty terms.

# 3.1 Expected PINN Loss

We now consider the problem of minimizing the Hölder regularized loss (3.3). In particular, we want to quantify how well a minimizer of the regularized empirical loss performs on the expected PINN loss (2.5).

We make the following assumptions on the classes of neural networks for the minimization problems (2.4).

Assumption 3.2. Let  $k$  be the highest order of the derivative shown in the PDE (2.1). For some  $0 < \alpha \leq 1$ , let  $f \in C^{0,\alpha}(U)$  and  $g \in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma)$ .

1. For each  $m$ , let  $\mathcal{H}_m$  be a class of neural networks in  $C^{k,\alpha}(U) \cap C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{U})$  such that for any  $h \in \mathcal{H}_m$ ,  $\mathcal{L}[h] \in C^{0,\alpha}(U)$  and  $\mathcal{B}[h] \in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma)$ .  
2. For each  $m$ ,  $\mathcal{H}_m$  contains a network  $u_m^*$  satisfying  $\text{Loss}_m^{\text{PINN}}(u_m^*; \lambda) = 0$ .

3. And,

$$
\sup  _ {m} \left[ \mathcal {L} [ u _ {m} ^ {*} ] \right] _ {\alpha ; U} <   \infty , \quad \sup  _ {m} \left[ \mathcal {B} [ u _ {m} ^ {*} ] \right] _ {\alpha ; \Gamma} <   \infty .
$$

All the assumptions are essential for the proof. Our proof relies on the use of Assumption 3.2 that guarantees the uniform equicontinuity of subsequences. All assumptions hold automatically if  $\mathcal{H}_m$  contains the solution to the PDE for all  $m$ . For example, [8,9] showed that the solution to some Hamilton-Jacobi PDEs can be exactly represented by neural networks. The second assumption could be relaxed, however, we do not discuss it here for the readability (See Appendix C).

Remark 3.1. The choice of classes of neural networks  $\mathcal{H}_m$  may depend on many factors including the underlying PDEs, the training data, and the network architecture. Assumption 3.2 provides a set of conditions for neural networks for the purpose of our analysis. We will not discuss this issue further in the present paper as it beyond our scope.

For the rest of this paper, we use the following notation. When the number of the initial/boundary training data points  $m_{b}$  is completely determined by the number of residual points  $m_{r}$  (e.g.  $m_{r}^{d - 1} = \mathcal{O}(m_{b}^{d})$ ), the vector of the number of training data  $m$  depends only on  $m_{r}$ . In this case, we simply write  $\mathcal{H}_{m}, \lambda_{m}^{R}$ ,  $\mathrm{Loss}_{m}$  as  $\mathcal{H}_{m_r}, \lambda_{m_r}^R$ ,  $\mathrm{Loss}_{m_r}$ , respectively.

We now show that minimizers of the Hölder regularized loss (3.3) indeed produce a small expected PINN loss.

Theorem 3.2. Suppose Assumptions 3.1 and 3.2 hold. Let  $m_r$  and  $m_b$  be the number of iid samples from  $\mu_r$  and  $\mu_b$ , respectively, and  $m_r = \mathcal{O}(m_b^{\frac{d}{d-1}})$ . Let  $\lambda_{m_r}^R$  be a vector satisfying (3.2). Let  $h_{m_r} \in \mathcal{H}_{m_r}$  be a minimizer of the Hölder regularized loss  $Loss_{m_r}(\cdot; \lambda, \lambda_{m_r}^R)$  (3.3). Then the following holds.

- With probability at least  $(1 - \sqrt{m_r} (1 - c_r / \sqrt{m_r})^{m_r})(1 - \sqrt{m_b} (1 - c_b / \sqrt{m_b})^{m_b})$  over iid samples,

$$
L o s s ^ {P I N N} \left(h _ {m _ {r}}; \lambda\right) = \mathcal {O} \left(m _ {r} ^ {- \frac {\alpha}{d}}\right).
$$

- With probability 1 over iid samples,

$$
\lim  _ {m _ {r} \rightarrow \infty} \mathcal {L} \left[ h _ {m _ {r}} \right] = f \text {i n} C ^ {0} (U), \quad \lim  _ {m _ {r} \rightarrow \infty} \mathcal {B} \left[ h _ {m _ {r}} \right] = g \text {i n} C ^ {0} (\Gamma). \tag {3.5}
$$

Proof. The proof can be found in Appendix C.

□

Remark 3.2. The main goal of this section is to establish the convergence of  $\mathcal{L}[h_{m_r}]$  and  $\mathcal{B}[h_{m_r}]$  as  $m_r\to \infty$ . Theorem 3.2 provides a general convergence result under the aforementioned conditions on classes of neural networks. The derived rate of convergence is largely due to the probabilistic space filling argument [7, 13].

Theorem 3.2 shows that the expected PINN loss (2.5) at minimizers of the Hölder regularized losses (3.3) converges to zero. And more importantly, it shows the uniform convergences of  $\mathcal{L}[h_{m_r}] \to f$  and  $\mathcal{B}[h_{m_r}] \to g$  as  $m_r \to \infty$ . (In fact, the mode of convergence is  $C^{0,\beta}$  for all  $\beta < \alpha$  by the compact embedding of Hölder spaces). These results are meaningful, however, it is insufficient to claim the convergence of  $h_{m_r}$  to the solution to the PDE (2.1). In the next subsections, we consider two classes of PDEs and discuss the convergence of neural networks.

Remark that the uniform convergences of (3.5) do not necessarily imply the convergence of  $h_m$  to the solution  $u^*$ . For example, let  $f_n(x) = n\cos(x/n)$  on  $U = (-1,1)$ . It can be checked that for any  $k \geq 1$ , the  $k$ -th derivative of  $f_n$  converges to 0 uniformly. However,  $f_n(x)$  does not even converge.

# 3.2 Linear Elliptic PDEs

We consider the second-order linear elliptic PDEs with the Dirichlet boundary condition:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \mathcal {L} [ u ] = f & \text {i n} U, \\ u = g & \text {i n} \partial U, \end{array} \right. \tag {3.6}
$$

where  $f\colon U\to \mathbb{R}$ $g:\partial U\mapsto \mathbb{R}$  , and

$$
\mathcal {L} [ u ] = \sum_ {i, j = 1} ^ {d} D _ {i} \left(a ^ {i j} (\mathbf {x}) D _ {j} u + b ^ {i} (x) u\right) + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {d} c ^ {i} (\mathbf {x}) D _ {i} u + d (\mathbf {x}) u.
$$

The coefficients are defined on  $U$ . To guarantee the existence, regularity and uniqueness of the classical solution to (3.6), we adopt the Schauder approach (Chapter 6 of [15]). For the proof, the following assumptions are made.

Assumption 3.3. Let  $\lambda (\mathbf{x})$  be the minimum eigenvalues of  $[a^{ij}(\mathbf{x})]$  and  $\alpha \in (0,1)$ .

1. (Uniformly elliptic) For some constant  $\lambda_0, \lambda(\mathbf{x}) \geq \lambda_0 > 0$  in  $U$  and  $a^{ij} = a^{ji}$ .  
2. The coefficients  $c^i,d$  of the operator  $\mathcal{L}$  are in  $C^\alpha (U)$  
3.  $a^{ij}$  and  $b^i$  are in  $C^{1,\alpha}(U)$ . Also,  $f\in C^{0,\alpha}(U)$  and  $g\in C^{0,\alpha}(\partial U)$ ,  
4.  $U$  satisfies the exterior sphere condition at every boundary point.  
5. There are constants  $\Lambda, v \geq 0$  such that for all  $x \in U$

$$
\sum_ {i, j} | a ^ {i j} (\mathbf {x}) | ^ {2} \leq \Lambda , \quad \lambda_ {0} ^ {- 2} (\| \boldsymbol {b} (\mathbf {x}) \| ^ {2} + \| \boldsymbol {c} (\mathbf {x}) \| ^ {2}) + \lambda_ {0} ^ {- 1} | d (\mathbf {x}) | \leq v ^ {2},
$$

where  $\pmb {b}(\mathbf{x}) = [b^{1}(\mathbf{x}),\dots ,b^{d}(\mathbf{x})]^{T}$  and  $c(\mathbf{x}) = [c^1 (\mathbf{x}),\dots ,c^d (\mathbf{x})]^T$

We now present our main convergence result for the linear elliptic PDEs.

Theorem 3.3. Suppose Assumptions 3.1, 3.2 and 3.3 hold and  $\tilde{c}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^{d} D_i b^i(\boldsymbol{x}) + d(\boldsymbol{x}) \leq 0$ . Let  $m_r$  and  $m_b$  be the number of iid samples from  $\mu_r$  and  $\mu_b$ , respectively, and  $m_r = \mathcal{O}(m_b^{\frac{d}{d-1}})$ . Let  $\lambda_{m_r}^R$  be a vector satisfying (3.2). Let  $h_{m_r} \in \mathcal{H}_{m_r}$  be a minimizer of the Hölder regularized loss  $Loss_{m_r}(:, \lambda, \lambda_{m_r}^R)$  (3.3). Then the following holds.

1. For each PDE data set  $\{f,g\}$  satisfying Assumption 3.3, there exists a unique classical solution  $u^{*}$  to the PDE (3.6).

2. With probability 1 over iid samples,

$$
\lim  _ {m _ {r} \to \infty} h _ {m _ {r}} = u ^ {*}, \qquad i n   C ^ {0} (U).
$$

Proof. The proof can be found in Appendix D.

Theorem 3.3 shows that neural networks that minimize the Hölder regularized empirical losses (3.3) converge to the unique classical solution to the PDE (3.6). This answers the question we posed in the introduction for linear second-order elliptic PDEs. It shows the consistency of PINNs in the sample limit. Equivalently, it can be interpreted as the convergence of generalization error measured with the uniform topology.

Next, we show that if each minimizer exactly satisfies the boundary conditions, the mode of convergence becomes  $H^1$ .

Theorem 3.4. Under the same conditions of Theorem 3.3, suppose each minimizer of (2.4) satisfies the boundary conditions. Then, with probability 1 over iid samples, the sequence of minimizers stated in Theorem 3.3 converges to the solution  $u^{*}$  to the PDE (3.6) in  $H^{1}(U)$ .

Proof. The proof can be found in Appendix E.

In the literature, there are several ways to enforce neural networks to satisfy the boundary conditions. The work of [22] considered the function classes that exactly satisfy the boundary conditions. The idea was then extended and generalized in [4, 23] to deal with irregular domains. This approach consists of two steps. First, extra structures are added on neural networks. The resulting surrogate is a sum of two networks where one is the boundary network that is designed for fitting boundary data and the other is for fitting residual data. Importantly, the boundary network is pre-trained (or trained first) to fit the boundary data. The rest of learning is done on the function that (approximately) satisfy the boundary conditions.

PINNs [23, 32] do require neither extra structures on the solution surrogate nor pre-training with the boundary data. However, it has been empirically reported [4, 23, 37] that properly chosen boundary weights  $(\lambda_{b})$  could accelerate the overall training and result in a better performance. It was mentioned in [23] that the boundary weight takes a large positive value to accurately satisfy the boundary conditions.

On the top of these existing works, Theorem 3.5 theoretically sheds light on the importance of learning the boundary conditions.

Finally, we show that when the solution to the PDE (3.6) is exactly represented by a neural network with a fixed architecture, one can further improve the mode of convergence.

Corollary 3.5. Under the same conditions of Lemma 2.1 and Theorem 3.3, suppose there exists a class  $\mathcal{H}_{\vec{n}}^{\mathrm{NN}}$  of neural networks with a fixed architecture (2.8) such that  $u^{*} \in \mathcal{H}_{\vec{n}}^{\mathrm{NN}}$  where  $u^{*}$  is the solution to the PDE (3.6). Let  $\mathcal{H}_{m_r} = \mathcal{H}_{\vec{n}}^{\mathrm{NN}}$  for all  $m_r$ . Then, with probability 1 over iid samples, the sequence of minimizers stated in Theorem 3.3 converges to  $u^{*}$  in  $C^k(U)$ , where  $k$  is stated in Lemma 2.1.

Proof. By Theorem 3.3, we have  $\| h_{m_r} - u^*\|_{C^0 (U)}\to 0$  as  $m_r\to \infty$ . Since  $u^{*}\in \mathcal{H}_{\vec{n}}^{\mathrm{NN}}$ ,  $u^{*}\in C^{k}(\overline{U})$  as well. It then follows from Lemma 2.1 that  $\| h_{m_r} - u^*\|_{C^k (U)}\to 0$  as  $m_r\to \infty$ .

# 3.3 Linear Parabolic PDEs

In this section, we consider the second-order linear parabolic equations. Let  $U$  be a bounded domain in  $\mathbb{R}^d$  and let  $U_{T} = U\times (0,T]$  for some fixed time  $T > 0$ . Let us denote the parabolic boundary as  $\Gamma_T = \overline{U}_T - U_T$ . Also, let  $(\mathbf{x},t) = (x_{1},\dots ,x_{d},t)$  be a point in  $\mathbb{R}^{d + 1}$ .

Let us consider the initial/boundary-value problem:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l l} - u _ {t} + \mathcal {L} [ u ] = f, & \text {i n} U _ {T} \\ u = \varphi , & \text {i n} \partial U \times [ 0, T ] \\ u = g, & \text {i n} \bar {U} \times \{t = 0 \}, \end{array} \right. \tag {3.7}
$$

where  $f\colon U_T\mapsto \mathbb{R}$ $g\colon \overline{U}\mapsto \mathbb{R}$ $\varphi \colon \partial U\times [0,T]\mapsto \mathbb{R}$ , and

$$
\mathcal {L} [ u ] = \sum_ {i, j = 1} ^ {d} D _ {i} \Big (a ^ {i j} (\mathbf {x}, t) D _ {j} u + b ^ {i} (\mathbf {x}, t) u \Big) + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {d} c ^ {i} (\mathbf {x}, t) D _ {i} u + d (\mathbf {x}, t) u.
$$

In order to ensure the existence, regularity and uniqueness of the PDE (3.7), the following assumptions are made. Again, we follow the Schauder approach [14].

Assumption 3.4. Let  $\lambda (\mathbf{x},t)$  be the minimum eigenvalues of  $[a^{ij}(\mathbf{x},t)]$  and  $\alpha \in (0,1)$ . Suppose  $a^{ij},b^i$  are differentiable and let  $\tilde{b}^i (\mathbf{x},t) = \sum_{j = 1}^d D_ja^{ij}(\mathbf{x},t) + b^i (\mathbf{x},t) + c^i (\mathbf{x},t)$  and  $\tilde{c} (\mathbf{x},t) = \sum_{i = 1}^{d}D_{i}b^{i}(\mathbf{x},t) + d(\mathbf{x},t)$ . Let  $\Omega = U\times (0,T)$ .

1. For some constant  $\lambda_0, \lambda(\mathbf{x}, t) \geq \lambda_0 > 0$  for all  $(\mathbf{x}, t) \in \Omega$ .  
2.  $a^{ij},\tilde{b}^i,\tilde{c}^i$  are Hölder continuous (exponent  $\alpha$ ) in  $\Omega$ , and  $|a^{ij}|_{\alpha},|\mathrm{d}\tilde{b}^i|_{\alpha},|\mathrm{d}^2\tilde{c} |_{\alpha}$  are uniformly bounded.  
3.  $f$  is Hölder continuous (exponent  $\alpha$ ) in  $U_T$  and  $|\mathrm{d}^2 f|_{\alpha} < \infty$ .  $g$  and  $\varphi$  are Hölder continuous (exponent  $\alpha$ ) on  $\overline{U} \times \{t = 0\}$ , and  $\partial U \times [0, T]$ , respectively, and  $g(\mathbf{x}) = \varphi(\mathbf{x}, 0)$  on  $\partial U$ .

4. There exists  $\theta > 0$  such that  $\theta^2 a^{11}(\mathbf{x}, t) + \theta \tilde{b}^1(\mathbf{x}, t) \geq 1$  in  $\Omega$ .  
5. For  $x' \in \partial U$ , there exists a closed ball  $\bar{B}$  in  $\mathbb{R}^d$  such that  $\bar{B} \cap \overline{U} = \{x'\}$ .  
6. There are constants  $\Lambda, v \geq 0$  such that for all  $(\mathbf{x}, t) \in \Omega$

$$
\sum_ {i, j} \left| a ^ {i j} (\mathbf {x}, t) \right| ^ {2} \leq \Lambda , \quad \lambda_ {0} ^ {- 2} \big (\left\| \boldsymbol {b} (\mathbf {x}, t) \right\| ^ {2} + \left\| \boldsymbol {c} (\mathbf {x}, t) \right\| ^ {2}) + \lambda_ {0} ^ {- 1} | d (\mathbf {x}, t) | \leq v ^ {2},
$$

where  $\pmb {b}(\mathbf{x},t) = [b^{1}(\mathbf{x},t),\dots ,b^{d}(\mathbf{x},t)]^{T}$  and  $c(\mathbf{x},t) = [c^1 (\mathbf{x},t),\dots ,c^d (\mathbf{x},t)]^T$

By adopting the notation of Theorem 3.3, we now present the convergence theorem for the linear parabolic PDEs.

Theorem 3.6. Suppose Assumptions 3.1, 3.2 and 3.4 hold and  $\tilde{c}(\boldsymbol{x},t) = \sum_{i=1}^{d} D_i b^i(\boldsymbol{x},t) + d(\boldsymbol{x},t) \leq 0$ . Let  $m_r$  and  $m_b$  be the number of iid samples from  $\mu_r$  and  $\mu_b$ , respectively, and  $m_r = \mathcal{O}(m_b^{\frac{d+1}{d}})$ . Let  $\lambda_{m_r}^R$  be a vector satisfying (3.2). Let  $h_{m_r} \in \mathcal{H}_{m_r}$  be a minimizer of the Hölder regularized loss  $Loss_{m_r}(\cdot; \lambda, \lambda_{m_r}^R)$  (3.3). Then the following holds:

1. For each PDE data set  $\{f,g,\varphi\}$  satisfying Assumption 3.4, there exists a unique classical solution  $u^{*}$  to the PDE (3.7).  
2. With probability 1 over iid samples,

$$
\lim  _ {m _ {r} \rightarrow \infty} h _ {m _ {r}} = u ^ {*}, \quad i n   C ^ {0} (\Omega).
$$

Proof. The proof can be found in Appendix F.

Theorem 3.6 shows that neural networks that minimize the Hölder regularized empirical losses (3.3) converge to the unique classical solution to the PDE (3.7). This answers the question we posed in the introduction for linear second-order parabolic PDEs.

The mode of convergence can be  $L^2(0, T; H^1(U))$  if each minimizer satisfies both the initial and the boundary conditions. The Bochner space  $L^2(0, T; H^1(U))$  [38] consists of all strongly measurable functions  $u: [0, T] \mapsto H^1(U)$  with  $\|u\|_{L^2(0, T; H^1(U))}^2 := \int_0^T \|u(t)\|_{H^1(U)}^2 < \infty$ .

Theorem 3.7. Under the same conditions of Theorem 3.6, suppose the sequence of minimizers of (2.4) satisfies both the boundary and the initial conditions. Then, with probability 1 over iid samples, the sequence of minimizers stated in Theorem 3.6 strongly converges to the solution  $u^{*}$  to (3.7) in  $L^2(0,T;H^1(U))$ .

Proof. The proof can be found in Appendix G.

Again, Theorem 3.7 shows the importance of learning the initial and boundary conditions for the better convergence.

Similarly to Corollary 3.5, when the solution to the PDE (3.7) can be exactly represented by a class of neural networks with a fixed architecture, one could further improve the mode of convergence.

Corollary 3.8. Under the same conditions of Lemma 2.1 and Theorem 3.6, suppose there exists a class  $\mathcal{H}_{\vec{n}}^{\mathrm{NN}}$  of neural networks with a fixed architecture (2.8) such that  $u^{*} \in \mathcal{H}_{\vec{n}}^{\mathrm{NN}}$  where  $u^{*}$  is the solution to the PDE (3.7). Let  $\mathcal{H}_{m_r} = \mathcal{H}_{\vec{n}}^{\mathrm{NN}}$  for all  $m_r$ . Then, with probability 1 over iid samples, the sequence of minimizers stated in Theorem 3.6 converges to  $u^{*}$  in  $C^k(U_T)$ , where  $k$  is stated in Lemma 2.1.

Proof. Since the proof is similar to the proof of Corollary 3.5, we omitted it.

# 4 Computational Examples

In this section, we provide computational examples to illustrate our theoretical findings. Mainly, we show the  $L^2$  and  $H^1$  convergence of the trained neural networks as the number of training data grows. We confine ourselves to one or two-dimensional problems.

As an effort to find a neural network that minimizes the loss, we employ a combination of two optimization methods; Adam [21] and L-BFGS [25]. We consecutively apply these in the order of Adam and L-BFGS. Adam is employed with its default hyper-parameter setting. This combination has been used in other works (e.g. [26]). We employ the Xavier initialization [16] to initialize network parameters.

# 4.1 Poisson's equation

Let us consider the one-dimensional Poisson's equation:

$$
- u _ {x x} (x) = f (x) \quad \text {i n} U = (- 1, 1), \qquad u (- 1) = g, u (1) = h.
$$

The force term is simply obtained by substituting exact solution in the equation. The training data are set to the equidistant points on  $[-1,1]$ . The prediction errors are computed based on the fixed grid of either 10,000 or 50,000 equidistant points on  $[-1,1]$ . The discrete  $L^2$  and  $H^1$  predictive errors are reported. We show the training results by the original PINN loss (2.5) and the Lipschitz regularized loss (3.4). We refer to the results by the original PINN loss as 'PINN' and to the results by the LIPschitz Regularized loss as 'LIPR'. Specifically, the results by 'PINN' and the results by 'LIPR' are obtained by minimizing

$$
\operatorname {L o s s} _ {m _ {r}} ^ {\mathrm {P I N N}} (h) = \frac {1}{m _ {r}} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {m _ {r}} \| \mathcal {L} [ h ] \left(\mathbf {x} _ {r} ^ {j}\right) - f \left(\mathbf {x} _ {r} ^ {j}\right) \| ^ {2} + \frac {\lambda_ {b}}{2} \left(| h (- 1) | ^ {2} + | h (1) | ^ {2}\right), \tag {4.1}
$$

$$
\operatorname {L o s s} _ {m _ {r}} ^ {\mathrm {L I P R}} (h) = \operatorname {L o s s} _ {m _ {r}} ^ {\mathrm {P I N N}} (h) + \lambda_ {r} ^ {R} \max  _ {i} | \nabla \mathcal {L} [ h ] (\mathbf {x} _ {r} ^ {i}) | ^ {2},
$$

respectively. Here  $m_r$  is the number of residual training data points.

We first consider the case where the exact solution is given by  $u^{*}(x) = \tanh (x)$ . For this task, we employ the 2-hidden layer residual neural network  $u_{\mathrm{NN}}$  having 50 neurons at each layer with the tanh-activation function. That is,

$$
u _ {\mathrm {N N}} (x) = W ^ {3} \tanh (W ^ {2} (\mathbf {1} x + \tanh (W ^ {1} x + b ^ {1})) + b ^ {2}) + b ^ {3},
$$

where  $W^{j} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{j} \times n_{j-1}}$ ,  $b^{j} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{j}}$ ,  $1 \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}$  whose entries are all 1, and  $\vec{n} = (n_{0} = 1, n_{1} = 50, n_{2} = 50, n_{3} = 1)$ . We remark that in this case, the solution can be exactly represented by a neural network. In Figure 3, the predictive errors are plotted with respect to the number of training data points from 1 to 10,000. We set  $\lambda_{b} = 1$  and  $\lambda_{r}^{R} = m_{r}^{-1.5}$  as suggested by Theorem 3.1. The maximum number of epochs of Adam is set to 25,000 and the full-batch is used. As expected by Corollary 3.5, the results by 'LIPR' exhibit both  $L^{2}$ - and  $H^{1}$ -convergence of the errors. We see that the rate of convergence approximately follows  $\mathcal{O}(m_r^{-1})$ , which matches the rate of convergence of the expected PINN loss (Theorem 3.2). For reference, we plot a dotted-line showing the  $\mathcal{O}(m_r^{-1})$  rate of convergence with respect to the number of training data. The results by 'PINN' converge very fast with respect to the number of training data and the errors are saturated at around 10 data points. Without any regularization terms, the 'PINN' somehow finds an accurate approximation to  $u^{*}$ . We observe that the predictive errors by 'PINN' stay at the level of  $10^{-6}$  at all time.

# 5 Conclusion

In this work, we prove the consistency of physics informed neural networks (PINNs) in the sample limit. It is equivalent to the convergence of generalization error in the context of machine learning. Upon deriving an upper bound of the expected PINN loss, we obtain a Hölder regularized empirical loss. Under some assumptions, we show that the expected PINN loss at minimizers of the Hölder regularized loss converges to zero. By considering two classes of PDEs – linear second-order elliptic and parabolic that admit classical solutions – we show that with probability 1 over iid training samples, a sequence of neural networks that minimize the Hölder regularized losses converges to the solution to the PDE uniformly. Furthermore, we show that if each minimizer exactly satisfies the boundary conditions, the mode of convergence becomes  $H^1$ . We provided a set of conditions for neural networks on which the convergence can be guaranteed.

# A Proof of Lemma 2.1

Proof. For each  $h_j$ , let  $\pmb{\theta}^j$  be its associated weights and biases. Since  $\{\pmb{\theta}^j\}$  is uniformly bounded, there exists a convergent subsequence, say,  $\{\pmb{\theta}^{j_i}\}$ . Let  $\{h_{j_i}\}$  be its corresponding neural network sequence. Let  $\pmb{\theta}^*$  be the limit of  $\{\pmb{\theta}^{j_i}\}$  and  $h^* \in \mathcal{H}_{\vec{n}}^{NN}$  be the corresponding limit network. Note that since  $\{\pmb{\theta}_j\}$  is uniformly bounded,  $U$  is bounded, and  $\sigma^{(s)}$  is bounded for all  $s \in \{0,\dots ,k\}$ ,  $D^s h_{j_i}$  is bounded continuous in  $\bar{U}$  for all  $s$  with  $0 \leq |s| \leq k$ .

Claim A.1. Let  $\{h^L (\mathbf{x};\pmb{\theta}^{j_i})\}$  be a sequence of  $L$  -layer neural networks having the same architecture  $\vec{n}$  whose activation function  $\sigma$  is of  $C^k (\mathbb{R})$  and  $\frac{d^s\sigma(x)}{dx^s}$  is bounded and Lipschitz continuous for  $s = 0,\dots ,k$ . Let  $s = (s_{1},\dots ,s_{d})$  be a multi-index with  $|\boldsymbol {s}| = \sum_{i = 1}^{d}s_{i}$ . Then, for each  $\pmb{s}$  with  $0\leq |\pmb {s}|\leq k$ , the sequence  $\{D^s h^L (\mathbf{x};\pmb{\theta}^{j_i})\}$  is uniformly convergent to  $D^{s}h^{L}(\mathbf{x};\pmb{\theta}^{*})$  on  $\bar{U}$ .

Proof of Claim A.1. Let  $\vec{n} = (n_0, \dots, n_L)$  be the network architecture. Let us recall that given  $\vec{n}$ ,

$$
h ^ {l} (\mathbf {x}) = \boldsymbol {W} ^ {l} \sigma (h ^ {l - 1} (\mathbf {x})) + \boldsymbol {b} ^ {l}, \qquad 1 <   l \leq L,
$$

and  $h^1(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{W}^1\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^1$ , where  $\mathbf{W}^l \in \mathbb{R}^{n_l \times n_{l-1}}$  and  $\mathbf{b}^l \in \mathbb{R}^{n_l}$ . For each  $l$ , let  $h_k^l$  be the  $k$ -th output of  $h^l$ , where  $1 \leq k \leq n_l$ . Let  $\theta_l = \{\mathbf{W}^j, \mathbf{b}^j\}_{j=1}^l$ . With a slight abuse of notation, we regard  $\theta_l$  as a column vector. Also, let  $\theta_l' = \{\mathbf{W}'^j, \mathbf{b}'^j\}_{j=1}^l$ .

We want to prove the following statement for all  $l = 1, \dots, L$ :

$$
D ^ {s} h ^ {l} (\mathbf {x}; \boldsymbol {\theta} ^ {j _ {i}}) \text {c o n v e r g e s t o} D ^ {s} h ^ {l} (\mathbf {x}; \boldsymbol {\theta} ^ {*}) \text {i n} C ^ {0} (U), \forall \mathbf {s} \text {w i t h} 0 \leq | \mathbf {s} | \leq k.
$$

We prove it by applying induction on  $l$ .

When  $l = 1$ , it is clear that for  $r = 1, \dots, n_1$ , we have

$$
\begin{array}{l} \left. \left| h _ {r} ^ {1} (\mathbf {x}; \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {1}) - h _ {r} ^ {1} (\mathbf {x}; \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {1} ^ {\prime}) \right| ^ {2} \leq 2 \left(\left| \boldsymbol {b} _ {r} ^ {1} - \boldsymbol {b} _ {r} ^ {\prime 1} \right| ^ {2} + \left\| \boldsymbol {W} _ {r} ^ {1} - \boldsymbol {W} _ {r} ^ {\prime 1} \right\| ^ {2} \| x \| ^ {2}\right) \right. \\ \leq C \| \pmb {\theta} _ {1} - \pmb {\theta} _ {1} ^ {\prime} \| ^ {2}, \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $C = 2\max \{1, \sup_{U} \|x\|^2\}$ , and for  $s = (s_1, \dots, s_d)$  with  $1 \leq |\pmb{s}| \leq k$ ,

$$
| D ^ {s} h _ {r} ^ {1} (\mathbf {x}; \boldsymbol {\theta}) - D ^ {s} h _ {r} ^ {1} (\mathbf {x}; \boldsymbol {\theta} ^ {\prime}) | = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 0 & \text {i f m a x s _ {i} > 1}, \\ | \boldsymbol {W} _ {r i} ^ {1} - \boldsymbol {W} _ {r i} ^ {\prime 1} | & \text {i f s _ {i} = 1}. \end{array} \right.
$$

Hence, for  $s = (s_1, \dots, s_d)$  with  $0 \leq |\pmb{s}| \leq k$ ,  $D^s h^1(\mathbf{x}; \pmb{\theta}_1^{j_i})$  converges to  $D^s h^1(\mathbf{x}; \pmb{\theta}_1^*)$  in  $C^0(U)$ .

Suppose the statement is true for  $l - 1$  where  $l \geq 2$  and we want to show the case for  $l$ . For  $s$  with  $0 \leq |\pmb{s}| \leq k$ , it then can be checked that for  $1 \leq r \leq n_{l}$ ,

$$
D ^ {s} h _ {r} ^ {l} (\mathbf {x}; \pmb {\theta} _ {l}) = \frac {\partial^ {| s |} h _ {r} ^ {l} (\mathbf {x} ; \pmb {\theta} _ {l})}{\partial x _ {1} ^ {s _ {1}} \cdots \partial x _ {d} ^ {s _ {d}}} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n _ {l}} \pmb {W} _ {r i} ^ {l} \sum_ {t = 0} ^ {| s |} Q _ {i, t} ^ {l - 1} (\pmb {\theta} _ {l - 1}, | s |, \mathbf {x}) \sigma^ {(t)} (h _ {i} ^ {l - 1} (\mathbf {x}; \pmb {\theta} _ {l - 1})),
$$

where  $\sigma^{(t)}$  is the  $t$ -th derivative of  $\sigma$  and  $\{Q_{i,t}^l (\pmb {\theta}_l,|\pmb {s}|,\pmb {x})\}_{t = 1}^{|s|}$  is recursively defined as follows: Let  $\bar{x}_j = x_i$  if  $\sum_{l = 1}^{i - 1}s_l < j\leq \sum_{l = 1}^{i}s_l$ . Then, for  $s\geq 2$

$$
\begin{array}{l} Q _ {i, t} ^ {l - 1} \left(\boldsymbol {\theta} _ {l - 1}, s, \boldsymbol {x}\right) \\ = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} Q _ {i, s - 1} ^ {l - 1} (\boldsymbol {\theta} _ {l - 1}, s - 1, \boldsymbol {x}) \frac {\partial h _ {i} ^ {l - 1} (\mathbf {x} ; \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {l - 1})}{\partial \bar {x} _ {s}} & \text {i f} t = s, \\ \frac {\partial}{\partial \bar {x} _ {s}} Q _ {i, t} ^ {l - 1} (\boldsymbol {\theta} _ {l - 1}, s - 1, \boldsymbol {x}) + Q _ {i, t - 1} ^ {l - 1} (\boldsymbol {\theta} _ {l - 1}, s - 1, \boldsymbol {x}) \frac {\partial h _ {i} ^ {l - 1} (\mathbf {x} ; \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {l - 1})}{\partial \bar {x} _ {s}} & \text {i f} 1 <   t <   s, \\ \frac {\partial}{\partial \bar {x} _ {s}} Q _ {i, 1} ^ {l - 1} (\boldsymbol {\theta} _ {l - 1}, s - 1, \boldsymbol {x}) & \text {i f} t = 1, \\ 0 & \text {i f} t = 0, \end{array} \right. \\ \end{array}
$$

with  $Q_{i,1}^{l - 1}(\pmb{\theta}_{l - 1},1,\pmb{x}) = \frac{\partial h_i^{l - 1}(\mathbf{x};\pmb{\theta}_1)}{\partial\bar{x}_1}$ ,  $Q_{i,0}^{l - 1}(\pmb{\theta}_{l - 1},1,\pmb{x}) = 0$ , and  $Q_{i,0}^{l - 1}(\pmb{\theta}_{l - 1},0,\pmb{x}) = 1$ . Thus,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \left| D ^ {s} h _ {r} ^ {l} (\mathbf {x}; \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {l - 1}) - D ^ {s} h _ {r} ^ {l} (\mathbf {x}; \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {l - 1} ^ {\prime}) \right| \\ \leq \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n _ {l}} \sum_ {t = 0} ^ {| s |} | \pmb {W} _ {r i} ^ {l} Q _ {i, t} ^ {l - 1} (\pmb {\theta} _ {l - 1}, | s |, \mathbf {x}) - \pmb {W} _ {r i} ^ {\prime l} Q _ {i, t} ^ {l - 1} (\pmb {\theta} _ {l - 1} ^ {\prime}, | s |, \mathbf {x}) | | \sigma^ {(t)} (h _ {i} ^ {l - 1} (\mathbf {x}; \pmb {\theta} _ {l - 1})) | \\ + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n _ {l}} \sum_ {t = 0} ^ {| \mathbf {s} |} | W _ {r i} ^ {\prime l} Q _ {i, t} ^ {l - 1} (\pmb {\theta} _ {l - 1} ^ {\prime}, | \mathbf {s} |, \mathbf {x}) | | \sigma^ {(t)} (h _ {i} ^ {l - 1} (\mathbf {x}; \pmb {\theta} _ {l - 1})) - \sigma^ {(t)} (h _ {i} ^ {l - 1} (\mathbf {x}; \pmb {\theta} _ {l - 1} ^ {\prime})) |. \\ \end{array}
$$

Since  $\sigma^{(s)}$  is Lipschitz continuous and bounded for all  $0 \leq s \leq k$ , there exists two constants  $M$  and  $L$  such that

$$
\begin{array}{l} \left| \sigma^ {(t)} \left(h _ {i} ^ {l - 1} (\mathbf {x}; \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {l - 1})\right) - \sigma^ {(t)} \left(h _ {i} ^ {l - 1} (\mathbf {x}; \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {l - 1} ^ {\prime})\right) \right| \leq L \left| h _ {i} ^ {l - 1} (\mathbf {x}; \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {l - 1}) - h _ {i} ^ {l - 1} (\mathbf {x}; \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {l - 1} ^ {\prime}) \right|, \\ \left| \sigma^ {(t)} (h _ {i} ^ {l - 1} (\mathbf {x}; \pmb {\theta} _ {l - 1})) \right| \leq M. \\ \end{array}
$$

Let  $G^{l}(\mathbf{x};\pmb{\theta}_{l}) = W_{ri}^{l}Q_{i,t}^{l - 1}(\pmb{\theta}_{l - 1},|\pmb{s}|,\mathbf{x})$ . By the induction hypothesis,  $D^{s}h^{l - 1}(\mathbf{x};\pmb{\theta}_{l - 1}^{j})$  converges to  $D^{s}h^{l - 1}(\mathbf{x};\pmb{\theta}_{l - 1}^{*})$  in  $C^0 (U)$  for any  $s$  with  $0\leq |\pmb {s}|\leq k$ . Recall that the product of two uniformly convergent sequences of bounded continuous functions is also uniformly convergent to the product of limits. Thus,  $G^{l}(\mathbf{x};\pmb{\theta}_{l}^{j_{i}})$  uniformly converges to  $G^{l}(\mathbf{x};\pmb{\theta}_{l}^{*})$  in  $U$ . Therefore, by combining all the above, we conclude that  $D^{s}h^{l}(\mathbf{x};\pmb{\theta}_{l}^{j_{i}})$  converges to  $D^{s}h^{l}(\mathbf{x};\pmb{\theta}_{l}^{*})$  in  $C^0 (U)$ . By induction, the proof is completed.

By Claim A.1, we conclude that  $\lim_{i\to \infty}\| h_{j_i} - h^*\|_{C^k (U)} = 0$ . Since  $\lim_{j\to \infty}\| h_j - u\|_{C^0 (U)} = 0$  we have  $h^{*} = u$ . Hence,  $\lim_{i\to \infty}\| h_{j_i} - u\|_{C^k (U)} = 0$ . Since the subsequence was chosen arbitrarily, the proof is completed.

Lemma B.2. Let  $X$  be a compact subset in  $\mathbb{R}^d$ . Let  $\mu$  be a probability measure supported on  $X$ . $$
\mu (A _ {n}) = \sum_ {i _ {j} \geq 1, | i | = n} \binom {n} {i _ {1}, \ldots , i _ {K}} p _ {1} ^ {i _ {1}} \dots p _ {K} ^ {i _ {K}},

By letting  $\frac{1}{c^{2}\epsilon^{2s}} = n \geq \frac{1}{c\epsilon^{s}}$ , the proof is completed. Note that the probability in the above statement becomes  $1 - \sqrt{n} (1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})^{n}$  that goes to 1 as  $n \to \infty$ .

\operatorname {L o s s} ^ {\text {P I N N}} (h; \lambda) \leq C _ {m} \cdot \operatorname {L o s s} _ {m} (h; \lambda , \lambda_ {m} ^ {R}) + C ^ {\prime} \left(m _ {r} ^ {- \frac {\alpha}{d}} + m _ {b} ^ {- \frac {\alpha}{d - 1}}\right),
$$

where  $\lambda_{m}^{R} = (\lambda_{r,m}^{R},\lambda_{b,m}^{R})$  . Then, the proof is completed.

# C Proof of Theorem 3.2

Proof of Theorem 3.2. Suppose  $m_r = \mathcal{O}(m_{\overline{b}}^{\frac{d}{d - 1}})$ . It then can be checked that  $\hat{\lambda}_{r,m}^{R} = \hat{\lambda}_{b,m}^{R} = \mathcal{O}(m_{r}^{-\frac{1}{2} -\frac{\alpha}{d}})$ , where  $\hat{\lambda}_{r,m}$  and  $\hat{\lambda}_{b,m}$  are defined in (3.1). Let  $\lambda$  be a vector independent of  $m$  and  $\lambda_{m}^{R}$  be a vector satisfying (3.2).

Let  $h_m \in \mathcal{H}_m$  be a function that minimizes  $\mathrm{Loss}_m(\cdot; \lambda, \lambda_m^R)$ . First, note that since  $u_m^* \in \mathcal{H}_m$ ,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \min  \left\{\lambda_ {r, m} ^ {R}, \lambda_ {b, m} ^ {R} \right\} \left(R _ {r} \left(h _ {m}\right) + R _ {b} \left(h _ {m}\right)\right) \leq \operatorname {L o s s} _ {m} \left(h _ {m}; \lambda , \lambda_ {m} ^ {R}\right) \\ \leq \operatorname {L o s s} _ {m} \left(u _ {\mathbf {m}} ^ {*}; \lambda , \boldsymbol {\lambda} _ {\mathbf {m}} ^ {R}\right) \leq \operatorname {L o s s} _ {m} \left(u _ {\mathbf {m}} ^ {*}; \boldsymbol {\lambda}, \mathbf {0}\right) + \left\| \boldsymbol {\lambda} _ {\mathbf {m}} ^ {R} \right\| _ {\infty} \left(R _ {r} \left(u _ {\mathbf {m}} ^ {*}\right) + R _ {b} \left(u _ {\mathbf {m}} ^ {*}\right)\right). \tag {C.1} \\ \end{array}
$$

Since  $\lambda_{m}^{R}\geq \hat{\lambda}_{m}^{R}$  and  $\| \lambda_m^R\|_\infty = \mathcal{O}(\| \hat{\lambda}_m^R\|_\infty)$ , we have  $\frac{\max_j(\lambda_m^R)_j}{\min_j(\lambda_m^R)_j} = \mathcal{O}(1)$ . Let  $R^{*} = \sup_{m}(R_{r}(u_{m}^{*}) + R_{b}(u_{m}^{*}))$ . By the third assumption in 3.2, we have  $R^{*} < \infty$ .

The second assumption of 3.2 can be relaxed to the following condition:

- For each  $m_r$ ,  $\mathcal{H}_{m_r}$  contains a function  $u_{m_r}^*$  satisfying  $\text{Loss}_{m_r}^{\text{PINN}}(u_{m_r}^*;\lambda) = \mathcal{O}(m_r^{-\frac{1}{2} - \frac{\alpha}{d}})$ .

Let us write  $m_r$  as  $m$  for the sake of simplicity. We then have  $R_r(h_m), R_b(h_m) \leq \mathcal{O}(R^*)$  for all  $m$ . Since  $R_r(h_m) = [\mathcal{L}[h_m]]_{\alpha;U}^2$  and  $R_b(h_m) = [\mathcal{B}[h_m]]_{\alpha;U}^2$ , the Hölder coefficients of  $\mathcal{L}[h_m]$  and  $\mathcal{B}[h_m]$  are uniformly bounded above. With the first assumption of (3.2),  $\{\mathcal{L}[h_m]\}$  and  $\{\mathcal{B}[h_m]\}$  are uniformly bounded and uniformly equicontinuous sequences of functions in  $C^{0,\alpha}(U)$  and  $C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma)$ , respectively. By invoking the Arzela-Ascoli Theorem, there exists a subsequence  $h_{m_j}$  and functions  $G \in C^{0,\alpha}(U)$  and  $B \in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma)$  such that  $\mathcal{L}[h_{m_j}] \to G$  and  $\mathcal{B}[h_{m_j}] \to B$  in  $C^0 (U)$  and  $C^0 (\Gamma)$ , respectively, as  $j \to \infty$ .

Since  $\mathrm{Loss}_m(h_m;\lambda ,\lambda_r^R) = \mathcal{O}(m_r^{-\frac{1}{2} -\frac{\alpha}{d}})$ , by combining it with Theorem 3.1, we have that with probability at least  $(1 - \sqrt{m_r}(1 - c_r / \sqrt{m_r})^{m_r})(1 - \sqrt{m_b}(1 - c_b / \sqrt{m_b})^{m_b})$

$$
\operatorname {L o s s} \left(h _ {m}; \boldsymbol {\lambda}, \mathbf {0}\right) = \mathcal {O} \left(m ^ {- \frac {\alpha}{d}}\right).
$$

Hence, the probability of  $\lim_{m\to \infty}\mathrm{Loss}(h_m;\lambda ,0) = 0$  is one. Thus, with probability 1,

$$
\begin{array}{l} 0 = \lim  _ {j \rightarrow \infty} \operatorname {L o s s} \left(h _ {m _ {j}}; \lambda , \mathbf {0}\right) \\ = \lim _ {j \to \infty} \lambda_ {r} \int_ {U} \| \mathcal {L} [ h _ {m _ {j}} ] (\mathbf {x} _ {r}) - f (\mathbf {x} _ {r}) \| ^ {2} d \mu_ {r} (\mathbf {x} _ {r}) + \lambda_ {b} \int_ {\Gamma} \| \mathcal {B} [ h _ {m _ {j}} ] (\mathbf {x} _ {b}) - g (\mathbf {x} _ {b}) \| ^ {2} d \mu_ {b} (\mathbf {x} _ {b}) \\ = \lambda_ {f} \int_ {U} \| G (\mathbf {x} _ {r}) - f (\mathbf {x} _ {r}) \| ^ {2} d \mu_ {r} (\mathbf {x} _ {r}) + \lambda_ {b} \int_ {\Gamma} \| B _ {k} (\mathbf {x} _ {b}) - g (\mathbf {x} _ {b}) \| ^ {2} d \mu_ {b} (\mathbf {x} _ {b}), \\ \end{array}
$$

which shows that  $G = f$  in  $L^2(U; \mu_r)$  and  $B = g$  in  $L^2(\Gamma; \mu_b)$ . Note that since  $\mathcal{L}[h_{m_j}]$  and  $\mathcal{B}[h_{m_j}]$  are uniformly bounded above and uniformly converge to  $G$  and  $B$ , respectively, the third equality holds by Lebesgue's Dominated Convergence Theorem. Since the subsequence was arbitrary, we conclude that  $\mathcal{L}[h_m] \to f$  in  $L^2(U; \mu_r)$  and  $\mathcal{B}[h_m] \to g$  in  $L^2(\Gamma; \mu_b)$  as  $m \to \infty$ . Furthermore, since  $\{\mathcal{L}[h_m]\}$  and  $\{\mathcal{B}[h_m]\}$  are equicontinuous, its convergence mode is improved to  $C^0$ .

# D Proof of Theorem 3.3

For readability, the existence and the uniqueness of the classical solution to (3.6) is stated as follow.

Theorem D.1 (Theorem 6.13 of [15]). For  $0 < \alpha < 1$ , let  $U$  satisfy an exterior sphere condition at every boundary point. Let the operator  $\mathcal{L}$  be strictly elliptic in  $U$  with coefficients in  $C^\alpha(U)$  and  $\tilde{c}(\pmb{x}) \leq 0$ . Then, the Dirichlet problem of (3.6) has a unique solution in  $C^{2,\alpha}(U) \cap C^0(\bar{U})$  for all  $f \in C^\alpha(U)$  and all  $g \in C^0(\partial U)$ .

Lemma D.1. Suppose Assumption 3.3 holds and  $\tilde{c}(\boldsymbol{x}) \leq 0$ . Then, there exists the unique classical solution  $u^*$  to the PDE (3.6). Furthermore, there exists a positive constant  $C$  that depends only on  $U$ ,  $\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}(\boldsymbol{x})$ ,  $\lambda_0$  and  $v$  such that

$$
\left\| u ^ {*} - h \right\| _ {C ^ {0} (U)} \leq C \left(\left\| f - \mathcal {L} [ h ] \right\| _ {C ^ {0} (U)} + \left\| g - h \right\| _ {C ^ {0} (\partial U)}\right), \tag {D.1}
$$

for any  $h \in C^0(U)$  satisfying  $\mathcal{L}[h] \in C^{0,\alpha}(U)$  and  $h \in C^{0,\alpha}(\partial U)$ .

Proof of Lemma D.1. By Theorem D.1, there exists a unique classical solution  $\xi \in C^{2,\alpha}(U) \cap C^0(\bar{U})$  to

$$
\mathcal {L} [ \xi ] = 0, \text {o n} U, \quad \xi | _ {\partial U} = h | _ {\partial U} - g.
$$

Since  $\xi \in C^{2,\alpha}(U) \cap C^0(\bar{U})$ ,  $\tilde{c}(\mathbf{x}) \leq 0$  and Assumption 3.3 holds, one can apply the Weak Maximum principle (e.g. Corollary 3.11 in [15]) to have

$$
\| \xi \| _ {C ^ {0} (U)} \leq \| h - g \| _ {C ^ {0} (\partial U)}.
$$

By Theorem D.1, the PDE (3.6) has a unique classical solution  $u^{*}$ . Let  $\tilde{e} = u^{*} - h + \xi$ . Then,  $\tilde{e}$  satisfies  $\mathcal{L}[\tilde{e}] = f - \mathcal{L}[h]$  in  $U$  and  $\tilde{e}|_{\partial U} = 0$ . From an apriori bound of the elliptic equation (e.g. Theorem 3.7. of [15]), we have

$$
\| \tilde {e} \| _ {C ^ {0} (U)} \leq \frac {C}{\lambda_ {0}} \| f - \mathcal {L} [ h ] \| _ {C ^ {0} (U)},
$$

where  $C$  is a constant depending only on  $\mathrm{diam}(U)$  and  $\sup_U\frac{\|\tilde{\pmb{b}}(\mathbf{x})\|}{\lambda_0}$ . Hence,

$$
\| u ^ {*} - h \| _ {C ^ {0} (U)} \leq \| \tilde {e} \| _ {C ^ {0} (U)} + \| \xi \| _ {C ^ {0} (U)} \leq \frac {C}{\lambda_ {0}} \| f - \mathcal {L} [ h ] \| _ {C ^ {0} (U)} + \| h - g \| _ {C ^ {0} (\partial U)},
$$

which completes the proof.

Proof of Theorem 3.3. Let  $\{h_m\}_{m\geq 1}$  be the sequence of the minimizers from Theorem 3.2. It follows from Theorem 3.2 that  $\| h_m - g\|_{C^0 (\partial U)}\to 0$  and  $\| f - \mathcal{L}[h_m]\|_{C^0 (U)}\to 0$ . By Assumption 3.2,  $\mathcal{L}[h]\in C^{0,\alpha}(U)$  and  $h\in C^{0,\alpha}(\partial U)$  for all  $h\in \mathcal{H}_m$ . Lemma D.1 shows  $h_m\rightarrow u^*$  in  $C^0 (U)$ , which completes the proof.

# E Proof of Theorem 3.4

Proof. The proof continues from the proof of Lemma D.1. Since  $\tilde{e}|_{\partial U} = 0$ , it follows from Assumption 3.3 that

$$
- \langle \mathcal {L} [ \tilde {e} ], \tilde {e} \rangle_ {L ^ {2} (U)} \geq \frac {\lambda_ {0}}{2} \| D \tilde {e} \| _ {L ^ {2} (U)} ^ {2} - \lambda_ {0} v ^ {2} \| \tilde {e} \| _ {L ^ {2} (U)} ^ {2}.
$$

Thus,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \| D \tilde {e} \| _ {L ^ {2} (U)} ^ {2} \leq \| \mathcal {L} [ \tilde {e} ] \| _ {L ^ {2} (U)} \| \tilde {e} \| _ {L ^ {2} (U)} + 2 v ^ {2} \| \tilde {e} \| _ {L ^ {2} (U)} ^ {2} \\ \leq C ^ {\prime} \left[ \| \mathcal {L} [ \tilde {e} ] \| _ {L ^ {2} (U)} ^ {2} + \| \tilde {e} \| _ {L ^ {2} (U)} ^ {2} \right], \\ \end{array}
$$

where  $C^\prime$  is a constant independent of  $h$  . Therefore,

$$
\| \tilde {e} \| _ {H ^ {1} (U)} ^ {2} = \| D \tilde {e} \| _ {L ^ {2} (U)} ^ {2} + \| \tilde {e} \| _ {L ^ {2} (U)} ^ {2} \leq C ^ {\prime \prime} \left[ \| \mathcal {L} [ \tilde {e} ] \| _ {L ^ {2} (U)} ^ {2} + \| \tilde {e} \| _ {L ^ {2} (U)} ^ {2} \right],
$$

for some constant  $C''$ . Then,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \| u ^ {*} - h \| _ {H ^ {1} (U)} ^ {2} \leq 2 \| \tilde {e} \| _ {H ^ {1} (U)} ^ {2} + 2 \| \xi \| _ {H ^ {1} (U)} ^ {2} \\ \leq C ^ {\prime \prime \prime} \left(\| f - \mathcal {L} [ h ] \| _ {C ^ {0} (U)} + \| g - h \| _ {C ^ {0} (\partial U)}\right) ^ {2} + 2 \| \xi \| _ {H ^ {1} (U)} ^ {2}, \\ \end{array}
$$

for some constant  $C^{\prime \prime \prime}$ . Since  $\xi = 0$  and  $h_m = g$  on  $\partial U$ , the proof is completed by Theorem 3.2 as  $\| f - \mathcal{L}[h_m] \|_{C^0(U)} \to 0$ .

# F Proof of Theorem 3.6

For reader's convenience, let us recall a result on the existence of the classical solution to the parabolic equation.

Theorem F.1 (Corollary 2 in Chapter 3 of [14]). Suppose Assumption (3.4) holds. Suppose further that for every point  $x' \in \partial U$ , there exists a closed ball  $\bar{B}$  in  $\mathbb{R}^n$  such that  $\bar{B} \cap \overline{U} = \{x'\}$ . Then, for any continuous function  $\varphi$  on  $\partial U \times (0,T]$  and  $g$  on  $\overline{U} \times \{t = 0\}$  such that  $g(\boldsymbol{x}) = \varphi(\boldsymbol{x},0)$  on  $\partial U$ , there exists a unique classical solution  $u^*$  to (3.7).

Lemma F.1. Suppose Assumption 3.2 and 3.4 hold and  $\tilde{c}(\boldsymbol{x}, t) \leq 0$ . Let  $u^{*}$  be the solution to the PDE (3.7) and let  $\psi(\boldsymbol{x}, t)$  be the combined boundary and initial conditions on  $\Gamma_{T} = \overline{U}_{T} - U_{T}$ . Then, there exists a positive universal constant  $C$  that depends only on the domain and the PDE (3.7) such that

$$
\left\| h - u ^ {*} \right\| _ {C ^ {0} (\Omega)} \leq C \left[ \left\| f + h _ {t} - \mathcal {L} [ h ] \right\| _ {C ^ {0} (\Omega)} + \left\| h - \psi \right\| _ {C ^ {0} \left(\Gamma_ {T}\right)} \right],
$$

for any  $h\in C_{2 + \alpha}\cap C^0 (\overline{U}\times [0,T])$

Proof. Let us combine the boundary and the initial conditions into one condition:

$$
u (x, t) = \psi (x, t) \quad \text {o n} \Gamma_ {T} = \overline {{U}} _ {T} - U _ {T}.
$$

For a fixed  $h \in C_{2 + \alpha} \cap C^0(\overline{U} \times [0, T])$ , let  $\xi$  be the solution to

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l l} - \xi_ {t} + \mathcal {L} [ \xi ] = 0, & \text {i n} U _ {T} \\ \xi = \psi - h, & \text {o n} \Gamma_ {T} \end{array} \right.
$$

We note that since  $\psi - h$  is continuous on  $\Gamma_T$ , the existence of  $\xi$  is guaranteed by Theorem F.1. Note that since  $\xi$  is continuous, and  $\mu(\Omega) > 0$ , it follows from the Weak Maximum Principle that

$$
\| \xi \| _ {C ^ {0} (\Omega)} \leq \| \psi - h \| _ {C ^ {0} \left(\Gamma_ {T}\right)}.
$$

Let  $u^{*}$  be a unique classical solution to the PDE (3.7), and let  $\tilde{e} = u^{*} - h - \xi$ . Since Assumption 3.4 holds and  $\tilde{c}(\mathbf{x}, t) \leq 0$ , it follows from a consequence of the weak maximum principle (e.g. p 42, Chapter 2 of Friedman [14]) that

$$
\| \tilde {e} \| _ {C ^ {0} (\Omega)} \leq C ^ {\prime} \| f + h _ {t} - \mathcal {L} [ h ] \| _ {C ^ {0} (\Omega)},
$$

for some constant  $C^\prime$  . Therefore,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \| h - u ^ {*} \| _ {C ^ {0} (\Omega)} \leq \| \tilde {e} \| _ {C ^ {0} (\Omega)} + \| \xi \| _ {C ^ {0} (\Omega)} \\ \leq C ^ {\prime \prime} \left[ \| f + h _ {t} - \mathcal {L} [ h ] \| _ {C ^ {0} (\Omega)} + \| \psi - h \| _ {C ^ {0} (\Gamma_ {T})} \right]. \\ \end{array}
$$

Here  $C''$  is a positive universal constant that is independent of  $h$ .

Proof of Theorem 3.6. Let  $\{h_m\}_{m\geq 1}$  be the sequence of minimizers of (2.4). Note that from Theorem 3.2, we have

$$
\lim  _ {m \rightarrow \infty} \| (h _ {m}) _ {t} - \mathcal {L} [ h _ {m} ] + f \| _ {C ^ {0} (\Omega)} = 0, \quad \lim  _ {m \rightarrow \infty} \| h _ {m} - \psi \| _ {C ^ {0} (\Gamma_ {T})} = 0.
$$

By Lemma F.1, it readily follows that  $\lim_{m\to \infty}\| h_m - u^*\|_{C^0 (\Omega)} = 0$

# G Proof of Theorem 3.7

Proof. The proof continues from the proof of Lemma F.1. Note that  $\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{e}_m - \mathcal{L}[\tilde{e}_m] = f + (h_m)_t - \mathcal{L}[h_m]$  on  $U_T$  and  $\tilde{e}_m|_{\Gamma_T} = 0$ . For a fixed  $t$ , by the integration by parts, one obtains

$$
\begin{array}{l} \left\langle \left(\tilde {e} _ {m}\right) _ {t} - \mathcal {L} [ \tilde {e} _ {m} ], \tilde {e} _ {m} \right\rangle_ {L ^ {2} (U)} = \frac {1}{2} \frac {d}{d t} \| \tilde {e} _ {m} \| _ {L ^ {2} (U)} ^ {2} - \left\langle \mathcal {L} [ \tilde {e} _ {m} ], \tilde {e} _ {m} \right\rangle_ {L ^ {2} (U)} \\ \geq \frac {1}{2} \frac {d}{d t} \| \tilde {e} _ {m} (t) \| _ {L ^ {2} (U)} ^ {2} + \beta \| \tilde {e} _ {m} (t) \| _ {H ^ {1} (U)} ^ {2} - \gamma \| \tilde {e} _ {m} (t) \| _ {L ^ {2} (U)} ^ {2}, \\ \end{array}
$$

for some  $\beta > 0$  and  $\gamma \geq 0$ . Also, note that

$$
\left\langle \left(\tilde {e} _ {m}\right) _ {t} - \mathcal {L} [ \tilde {e} _ {m} ], \tilde {e} _ {m} \right\rangle_ {L ^ {2} (U)} \leq \frac {1}{2} \| f + \left(h _ {m}\right) _ {t} - \mathcal {L} [ h _ {m} ] \| _ {L ^ {2} (U)} ^ {2} + \frac {1}{2} \| \tilde {e} _ {m} (t) \| _ {L ^ {2} (U)} ^ {2}.
$$

By combining the above two inequalities, we have

$$
\frac {d}{d t} \| \tilde {e} _ {m} (t) \| _ {L ^ {2} (U)} ^ {2} + \beta^ {\prime} \| \tilde {e} _ {m} (t) \| _ {H ^ {1} (U)} ^ {2} \leq C _ {1} \| \tilde {e} _ {m} (t) \| _ {L ^ {2} (U)} ^ {2} + C _ {2} \| f + (h _ {m}) _ {t} - \mathcal {L} [ h _ {m} ] \| _ {L ^ {2} (U)} ^ {2},
$$

for some positive constants  $\beta', C_1, C_2$  that are independent of  $m$ . By integrating it with respect to  $t$  from 0 to  $T$ ,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \| \tilde {e} _ {m} (T) \| _ {L ^ {2} (U)} ^ {2} - \| \tilde {e} _ {m} (0) \| _ {L ^ {2} (U)} ^ {2} + \beta^ {\prime} \| \tilde {e} _ {m} \| _ {L ^ {2} (0, T; H ^ {1} (U))} ^ {2} \\ \leq C _ {1} \| \tilde {e} _ {m} \| _ {L ^ {2} (0, T; L ^ {2} (U))} ^ {2} + C _ {2} \| f + (h _ {m}) _ {t} - \mathcal {L} [ h _ {m} ] \| _ {L ^ {2} (0, T; L ^ {2} (U))} ^ {2}. \\ \end{array}
$$

Since  $\tilde{e}_m|_{\Gamma_T} = 0$ , we have  $\| \tilde{e}_m(0)\|_{L^2 (U)}^2 = 0$ . Hence,

$$
\| \tilde {e} _ {m} \| _ {L ^ {2} (0, T; H ^ {1} (U))} ^ {2} \leq C _ {1} ^ {\prime} \| \tilde {e} _ {m} \| _ {L ^ {2} (0, T; L ^ {2} (U))} ^ {2} + C _ {2} ^ {\prime} \| f + (h _ {m}) _ {t} - \mathcal {L} [ h _ {m} ] \| _ {L ^ {2} (0, T; L ^ {2} (U))} ^ {2}.
$$

By Lemma F.1, we have

$$
\| \tilde {e} _ {m} \| _ {L ^ {2} (0, T; H ^ {1} (U))} ^ {2} \leq C ^ {\prime \prime} \| f + (h _ {m}) _ {t} - \mathcal {L} [ h _ {m} ] \| _ {C ^ {0} (0, T; C ^ {0} (U))} ^ {2},
$$

for some constant  $C''$ . Hence,  $\lim_{m\to \infty}\| \tilde{e}_m\|_{L^2 (0,T;H_0^1 (U))} = 0$ . Therefore,  $h_m\rightarrow u^*$  in  $L^2 (0,T;H^1 (U))$ .
